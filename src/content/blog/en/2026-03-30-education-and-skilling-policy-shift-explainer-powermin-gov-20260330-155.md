The National Education Policy 2020 is still the design document. Later surveys measure progress against it. They do not replace it. The text is on the [Ministry of Education site](https://www.education.gov.in/nep/nep2020_final_eng.pdf). It is worth reading as a set of structural choices, because many summaries flatten it into a slogan about "skills."

## School years were redrawn, not just renamed

The policy replaces the 10+2 frame with a 5+3+3+4 curricular structure for ages 3 to 18. The foundational stage covers three years of early childhood education plus Grades 1 and 2. Preparatory is Grades 3 to 5. Middle is Grades 6 to 8. Secondary is Grades 9 to 12. The point of the redrawing is that ages 3 to 6 enter the formal design. Under the old structure, Class 1 began at age 6.

The policy treats foundational literacy and numeracy as the precondition for everything else. Its own words set that goal for primary school by 2025, and say the rest of the policy is largely irrelevant for students who do not get it. The National Mission that followed, NIPUN Bharat, was launched on 5 July 2021 under Samagra Shiksha. Its guidelines aim at universal foundational literacy and numeracy by the end of Grade 3 in 2026-27, for children aged 3 to 9. The mission year is a year later than the sentence in the policy. Anyone writing a timeline should keep both dates and say which document they come from.

The policy also asks that, wherever possible, the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5, and preferably until Grade 8, be the home language, mother tongue, local language, or regional language. That applies, in the text, to public and private schools. It is a pedagogical rule, not a ban on English. The same chapter asks for high-quality bilingual material so students can think in the home language and in English, especially in science and mathematics.

## The hierarchy the policy tries to remove

A principle printed in the introduction is that there should be no hard separation between arts and sciences, between curricular and extra-curricular work, or between vocational and academic streams. The vocational chapter then says why that separation had consequences. Vocational courses had been concentrated in Grades 11 and 12 and among students who had left earlier. Higher-education admissions were not built to take vocational qualifications forward. The National Skills Qualifications Framework, announced in 2013, is the policy's named attempt to restore a ladder.

The operational target is specific. By 2025, at least 50% of learners through the school and higher education system are to have exposure to vocational education. Every child is to learn at least one vocation and see several more. Secondary schools are to collaborate with ITIs, polytechnics, and local industry. Skill labs may be shared in a hub-and-spoke pattern. Bachelor of Vocation degrees continue, and vocational courses are meant to be available inside other bachelor's programmes, including the four-year multidisciplinary degree. Higher-education institutions may also offer short certificate courses.

That 2025 exposure target is now a past calendar date. The policy does not, by itself, tell you whether the share was reached. A later skill-ministry statistic on vocationally trained youth is a different measure. Do not substitute one for the other.

## Higher education, regulation, and money

The policy's higher-education aim is a gross enrolment ratio, including vocational education, of 50% by 2035, from 26.3% in 2018. It also says there should be at least one large multidisciplinary institution in or near every district by 2030. Growth is supposed to come largely from expanding and improving institutions that already exist, not only from new campuses. Undergraduate programmes are meant to allow multiple entry and exit.

On regulation, the policy proposes one umbrella, the Higher Education Commission of India, with four verticals: regulation, accreditation, funding, and academic standards. The regulatory vertical is described as a single point for higher education, including teacher education, and excluding medical and legal education. The text says this requires a relook at, and repeal of, existing Acts. It is a design proposal. Universities and colleges still deal with the University Grants Commission, the All India Council for Technical Education, and professional councils until those statutes actually change. Vocational awarding and assessment sit with the National Council for Vocational Education and Training, which is outside this higher-education chapter.

On money, the policy repeats the commitment, going back to the 1968 policy, to raise public expenditure on education to 6% of GDP. It records that combined Centre and state expenditure was around 4.43% of GDP in the Analysis of Budgeted Expenditure 2017-18, and around 10% of total government spending in the Economic Survey 2017-18. Those are the policy's own benchmarks, not a current ratio. This page does not invent a newer education-to-GDP figure. The financing chapter's thrust list is concrete: early childhood education, foundational literacy, school complexes, meals, teacher education, and rebuilding colleges so that they are multidisciplinary.

Read this way, the shift is not a single scheme. It is a change in stages, in the status of vocational learning, in how a degree can be entered and left, and in which regulator the policy eventually wants. Scheme budgets and survey ratios are how you test the design. They are not the design.

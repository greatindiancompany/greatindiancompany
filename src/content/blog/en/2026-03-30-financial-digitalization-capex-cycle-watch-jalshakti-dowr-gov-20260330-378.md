# The banking technology spend India's payments rules already require

There is no official series, in the documents below, that says Indian banks will spend a stated rupee amount on payments technology this cycle. What the rulebook does specify is the machinery a regulated entity must already be able to run. That machinery is the capex. A forecast that puts a number on it without a filing is not a reading of the rulebook.

## Data has a location, a lifetime, and a ban list

The [Digital Lending Directions, 2025](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=12848) make storage a design constraint. All data is stored on servers in India. If data is processed outside India, it is deleted from those servers and brought back within 24 hours. A lending service provider stores only basic contact data needed for the job in the contract. Biometric data is not collected or stored unless a statute allows it. The app does not take ongoing access to files, media, contacts, call logs, or telephony. Camera, microphone, and location are a one-time access for onboarding or KYC, with explicit consent.

Those rules are expensive in a boring way. They force an audit trail on consent, a retention schedule the lender and the service provider both publish, a destruction protocol, and a breach-handling standard. They also force a key-fact-statement pipe: digitally signed documents on the lender's letterhead have to reach the borrower by verified email or SMS when the contract is executed. A loan-origination system that cannot emit that packet is not finished.

The chief compliance officer, or a board-designated official, certifies the digital-lending-app inventory filed on the Reserve Bank's Centralised Information Management System. Certification of a live inventory is an operating system, not a one-time PDF.

## Authentication and aggregation add their own builds

The [Authentication mechanisms for digital payment transactions Directions, 2025](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=12898) required compliance by 1 April 2026 for domestic digital payments: at least two distinct factors, unless an exemption applies. The issuer that deploys a weak mechanism bears the loss in full. That liability pushes spend into the authentication stack and into monitoring, not into a marketing feature. Card issuers have a further build by 1 October 2026 for non-recurring cross-border card-not-present transactions, including BIN registration with networks. The directions also point issuers at the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. The payments stack and the privacy stack are the same project.

Payment aggregators, under the [15 September 2025 directions](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=12896), need a dispute framework with timelines for refunds, a security and fraud framework, merchant due diligence, and escrow accounts for customer funds. A non-bank must show net worth of ₹15 crore at application and ₹25 crore by the end of the third financial year. Escrow and merchant onboarding are balance-sheet and systems work together. A gateway that never touches funds is outside that definition.

## UPI membership and e₹ distribution are bank projects

Joining UPI is not a slide in a fintech deck. MeitY's [Digital India note](https://www.digitalindia.gov.in/initiative/unified-payment-interface-upi/) describes a system in which the bank account remains the source of funds and the app is the interface. NPCI's [UPI product statistics](https://www.npci.org.in/product/upi/product-statistics) page is also where uptime and incident disclosures sit. A member bank's spend is core connectivity, reconciliation, fraud controls, and grievance handling at national scale. The July 2026 factsheet count of 741 live banks is a measure of how widely that spend has already been made, not a budget for the 742nd.

The wholesale Digital Rupee pilot, [announced for 1 November 2022](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=54616), used central-bank money to settle secondary-market government securities so that banks would not need a separate settlement-guarantee infrastructure for that use. The retail model in the concept note has the Reserve Bank issuing and redeeming e₹ while banks distribute wallets. Wallet distribution, QR acceptance, and conversion to deposits are bank technology. They are not a second UPI.

A capex committee can therefore budget against obligations it can point to: in-country storage, a 24-hour deletion control, two-factor authentication, escrow, certified app inventories, and wallet distribution for e₹. It cannot honestly budget against a national "digitalization capex" total that no Reserve Bank table in this note publishes.

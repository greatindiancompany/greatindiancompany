# Risks and Scenarios in India's Energy Transition

A scenario is useful when it is tied to a document and to a fact that would falsify it. The four below are not alternative universes. They are readings already available in the Electricity Plan, the generation accounts, the hydrogen Mission, and the coal and manufacturing statistics. More than one can be true at the same time.

## The notified build-out

In this reading, India follows the National Electricity Plan. By 2031-32 installed capacity is about 900 GW. Solar is about 365 GW, wind about 122 GW, coal about 260 GW, nuclear about 20 GW, pumped storage about 27 GW, and batteries about 47 GW. Non-fossil capacity is about 68 percent. Peak demand is 366 GW and energy requirement is 2,474 billion units. The system still burns on the order of a billion tonnes of domestic coal. The emission factor in the plan is 0.430 kg CO2 per kWh, down from 0.548 in the 2026-27 snapshot. Fund requirement across 2022-32 is about ₹33.60 lakh crore for generation alone, plus the separate transmission estimate of ₹2,44,200 crore for inter-state renewable integration.

What supports it now: non-fossil capacity is already 300.50 GW, over 54 percent of a system around 552 GW, and FY 2025-26 added 55.29 GW of non-fossil capacity. What weakens it: wind is 58 GW against a March 2027 plan figure of 73 GW, and batteries are a requirement in the plan without a national installed-capacity row on the MNRE sheet. The risk is not that the plan forgot coal. The risk is that storage and wind slip while coal plants are still required at a 58 percent load factor, and the wires lag the plants. The transmission report says solar and wind are built faster than the lines that evacuate them. That sentence is the operational risk inside an otherwise orderly plan.

## Capacity success, energy lag

In this reading, India keeps meeting and beating its capacity-share pledge while most electrical energy still comes from fossil plants. It is the reading the latest accounts already support. The updated NDC's capacity goal, about 50 percent non-fossil installed capacity by 2030, was met in June 2025 on the Government's own statement. The generation share of non-fossil sources in FY 2025-26 was 29.2 percent. The August 2026 PIB factsheet says coal still supports over 70 percent of electricity generation and nearly 55 percent of primary energy. Coal production was 1,040.08 million tonnes in FY 2025-26. Imports by thermal plants fell to 45.4 million tonnes from 62.5 million tonnes the year before, which is an energy-security improvement inside a coal-heavy system, not an exit from coal.

The risk is a policy conversation that stops at the capacity percentage. A buyer of round-the-clock power, a state facing evening peaks, and a climate inventory all care about megawatt-hours. July 2025, when renewables met 51.5 percent of demand of 203 GW on the Minister's account, shows the ceiling of a good period. It is not the year. This scenario weakens only when the annual generation share moves by a margin you can see in the year-end energy accounts, not when another capacity milestone is crossed.

## Factories first

In this reading, industrial policy lands before the project pipeline absorbs it. Module manufacturing nameplate reached about 172 GW by 31 March 2026. Module imports in the Ministry's comparison fell from USD 2,152 million to USD 758 million. Eight of twelve PLI manufacturers had started some production. The ingot-and-wafer gate is announced for June 2028. Cell-making equipment for storage has a customs exemption through March 2028. The advanced-chemistry-cell incentive covers 50 GWh, with 40 GWh awarded and 10 GWh still to be bid for grid-scale storage.

The risk on this path is a capacity that does not sell. PLI pays on manufacture and sale for five years after commissioning, so nameplate without offtake does not complete the scheme. The risk in the other direction is a project boom that still imports the upstream product the 2028 list is meant to localise. Watching sales, the approved-model list, and the grid-scale storage award will tell you which risk is live. Installed solar of 164.59 GW does not, by itself, tell you.

## Molecules stay small while electrons scale

The hydrogen Mission's own case is at least 5 million tonnes a year by 2030, about 125 GW of associated renewable capacity, over ₹8 lakh crore of investment, over 6 lakh jobs, and nearly 50 million tonnes a year of greenhouse-gas abatement. Those are the Cabinet's "likely" outcomes. The stock PIB could cite for February 2026 was about 8,000 tonnes a year of commissioned production. July 2026 added pilots: 12 projects, 70 vehicles, 21 routes, and a hydrogen fuel-cell train on Jind–Sonipat.

The risk is treating the Mission outlay of ₹19,744 crore, or the ₹600 crore provided in Budget 2026-27, as if the 125 GW were already in the renewable pipeline. The Electricity Plan's demand forecast says it includes a hydrogen effect. It does not list 125 GW of hydrogen-linked renewables as capacity already identified. This scenario shifts when a public series shows production moving from thousands of tonnes toward millions, with renewable capacity contracted specifically for it. Pilot vehicles do not make that shift. They do show that the Mission is spending on use-cases while production is still a fraction of the aim.

## Holding more than one at once

The most accurate map in late 2026 is a stack. The capacity scenario is ahead of the NDC clock. The energy scenario is still coal-heavy over a full year. The factory scenario is ahead in modules and unfinished upstream. The molecule scenario is a target with a small commissioned base. A risk register that picks only the flattering layer will miss the constraint that actually binds the next project.

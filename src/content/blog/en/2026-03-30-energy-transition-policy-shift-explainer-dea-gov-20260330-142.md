# India's Energy Transition Policy, Explained

India's energy transition is a stack of documents with different jobs. Mixing them up is how a capacity target gets quoted as if it were a limit on emissions, and how a carbon-market notification gets quoted as if the market were already the main driver of new solar.

## Two climate sentences, and a planning target beside them

India's first nationally determined contribution, submitted in 2015, had three quantified goals through 2030: non-fossil sources at 40 percent of cumulative electric-power installed capacity, a 33 to 35 percent cut in the emissions intensity of GDP from 2005, and an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent from forest and tree cover.

The update approved by the Union Cabinet and communicated to the UNFCCC in August 2022 changed two of those numbers. The [updated NDC](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/NDC/2022-08/India%20Updated%20First%20Nationally%20Determined%20Contrib.pdf) commits India to reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45 percent by 2030 from the 2005 level, and to achieve about 50 percent cumulative electric power installed capacity from non-fossil fuel-based resources by 2030. The capacity goal is written with a condition: help through transfer of technology and low-cost international finance, including from the Green Climate Fund. The forest sink goal stayed. The update also carries the Lifestyle for Environment formulation.

That 50 percent line is a share of installed capacity. It is not a share of electricity generated, and it is not an absolute cap on tonnes of CO2. PIB recorded that India reached the 50 percent non-fossil capacity mark in June 2025. By 31 July 2026 the non-fossil stock was 300.50 GW, and PIB described that as over 54 percent of a system of around 552 GW.

A second number sits next to the NDC and is easy to confuse with it. At COP26 the Prime Minister announced a 500 GW non-fossil electricity capacity aim for 2030. MNRE and the Central Electricity Authority use that figure as the planning target. The National Electricity Plan notified in 2023 says its capacity path is in line with about 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2029-30. When a speech says "500 GW" and the NDC says "about 50 percent," both are official. They answer different questions. One is a gigawatt stock. The other is a share, and it moves when coal capacity moves too.

Net zero by 2070 is the long aim announced with that COP26 package. It is not a line you can audit on this year's MNRE sheet.

## The legal shift is about who must buy clean electricity

The Electricity (Promoting Renewable Energy Through Green Energy Open Access) Rules, notified by the Ministry of Power on 6 June 2022, lowered the open-access threshold for green energy from 1 MW to 100 kW of contracted demand or sanctioned load. Captive consumers have no load floor. Grid-India is the central nodal agency. Applications are supposed to be decided within fifteen days. A national portal, greenopenaccess.in, has been in use since November 2022.

The consumption obligation has since moved statutes. The Energy Conservation Act was amended in 2022. In October 2023 the Ministry of Power set a renewable consumption obligation trajectory under that Act. On 27 September 2025 it issued a revised notification that supersedes the October 2023 notification. MNRE's FY 2025-26 review says state-level renewable purchase obligations are subsumed in this single framework, so a consumer is not meant to carry two parallel renewable-purchase targets, one under the Electricity Act and one under the Energy Conservation Act.

For distribution licensees in 2026-27, the Bureau of Energy Efficiency's operational guidelines reproduce the notification as a 35.95 percent total: 1.97 percent wind, 1.34 percent hydro, 2.70 percent distributed renewable energy, and 29.94 percent other renewable energy. Wind and hydro count from projects commissioned after 31 March 2024. Open-access consumers and captive users are on the total percentage. Hilly and North-Eastern states, plus Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand, carry half the distributed component, with the rest folded into the other component. A licensee that serves only urban consumers carries 75 percent of the distributed component.

Compliance can be physical consumption, renewable energy certificates including virtual power purchase agreements, or the buyout price specified by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission. The obligation is the demand-side twin of the capacity target. Building a plant does not, by itself, retire the obligation of a designated consumer.

## The carbon market prices intensity, plant by plant

The same 2022 amendment lets the Central Government specify a carbon credit trading scheme. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme was notified on 28 June 2023 (S.O. 2825(E)), with an amendment on 19 December 2023. A carbon credit certificate represents one tonne of CO2 equivalent. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency administers the scheme. A National Steering Committee is chaired by the Power Secretary and co-chaired by the Environment Secretary.

Under the compliance mechanism, the Environment Ministry notifies greenhouse-gas emission intensity targets for obligated entities. An entity that beats its intensity receives certificates. An entity that misses it buys and surrenders certificates. The detailed compliance procedure, including measurement, reporting, and verification, was published in July 2024. This is an intensity market for named industrial and power-sector entities. It is not a national cap, and it does not replace the renewable consumption obligation.

## Manufacturing policy is a third track

Two production-linked incentive schemes sit beside the climate targets. MNRE's solar-module programme has an outlay of ₹24,000 crore, of which tranche II is ₹19,500 crore. The Ministry of Heavy Industries' advanced-chemistry-cell programme has an outlay of ₹18,100 crore for 50 GWh. The National Green Hydrogen Mission, approved on 4 January 2023, has an initial outlay of ₹19,744 crore and a 2030 production aim of at least 5 million tonnes a year. These are industrial policies. Their success shows up in factories and offtake, not in the NDC percentage.

## A way to read the next announcement

Ask which layer it belongs to. A Cabinet scheme changes a budget line and a beneficiary. An NDC change changes a number filed at the UNFCCC. A Ministry of Power notification changes who must consume renewable electricity this year. A Bureau of Energy Efficiency procedure changes how an obligated plant proves its emissions intensity. Those are four different events, even when they are announced in the same week.

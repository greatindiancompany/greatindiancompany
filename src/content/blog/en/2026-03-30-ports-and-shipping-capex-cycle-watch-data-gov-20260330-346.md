# The major-port capex cycle: berths, rail, and green tugs

A capex cycle at an Indian major port is a stack of different assets, paid for by different balance sheets. Berth civil works, a private terminal concession, a rail siding, a dredging campaign, and a green tug can be announced in one Sagarmala paragraph and still be four contracts. Watching "the port budget" as one number hides which contract is late.

## What the port board is allowed to spend on

The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 put the master plan and the use of funds into rules. The Ports Wing lists the Major Port Authorities (Master Plan and Application of Funds) Rules, 2021, alongside rules for the sinking fund, accounts and audit, and the scale of rates. Capital planning is now a board function inside that rule set, not a trust resolution under the repealed 1963 Act. Tariff freedom under the Act is what makes a concession financeable: the port can price services and access to land. The Adjudicatory Board, constituted on 13 August 2025, can be asked to look at a stressed public-private project and suggest how to revive it. That is the downside of the cycle. A berth that was bid in a different trade year does not automatically get a tariff reset from a national authority. The old tariff authority is gone.

The physical list the Ports Wing actually names is practical: capacity projects, mechanisation, digitisation, and public-private terminals under model concession agreements. Land policy, a berthing policy, and a policy for awarding waterfront and associated land to port-dependent industry are published as guidelines on the same page. A capex watcher should read those before reading a project brochure. Waterfront handed to a port-based plant is industrial capex that happens to sit inside a port limit.

## Connectivity capex is a different company

Sagarmala's own warning is that capacity without evacuation does not carry more cargo. Indian Port Rail and Ropeway Corporation Limited, established in 2015, is the joint venture the Sagarmala page assigns to last-mile rail into major ports, port-railway modernisation, and some ropeway work. The page describes it as a venture of major ports and Rail Vikas Nigam, and says that as of 2025 the ministry held a 90 percent indirect stake. A delayed siding will show up in dwell time and pre-berthing detention, which are Ports Wing indicators, not in the rail company's name on a port press note.

Sagarmala Finance Corporation Limited is the financing vehicle, not the asset owner. The page says it was recast from Sagarmala Development Company Limited, incorporated on 31 August 2016, and registered with the Reserve Bank as a maritime non-banking financial company on 19 June 2025. Money that passes through that corporation still has to become a berth, a jetty, or a yard. The corporation's existence is not itself port capacity.

## The green slice of the cycle

Harit Sagar, the green port guidelines of May 2023, is the environmental capex frame: shore power, electrified cargo gear, renewable energy, and waste. The Ports Wing says renewable energy share at major ports is to be raised to 60 percent. It does not put a year on that sentence, so do not invent one. The dated target is the Green Tug Transition Programme. The page says at least half of tugs at major ports should be green by 2030, using electricity, LNG, green methanol, or green hydrogen, and that all tugs should be green by 2047. The Indian Ports Association lists that programme among the work it carries for the major ports. A tug is a small asset next to a berth and a large one in an emissions inventory. It is also one of the few capex items with two official years attached.

Shipyard capex is adjacent and should not be folded into the port cycle. Dry docks and shiplifts under the Shipbuilding Development Scheme are yard assets. The shipping division's count of 61 yards, eight of them central public sector, is the universe for that spend. Port capex moves cargo. Yard capex builds the ship that might later call.

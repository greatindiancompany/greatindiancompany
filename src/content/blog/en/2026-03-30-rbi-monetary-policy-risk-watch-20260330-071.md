The August 2026 hold was a decision to wait, and the minutes say what the wait is for. The Monetary Policy Committee kept the repo rate at 5.25 per cent and the stance neutral. The resolution’s own summary is that headline inflation is projected to rise mainly because of food and fuel, that the rise is not yet broad-based, and that the outlook is hazy because of the monsoon, El Niño, geopolitics and global trade policy. The next vote is 5 to 7 October 2026.

## The baseline the committee is already living with

CPI inflation for 2026-27 is projected at 5.0 per cent. The path rises to 5.9 per cent in the third quarter, then 5.5 per cent in the fourth, and 5.3 per cent in the first quarter of 2027-28. The upper tolerance level of the target is 6 per cent. The baseline peak sits inside the band and close to the ceiling. Core inflation is projected at 4.3 per cent for the year. Real GDP growth is projected at 6.7 per cent, with risks evenly balanced on both growth and inflation.

June’s print had already crossed the 4 per cent point target. The resolution records CPI at 4.4 per cent in June after 16 months below the target. The August Bulletin’s later reading is 4.38 per cent in June and 4.45 per cent in July. Core stayed at 3.9 per cent. Core excluding precious metals moved from 2.5 per cent in June to 2.7 per cent in July. Realised inflation in the first quarter, 3.9 per cent, was 30 basis points under the June projection.

Indranil Bhattacharyya’s statement gives a diffusion count for June: 69 per cent of the weighted CPI basket was still at or below 4 per cent inflation. He also says the distribution is shifting toward higher prints, and that this shift has to be watched before anyone treats a rate increase as the next step.

## The shock the members are not yet calling general

The Governor’s statement is specific about the two prices doing the work. Food-and-beverages inflation was 4.5 per cent in May and 5.1 per cent in June, up from 4.0 per cent in April, with meat, edible oils, fruits and spices above 8 per cent in June. Fuel inflation rose to 4.5 per cent in June from 0.4 per cent in April, after retail petrol and diesel prices were raised 7.4 per cent and 8.4 per cent in May. Restaurant and accommodation inflation rose from 4.2 per cent in April to 6.9 per cent in June. That restaurant move is the second-round channel the resolution flags: fuel costs showing up in a service price.

Oil has not settled. The Indian basket averaged US$106.23 a barrel in May and US$83.22 in June. By 31 July, Brent and the Indian basket were 34.5 per cent and 32.5 per cent above end-June levels. The August Bulletin then puts the Indian basket at US$89.7 a barrel in August up to the 20th. Gupta’s minutes statement says the August forecast is built on oil averaging about US$90 for the year, after the June projection had used US$95 and the April projection US$85. A forecast that has already been rewritten twice for oil will be rewritten again if the barrel price leaves that neighbourhood.

Rain is the domestic half of the same problem. As on 3 August, rainfall was about 12 per cent below the long-period average, and 15 of 36 subdivisions were deficient. Reservoirs were at 44.4 per cent of capacity on 30 July, against 69.3 per cent a year earlier. Rice and wheat stocks, at 4.8 times and 1.9 times their buffer norms on 16 July, are the committee’s stated cushion. El Niño is the risk that the cushion has to cover.

## What would turn a pause into a move

The minutes do not instruct the October meeting. They do state the tests.

Sanjay Malhotra writes that a monetary response to a supply shock is warranted when the shock generalises, unanchors expectations, or becomes persistent. He says the evidence of that is limited, that inflation is expected to taper after the third-quarter peak, and that he would rather wait for more certainty on where inflation settles. He also says any evidence that food, fuel and input prices are spreading, or that expectations are coming loose, may need policy tightening. He notes that underlying inflation is normalising from the very low levels seen when the rate was cut to 5.25 per cent, and that this normalisation itself may argue for a recalibration later.

Poonam Gupta is more explicit about the direction. She writes that the scope for any further easing does not seem to exist, and that with headline inflation projected to peak at 5.9 per cent, a case for a hike may emerge during the year. She still votes to wait, so that weather, the extent of supply-side entrenchment, and the global picture can clear.

Saugata Bhattacharya frames the error. Tightening too early has a cost. Waiting until inflation is persistent can force a larger tightening later. He wants evidence of a further pickup in demand and of price pressure becoming general. Ram Singh wants the committee to keep room to move quickly if El Niño or oil worsens, or if second-round effects spread. Nagesh Kumar votes for the status quo because the agriculture, conflict and trade-policy risks do not yet make a case for action, and because a neutral stance fits that uncertainty.

Bhattacharyya adds a communication point. In this environment he prefers framework guidance, meaning a statement of how the committee tells demand from supply and a temporary price from a persistent one, over a promised path for the rate. A pause, on that view, is not forward guidance that October will also be a pause.

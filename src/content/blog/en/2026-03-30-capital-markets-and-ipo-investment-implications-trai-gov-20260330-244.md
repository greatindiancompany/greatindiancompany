# RBI debt caps, SIP flows, and the IPO bid

An IPO is a primary-market event. The bid around it is set by three different rulebooks: SEBI's issue and listing rules, the domestic fund industry that now buys most of the equity dip, and the Reserve Bank's ceiling on foreign money in rupee debt. Mixing those three up is how investors misread a "foreign exit" headline.

## Foreign equity selling and foreign debt limits are different pipes

SEBI's Annual Report 2025-26, Chapter 2, says foreign portfolio investors recorded net equity outflows of USD 19.7 billion in 2025-26, the largest equity outflow in the series the chapter describes, and that the selling was concentrated in the secondary market. The same chapter says primary issues still drew selective foreign participation. Chapter 1 puts the ownership result in one line: by March 2026, foreign portfolio ownership of the market it measures was 15.8 per cent, a 15-year low, while domestic institutional holdings reached 17 per cent.

That equity outflow is not the debt quota. On 6 April 2026, RBI circular RBI/2026-27/07, A.P. (DIR Series) Circular No. 05, set foreign-portfolio investment limits in debt for 2026-27 under the Foreign Exchange Management (Debt Instruments) Regulations, 2019. For the General Route, the percentages are unchanged: 6 per cent of the outstanding stock of central government securities that are not in the Fully Accessible Route, 2 per cent of state government securities, and 15 per cent of outstanding corporate bonds. Specified securities stay in the Fully Accessible Route and are not rationed under those percentages. From 1 April 2026, Voluntary Retention Route holdings count toward the General Route limits.

The circular prints the absolute ceilings in crore rupees. The all-in debt ceiling moves from ₹14,70,655 crore to ₹15,51,646 crore for April-September 2026, and to ₹16,32,640 crore for October 2026-March 2027. A separate cap on credit-default swaps sold by these investors is 5 per cent of the outstanding corporate-bond stock, which the circular puts at an additional ₹3,30,464 crore for the year. None of those lines is an equity allocation. A foreign fund can be at the debt ceiling and still bid, or not bid, an IPO under SEBI's Foreign Portfolio Investors Regulations, 2019. SEBI's own FPI statistics page is the running equity, debt, and hybrid record. The April circular is the legal ceiling for the debt part.

## Domestic institutions were the other side of the equity trade

Chapter 4 says domestic institutions, a group that includes banks, development finance institutions, insurance, mutual funds, and the National Pension System, put a net ₹8.5 lakh crore into equities in 2025-26. Mutual funds were about three quarters of that, ₹6.4 trillion against ₹4.9 trillion the previous year. Chapter 5 puts a household mechanism under the mutual fund number. Contributing SIP accounts rose from 10.05 crore to 10.45 crore. Average net monthly SIP inflow rose from ₹13,052 crore to ₹16,413 crore. SIP assets were ₹15.11 lakh crore. At the same time, 7.2 crore SIPs were registered and 6.8 crore were discontinued or matured. The support is real in the annual net figure. It is not a locked-in balance.

Nifty 50's path in the same chapter is the price context. The index closed at a high of 26,328.6 on 2 January 2026, up 11.9 per cent from the start of the year's rally the chapter describes, then fell 15.2 per cent from that peak. The financial-year move was a decline of 5.1 per cent, and 13.9 per cent in US dollar terms. MSCI Emerging Markets rose 24.7 per cent and MSCI World rose 17.2 per cent over the same year, on the report's comparison. Domestic buying absorbed foreign selling. It did not produce outperformance against those indices.

## What that means for an IPO allocation

Three implications follow from the documents, and they stop where the documents stop.

First, an offer for sale and a fresh issue do not draw from the same economic pocket, even when they clear in one book. Chapter 3 shows mixed IPOs in 2025-26 raising ₹1,15,901 crore, split almost evenly between fresh capital (₹58,166 crore) and sales by existing holders (₹57,735 crore). A domestic SIP book that is structural can still be asked to fund an exit. The offer document, not the SIP headline, says which.

Second, the anchor reservation described in Chapter 3 puts 33.33 per cent of the anchor portion with domestic mutual funds and 6.67 per cent with life insurers and pension funds, inside a 40 per cent anchor reservation. Those buyers are the same domestic pool that Chapter 4 credits with the ₹8.5 lakh crore. Their mandate, exit load, and categorisation now sit under the Mutual Funds Regulations, 2026, not under the IPO timetable.

Third, the foreign bid for a rupee IPO should be read off SEBI's FPI equity data, not off the RBI debt table. The debt table can tighten or loosen without a word in ICDR. Through September 2026 the operative absolute ceiling is the April-September column. From October the higher column in the same circular applies, still at the same 6, 2, and 15 per cent ratios.

# Fixed Wireless, Anchor Institutions, and Villages Beyond the Panchayat

Opportunity, in a market this counted, is the gap between two official numbers. It is not a list of unnamed startups. Three gaps are large enough to plan against: 5G fixed wireless beside a still-small wired book, public institutions short of the 2030 connectivity mark, and villages that sit beyond the gram panchayat.

## A fixed market that is not yet the mobile oligopoly

At end-July 2026 the top five broadband providers held 98.59 percent of all broadband lines, because mobile lines dominate the total. On fixed wired access alone their share was 71.71 percent, and the fifth name was Kerala Vision Broadband at 1.55 million, behind Jio (14.69 million), Airtel (11.28 million), BSNL (4.53 million), and Atria Convergence Technologies (2.47 million). Fixed wired access as a whole was 48.14 million, growing 0.90 percent in July. The fixed-wireless segment grew 2.60 percent, to 18.84 million.

5G fixed wireless access was 13.21 million, with 6.64 million rural and 6.56 million urban. That rural tilt is the product fact. Unlicensed-band radio fixed wireless was 5.14 million and 71.07 percent urban, and TRAI notes that only Reliance Jio reported those unlicensed-band lines. A rural home-broadband offer and an urban radio-link offer are both visible in the same annex. Public Wi-Fi, 56,222 hotspots consuming 8,576 terabytes in the March 2026 quarter, is a third and much smaller channel.

## Institutions the mission has already named

National Broadband Mission 2.0 sets broadband at 90 percent of anchor institutions by 2030. The February 2026 baseline was 68.8 percent for schools, anganwadis, and panchayat offices. The January 2025 vision note also names primary health centres. Tamil Nadu was already at 84.74 percent as of December 2025, so the remaining national gap is uneven. An operator selling a standard urban leased line into this gap will miss the sites that are still dark, and will also miss the states that are close to the target and need uptime rather than a first connection.

The fixed-speed target beside this is a national average of 100 Mbps by 2030, from 61.55 Mbps in the minister's December 2025 citation of the Ookla index. Institution connectivity and a speed average are related, and they are not the same purchase order.

## Villages the amended programme left on demand

The Amended BharatNet Programme, in the December 2024 PIB note, provides fibre to about 3.8 lakh non-gram-panchayat villages on demand, on top of ring fibre to 2.64 lakh gram panchayats. "On demand" is an opportunity with a condition: the village is in the design, and the build waits on a request and on the implementation agency. In Tamil Nadu the reply is specific: the remaining gram panchayats and 4,767 non-gram-panchayat villages are to be covered under the amended programme, and the state plans 5,27,506 household fibre connections over ten years. The current BharatNet fibre-to-the-home count there is 808.

Islands are a worked example of demand that the public network has already started to meet. PIB's February 2025 note records submarine cables of 2,312 km to the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and 1,869 km to Lakshadweep, laid so that 4G and 5G can follow. Mainland non-panchayat villages do not have that cable. They have an on-demand clause.

Machine-to-machine connections, 137.70 million at end-July, are a further book, led by Airtel at 61.33 percent. They matter to a device business. They are a poor description of a household broadband opportunity, which is why TRAI publishes a broadband total with and without them.

The skills those installs require are spelled out in the [jobs brief](https://greatindiancompany.com/blog/telecom-broadband-jobs-and-skills-impact-mohfw-gov-20260330-392).

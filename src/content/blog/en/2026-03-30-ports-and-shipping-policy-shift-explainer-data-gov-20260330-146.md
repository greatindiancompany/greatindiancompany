# The port governance shift: trusts, tariffs, and shipyard assistance

Two shifts now govern India's maritime public sector. One changed who runs a major port and who sets its tariff. The other changed how the Centre pays an Indian shipyard to build a ship. They are often announced in the same breath. They bind different people.

## From port trusts to port authorities

For decades the major ports were port trusts under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963. Tariffs for many of their services were set outside the port, by the Tariff Authority for Major Ports. The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 replaced that model. The ministry's Ports Wing says the Act came into force on 3 November 2021 and, with it, the 1963 Act stood repealed.

The new unit is a board of a major port authority. The board holds administration, control, and management. Section 27 lets the port determine the tariff for services it renders and for access to port assets, including port land. The Centre still writes the framework. The rules already notified under the Act cover accounts and audit, corporate social responsibility, the master plan and application of funds, the sinking fund, the scale of rates and fees, the chairperson and deputy chairperson, and the adjudicatory board.

The second half of the shift is newer than the Act. On 13 August 2025 the Centre constituted the Adjudicatory Board for Major Ports. The Ports Wing says the old tariff authority then ceased to exist. The board does not set tariffs. It hears disputes between a major port and a concessionaire or captive berth user, reviews stressed public-private projects referred to it, takes user complaints about services, and carries residual work from earlier tariff guidelines and orders. The presiding officer named on the ministry page is Justice Ashish Jitendra Desai, former Chief Justice of the Kerala High Court. The board sits in Mumbai, in the premises of the former tariff authority.

Kamarajar is the exception inside the list. The Ports Wing places it with the major port authorities, and the Indian Ports Association lists it as Kamarajar Port Limited. A company port and a port authority are not the same legal person, even when both are "major ports" for cargo statistics.

## From an assistance policy to a scheme with a fund

Shipyards were on a different track. The Press Information Bureau note of 24 July 2026 describes the Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme as an extension of the Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Policy, with an outlay of ₹24,736 crore, aimed at the cost gap between Indian yards and yards elsewhere. The same reply puts a ₹25,000 crore Maritime Development Fund beside it, split into a ₹20,000 crore Maritime Investment Fund and a ₹5,000 crore interest incentivisation fund, plus a ₹19,989 crore Shipbuilding Development Scheme. The package in that reply is ₹69,725 crore.

The 27 December 2025 bureau note is the operational guide for the two schemes it covers: assistance of 15 to 25 percent per vessel, paid in stages, and a shipbreaking credit note equal to 40 percent of scrap value if the ship is recycled in India. Both schemes run to 31 March 2036, with an in-principle extension contemplated to 2047. Guidelines for the assistance scheme were issued on 26 December 2025. Fund guidelines followed on 20 February 2026. A fund manager was named on 26 May 2026.

A related classification change sits underneath the finance. On 19 September 2025, infrastructure status was notified for Indian-flagged commercial vessels of 10,000 gross tonnage and above, and for vessels built in India of 1,500 gross tonnage and above. That is a financing classification, not a port tariff.

## What the shift does not do

Non-major ports stay with state maritime boards or state governments. The Ports Wing lists an Indian Ports Act, 2025 next to the 2021 Act. This brief does not paraphrase that 2025 statute section by section. Coastal shipping policy and the Directorate General of Shipping's merchant-ship rules are a third regime again: they follow the ship, not the port board.

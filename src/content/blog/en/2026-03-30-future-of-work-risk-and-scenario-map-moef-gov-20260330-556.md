# Three implementation paths for gig work and social security

A scenario is useful when the statute already contains the fork. It is not useful when someone assigns a probability the documents do not contain. The Code on Social Security, the 21 November 2025 transition language, and the Rajasthan and Karnataka Acts describe three operating paths. Bihar and Telangana have their own Acts — Bihar's starts by notification, and Telangana's Act 21 of 2026 came into force on 2 June 2026 — so a fourth mistake is pasting one state's clause into another state. The three paths below are still the ones the central code and these fee designs force you to prepare for. A firm or a labour department can prepare for each without pretending to know which one a future Gazette will pick.

## Path A: the rate and the start date are both notified

Section 114(4) sets the aggregator's contribution, once notified, between 1 and 2 percent of annual turnover excluding central taxes, levies, and cess, and not above 5 percent of amounts paid or payable to gig and platform workers. Section 114(5) requires a separate notification of the date that contribution begins. Section 114(1) allows schemes for life and disability cover, accident insurance, health and maternity, old-age protection, and creche. Subsection (3) allows those schemes to be funded by the Centre, the states, aggregators, beneficiaries, corporate social responsibility funds, or a mix.

On this path, a platform in the Seventh Schedule can put a number in its accounts. Workers get a scheme only if a scheme is also notified and actually funded. The contribution and the benefit are different instruments. Collecting the levy without framing the scheme, or framing the scheme without a start date for the levy, are both open under the way the section is written.

Karnataka's Act is built to meet this path. It says the state welfare fee counts as the contribution payable under section 114(4), and that any difference is reconciled yearly. A platform that has paid the Karnataka fee would, on the Act's text, not automatically owe the central amount again in full. The reconciliation rule is the protection against double charging, and it works only if both sides are computing the same workers and the same year.

## Path B: the transition stays long

The Ministry's 21 November 2025 note is explicit. Rules, regulations, and schemes will be framed with consultation. During the transition, the relevant provisions of the existing labour Acts and their rules, notifications, standards, and schemes continue. The Economic Survey 2025–26 still spoke of rules expected over the following months.

On this path, an employer who rewrote standing orders, wage periods, or contractor agreements on the commencement date may be early. The code is effective, and a particular old rule is still the one the Ministry said would continue until replaced. The practical risk is a dual file: a new appointment-letter practice, which the Ministry presents as part of the new frame, sitting beside an untouched licensing or inspection practice because the replacing rule is not out.

For gig work the risk is sharper and quieter. If section 114(5) is not notified, there is no central contribution to pay, and also no central scheme being funded by that contribution. Platforms that booked a provision anyway have a margin that the law has not yet taken. Workers who were told the code "covers gig workers" have a definition and no scheme. Both mistakes come from reading the commencement date as if it were section 114(5).

## Path C: state and Centre charge the same payout differently

Rajasthan's fee is a notified percent of the value of each transaction, excluding tax. The Act starts on a date the state notifies. Interest on a delayed fee, once due, is 12 percent a year. The fee sections do not say that this amount is the section 114(4) contribution.

Karnataka's fee is a notified percent of payout, between 1 and 5 percent, and the Act does say that it counts as that contribution.

A platform with workers in both states cannot use one accrual. On Path A, Karnataka may be largely a reconciliation against the Centre, while Rajasthan may be an additional charge on transaction value unless a later rule says otherwise. On Path B, Karnataka's own fee can still be due under a state Act deemed in force from 30 May 2025, while the central levy has not started. That is not a hypothetical conflict of principles. It is what the two commencement clauses and the two fee bases already say.

There is a further state-capacity version of this path. Karnataka's board exists from a date the government appoints. Rajasthan's system for seeing each transaction is the Central Transaction Information and Management System described in the Act. A fee that is due in law and invisible in a payments system is a dispute waiting for the recovery provisions, which in Rajasthan include recovery as an arrear of land revenue.

## The data risk that sits under all three

e-Shram registers unorganised workers, including gig and platform workers, on self-declaration. As on 14 July 2026 the register held 31,78,31,749 people, and the Ministry reported large overlapping enrolments under schemes such as One Nation One Ration Card (24.46 crore) and Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY (15.10 crore). A scheme that treats registration as proof of eligibility will leak, because the design of the register is self-declaration. A scheme that waits for a perfect census will not use the register the Ministry has spent five years building. The operational choice, which the Lok Sabha reply does not make for the reader, is verification at the point of benefit, not a debate about whether 31 crore is "too big."

NITI Aayog's 77 lakh estimate for 2020–21 is not a fourth scenario for 2026. It is a reminder that the household survey still does not label gig workers. Any benefit roll built only from PLFS will miss them. Any benefit roll built only from e-Shram will include many workers who are not on platforms. The risk map is the gap between those two lists, plus the three legal paths above. It does not need a probability to be usable.

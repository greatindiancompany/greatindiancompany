# What to read next on India’s bank credit numbers

The next credit headline will be wrong for most decisions if it is read alone. This is the order in which a founder, a lender, an investor, or a policy reader can check it against the Reserve Bank’s own releases. The figures below are the latest anchors, not forecasts.

## 1. Start with the monthly sectoral note, and name the series

The working release is “Sectoral Deployment of Bank Credit.” The latest one, dated 31 August 2026, covers the fortnight ended **31 July 2026**. Non-food credit, from the Section-42 return for all scheduled commercial banks, grew **19.1%**, against **9.9%** a year earlier. The four sector rates beside it — agriculture **17.0%**, industry **20.0%**, services **22.9%**, personal loans **16.2%** — come from 41 banks and about 95% of non-food credit.

When the next month appears, write down four comparisons before forming a view:

- Non-food credit versus 19.1%, and versus the year-ago fortnight printed in the new note.
- Industry versus personal loans. From March to July 2026, personal loans stayed at 16.2% while industry moved from 15.0% to 20.0%. If that gap closes or widens, the cycle has changed character.
- The adjectives on micro and small industry versus large and medium. In July, large and medium had accelerated and micro and small were steady.
- The three named services lines: non-bank finance companies, trade, and commercial real estate.

Also read the footnote. Since 31 December 2025 the last reporting fortnight is the last day of the month. The year-on-year rate compares that date with the old fortnight a year earlier.

## 2. Refuse to average it with the other two official rates

If a commentator quotes **14.1%** or **14.5%** next to 19.1%, the gap is not automatically a revision.

- **14.1%** is outstanding credit in BSR-1 at end-March 2026, including regional rural banks, up from 11.1%. Use it for borrower type, interest-rate bands, and population group. The share of loans priced below 9% was **64.2%**. Metropolitan places of utilisation held **58.2%**.
- **14.5%** is supervisory credit growth for the whole of 2025-26 in the June Financial Stability Report, next to deposit growth of **11.5%**. Use it when the question is the year, the margin, or the capital ratio.

Quote the July non-food rate for the latest pace. Quote BSR for who borrowed. Quote the stability report for whether the system can carry the pace.

## 3. Check quality on the stability-report calendar, not on the monthly release

The monthly note does not update bad loans. The June 2026 report set the gross non-performing asset ratio at **1.8%**, the slippage ratio at **1.2%**, and the provisioning coverage ratio at **75.6%**. Agriculture was **5.1%** and **37.2%** of gross bad loans. The baseline stress test takes the 46-bank bad-loan ratio to **1.9%** by March 2028. The two adverse cases take it to **3.8%** and **4.1%**. The next Financial Stability Report is the document that confirms or breaks that path. A monthly credit beat does not.

While waiting, the funding line is the live monthly companion. Credit at 14.5% against deposits at 11.5% for 2025-26 is why the report calls funding a challenge. If the next sectoral release is strong and deposit commentary in the Bulletin is not, that gap is the risk, not the growth rate.

## 4. Use a separate table for MSMEs

Priority-sector returns, not the industry paragraph, are the MSME stock. At end-December 2025 outstanding MSME credit was **₹36.8 lakh crore**, up **23.5%**, with **234.7 lakh** accounts — almost the same account count as a year earlier. From 1 April 2026 the collateral-free micro and small enterprise ceiling is **₹20 lakh**, up from ₹10 lakh. A founder should not treat a 20% industry-credit headline as evidence that this ceiling is already visible in the stock. The December table predates the rule, and the July industry note does not publish a micro and small percentage.

## 5. Read the policy statement for the price, not the pace

The repo rate is **5.25%**, with a neutral stance, as of the 3–5 August 2026 meeting. Through June 2026, fresh rupee lending rates were down **80 basis points** against **125 basis points** of cumulative cuts since February 2025. Outstanding loan rates were down **91 basis points**. The statement says fresh-rate pass-through has moderated because credit demand is strong, and bank credit was up **17.7%** on 15 July 2026. If the next statement shows credit still fast and fresh-rate pass-through still stalled, pricing power is with loan demand. If fresh rates start falling again while credit slows, the cycle is the easing cycle, not a demand cycle.

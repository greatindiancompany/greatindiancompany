State public finance in India is not one budget. The Reserve Bank of India’s report *[State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025-26](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/AnnualPublications.aspx?head=State+Finances+%3A+A+Study+of+Budgets)*, released on 23 January 2026, covers 31 state governments and union territories with a legislature. The theme of that issue is demographic transition and what it implies for state finances. The consolidated ratios are the headline. The state-wise statements are the actual map.

## The consolidated picture, labelled by year

After three years below 3 percent of GDP (2021-22 through 2023-24), the consolidated gross fiscal deficit of the states widened to 3.3 percent in 2024-25. The report gives the cause as slower revenue growth together with higher capital expenditure. For 2025-26, states budgeted the same 3.3 percent, and the report says they budgeted a further improvement in the quality of spending.

In rupees, the provisional accounts for 2024-25 show a gross fiscal deficit of ₹10.8 lakh crore. The 2025-26 Budget estimates show ₹11.8 lakh crore. The revenue deficit moved from 0.6 percent of GDP in the 2024-25 provisional accounts to a budgeted 0.2 percent in 2025-26. The primary deficit moved from 1.7 percent to a budgeted 1.5 percent.

The report says this consolidated deficit stayed inside the Centre’s prescribed ceiling of 3.5 percent, a ceiling that includes 0.5 percent linked to power-sector reform. That ceiling is not the same object as the Union’s own 4.3 percent deficit for 2026-27. Adding the two ratios does not produce “general government.” Each side records transfers the other side also records.

## Dispersion is the pattern

Sixteen states budgeted a gross fiscal deficit above 3 percent of their own gross state domestic product for 2025-26. Thirteen of those sixteen budgeted a ratio above 3.5 percent. A national 3.3 percent can coexist with a long list of states outside the number people quote as the norm. The report’s chart, not a blog ranking, is the place to see which states those are.

Receipts explain part of the recent widening. Grants-in-aid from the Centre fell as GST compensation and post-devolution revenue-deficit grants declined. Tax receipts were stronger, pulled by devolution. States’ own taxes are concentrated: GST, sales tax, excise, and stamp duty and registration fees are about 90 percent of own-tax collections. Stamp duty is the piece the report singles out as still varying sharply across states.

On the spending side, provisional revenue expenditure in 2024-25 was 13.3 percent of GDP, down from 14.9 percent in 2020-21. Capital expenditure was 2.7 percent of GDP in those provisional accounts and is budgeted at 3.2 percent for 2025-26. The share of capital expenditure in total expenditure is budgeted at 18.0 percent, against 13.4 percent in 2020-21. The share of the revenue deficit inside the gross fiscal deficit is budgeted at 6.9 percent, against 46.1 percent in 2020-21. Borrowing, on the states’ own estimates, is being pointed more at assets than it was in the pandemic year. “Budgeted” is still doing a lot of work in that sentence.

## Market borrowing is not evenly spread

Gross market borrowing of states and union territories rose 6.6 percent to ₹10.73 lakh crore in 2024-25, from ₹10.07 lakh crore the year before. That was a much smaller increase than the 32.8 percent jump in the previous year. States have budgeted ₹12.45 lakh crore of gross market borrowing for 2025-26. By end-September 2025 they had raised ₹4.67 lakh crore, 37.5 percent of the budgeted amount and 21 percent above the previous year’s corresponding period.

The report notes that borrowing still bunches in the last quarter. It also notes that the 2024-25 increase was not universal: Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Goa, Punjab, and Uttar Pradesh did not see higher gross market borrowing. Uttar Pradesh’s gross borrowing fell from ₹97,650 crore in 2023-24 to ₹45,000 crore in 2024-25. Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu were at the other end of the table, at ₹1,23,000 crore and ₹1,23,625 crore. Those are examples of spread, taken from the Reserve Bank’s table. They are not a credit ranking.

In the 2025-26 Budget estimates, market borrowing is expected to finance about 76 percent of the consolidated state deficit. Loans from the Centre, including the 50-year interest-free capital-investment loans, are the other large pipe. The weighted average yield on state government securities was 7.2 percent in 2024-25 and 7.0 percent in issuances up to end-September 2025, with a spread over the corresponding central government security of 30 and 38 basis points.

## A timing caution

This Reserve Bank study is dated January 2026 and reads state budgets for 2025-26. The Union Budget for 2026-27, presented the next month, puts SASCI support at ₹2.0 lakh crore. The study’s discussion of a ₹1.5 lakh crore scheme belongs to the earlier year: in 2024-25, ₹1,49,484 crore of a ₹1.5 lakh crore allocation was released. Do not paste the February Union figure back into the January state tables.

Provisional state accounts for April–November 2025-26 showed the consolidated deficit at 42.8 percent of Budget estimates, against 45.5 percent a year earlier. Capital spending in that window grew 8.7 percent. The report’s judgment at the time was that states were likely to stay inside their budgeted deficits if revenue spending remained slower than budgeted. That judgment is the Reserve Bank’s, as of the study. It is not a closing print for the year.

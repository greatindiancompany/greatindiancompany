A national enrolment ratio is a weighted average of very different states. Using it as "the India number" for a district plan, a college location, or a training bid will send you to the wrong problem. The official tables exist so you do not have to. This brief is about how to read them. It does not publish a state ranking, because a ranking that mixes two definitions of "secondary" is worse than no ranking.

## Two maps of the same schools

UDISE+ is the Ministry of Education's school census. The 2024-25 report, released on 28 August 2025, is summarised in the [Ministry's note](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2161543). The dashboard is at [udiseplus.gov.in](https://udiseplus.gov.in/).

The note's headline ratios use the National Education Policy stages, not the older primary / upper-primary / secondary / higher-secondary cut. Middle is Grades 6–8. Secondary, in this structure, is Grades 9–12. On that definition, middle-stage gross enrolment was 90.3% in 2024-25, up from 89.5% in 2023-24. Secondary-stage gross enrolment was 68.5%, up from 66.5%.

The same note's retention rates show why gross enrolment is not the whole pattern. Secondary-stage retention rose from 45.6% in 2023-24 to 47.2% in 2024-25. Foundational retention was 98.9%. A system can look nearly universal at the start and still keep fewer than half of students through the secondary stage. Transition from middle to secondary improved from 83.3% to 86.6%. Dropout at the secondary stage fell from 10.9% to 8.2%, and is still the highest of the stages the note reports. Preparatory dropout was 2.3% and middle dropout was 3.5%.

Any state comparison has to say which of these it is using. A state can have a high gross enrolment ratio because over-age and under-age pupils are in the numerator, and still have a weak retention rate. Gross enrolment can also exceed 100 for that reason. A figure above 100 is not a data error by itself.

## What the national sheet already shows, before you open a state

Several all-India facts in the 2024-25 note are themselves clues to state variation, because they cannot be uniform.

Teachers crossed one crore for the first time in a UDISE+ year: 1,01,22,420 in 2024-25, a 6.7% rise compared with 2022-23. Pupil-teacher ratios were 10 at foundational, 13 at preparatory, 17 at middle, and 21 at secondary, all inside the policy's 30:1 reference. A comfortable national ratio is compatible with single-teacher schools. The note says single-teacher schools fell by around 6% from the previous year, and zero-enrolment schools by around 38%. The leftovers are a location problem, not a national-average problem.

Infrastructure is in the same position. In 2024-25, 64.7% of schools had computer access, up from 57.2%, and 63.5% had internet, up from 53.9%. Electricity was in 93.6% of schools, drinking water in 99.3%, girls' toilets in 97.3%, and boys' toilets in 96.2%. Ramps with handrails were in 54.9% of schools. A state average near the national computer figure still hides blocks where a digital lesson cannot run. Girls were 48.3% of enrolment. Women were 54.2% of teachers. Those are national compositions. A district can miss both.

## Higher education has its own state table

AISHE is not UDISE. It is a voluntary, self-reported return from higher-education institutions. The 8 July 2026 release put the all-India gross enrolment ratio for ages 18–23 at 30 in 2023-24. Female gross enrolment was 31.2 and the gender parity index was 1.08. Scheduled Caste gross enrolment was 27.8 and Scheduled Tribe gross enrolment was 22.8. The Ministry says the state and Union Territory series sits in the full report at [aishe.gov.in](https://aishe.gov.in/).

Do not paste a school's secondary-stage 68.5% and a college's 30 into one "state education score." They use different ages, different institutions, and different collection methods. A state can be strong on school retention and thin on college seats, or the reverse. The policy's own older baseline, 56.5% gross enrolment in Grades 11–12, was already a warning that the last two school years were the break. The new secondary-stage ratio of 68.5% covers Grades 9–12 as one block, so it is not a statistical update of that 56.5% figure. Compare a state with itself across years on one definition.

## A practical order of reading

Start with the stage where the national system frays: secondary retention, secondary dropout, and the middle-to-secondary transition. Then open the state table for that same indicator, not for a blended index. Check single-teacher schools and computer or internet access in the same state, because a training or foundation-learning programme assumes a teacher and, often, a device. For colleges, read the state's higher-education ratio next to the Scheduled Tribe and Scheduled Caste ratios, not only next to the male-female index. The national gender parity index above 1 does not answer the tribal gap.

Skill schemes add a third geography. PMKVY 4.0 coverage, as on 7 December 2025, was reported across 732 districts. District presence is not the same as a secondary-retention problem being solved. A state skill mission and a state school department can both be "active" while students still leave between middle and secondary. The pattern that matters is the one in the stage table for that state, read with the definition written on the page.

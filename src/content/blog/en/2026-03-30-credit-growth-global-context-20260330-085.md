# India’s credit cycle in a tighter global system

India’s credit upswing is a domestic banking fact sitting inside a global system the Reserve Bank describes as resilient so far and still fragile.

The June 2026 Financial Stability Report, which is the collective view of the Financial Stability and Development Council’s sub-committee, says the global financial system has absorbed repeated shocks. Markets settled after the first burst of volatility from the West Asia conflict. The same note lists what could still amplify the next shock: supply-chain uncertainty, high public debt, fragile bond markets, stretched asset prices, and leveraged non-bank intermediaries. The Governor’s June statement adds the policy split. Major advanced-economy central banks were likely to turn toward tighter policy. India’s committee, facing the same energy and shipping shocks, left the repo rate at 5.25%.

## What “global credit” means in this report

One global market the report treats directly is private credit, a non-bank form of lending. Estimates of its size run from **US$1.5 trillion to US$2 trillion**, and the range itself is a warning: definitions differ and the data are poor. Banks and non-banks are tied together in that market in ways that are hard to see. The report notes defaults and rising redemption requests. It says the stress visible then may not be systemic, and that the linkages still have to be watched. It points to the Financial Stability Board’s May 2026 report on private-credit vulnerabilities. The expansion of non-bank credit, the Reserve Bank writes, is partly what happens when bank rules are tightened to protect the core of the system.

That global private-credit stock is not India’s bank-credit growth rate. Mixing them produces a false comparison. The domestic fact is that India remains bank-centred. The stability report says so in terms: the resilience of banks is the resilience of the financial system. In 2025-26, bank credit to the commercial sector grew **15.9%**, while credit from non-bank sources grew **13.3%** after **15.1%** the year before. Banks outgrew the non-bank channel in the year the global conversation was about non-banks.

The report also records a domestic version of the same switch at the borrower level. Corporate bond issuance through the listed private-placement route fell in 2025-26 as bond yields hardened. With policy-rate cuts passing through to lending rates, large companies and non-bank lenders used bank credit more. India’s faster bank-credit year is, in part, borrowers coming back to banks because bank loans got cheaper relative to bonds. That is the opposite of a world in which credit quietly leaves the banking system.

## The macro buffer the credit cycle sits on

The Annual Report says India remained the fastest-growing major economy in 2025-26, at **7.6%**, after **7.1%** the year before. The figure uses the National Statistics Office’s second advance estimates and the 2022-23 GDP base. Inflation was distinctly low for much of the year. The current account deficit was modest, foreign-exchange reserves were adequate, and portfolio flows were outward. The stability report’s opening chapter says those fundamentals leave India better placed against external shocks than in earlier crises, with the live exposures being imported energy and broken supply chains. The current account stayed manageable in 2025-26. The overall balance of payments was in deficit for a second year, mainly because capital inflows were weak.

Household debt is the comparison the report is willing to make with other emerging economies. It puts India’s household debt at **45.5% of GDP** at end-September 2025 and titles the chart as a relatively low level, driven mainly by non-housing retail loans rather than by mortgages. The chart uses Bank for International Settlements data plus a credit-bureau split. This brief does not assign other countries’ ratios off the chart graphic. The sentence the report supports is the relative one: India’s household debt is presented as low beside other emerging markets, and the composition is retail rather than housing.

## Why a domestic 19% rate is not a global green light

By 31 July 2026, non-food bank credit was growing at 19.1%. Nothing in the global chapter converts that rate into a claim that India has decoupled. The report’s own risk list for the domestic system is energy prices, supply chains, and the chance that higher sovereign yields hit investment portfolios. Its risk list for the world is leveraged non-bank credit, which matters here because Indian banks fund domestic non-banks and because services credit — a large piece of the 19.1% — is growing through that channel.

The reading Great Indian Company wants a cross-border investor to keep is narrow. India’s credit acceleration is happening in banks, with a growing economy and a paused policy rate, while the global system the Reserve Bank describes is one shock away from tighter financial conditions.

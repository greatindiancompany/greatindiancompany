The fiscal deficit is a financing plan before it is a slogan. For 2026-27 the Union says it will borrow a gross ₹17.20 lakh crore through dated government securities, and a net ₹11.73 lakh crore. Those two numbers are the supply signal the bond market can actually calendar. Equity-market circulars do not set them. The Budget’s fiscal statement does.

## The size of the programme

The fiscal deficit in the 2026-27 Budget estimates is ₹16.96 lakh crore. Dated securities do not cover all of it. The statement lists other financing: about ₹3.87 lakh crore from the National Small Savings Fund, ₹15,385 crore of net external assistance, and ₹3,500 crore from state provident funds. The speech rounds the market lines the same way: net dated borrowings of ₹11.7 lakh crore and gross of ₹17.2 lakh crore, with the balance from small savings and other sources.

Gross is the number that hits the auction calendar. Net is gross minus redemptions. The gap between ₹17.20 lakh crore and ₹11.73 lakh crore is the refinancing load, not new deficit. Comparing only the gross figures across years overstates how much extra paper the deficit itself requires.

In the 2025-26 revised estimates, gross and net dated borrowings are ₹14.61 lakh crore and ₹10.40 lakh crore. The original 2025-26 Budget estimates had been ₹14.82 lakh crore gross and ₹11.54 lakh crore net. The gross programme for 2026-27 is larger than both. As a share of GDP the statement puts 2026-27 gross dated borrowing at 4.4 percent and net at 3.0 percent.

## What the last programme actually cleared

By 27 January 2026, the government had completed gross dated borrowing of ₹12.75 lakh crore and net of ₹7.87 lakh crore in 2025-26. That was 86.0 percent of the year’s gross Budget estimate and 68.2 percent of the net estimate. The weighted average yield on those primary issuances was about 6.65 percent, down from 6.96 percent in 2024-25. The weighted average maturity was about 19.03 years, against 20.65 years in 2024-25 and 18.09 years in 2023-24.

A lower yield on a still-long maturity is the fact pattern the statement reports. It is not a promise that 2026-27 will clear at 6.65 percent. The same pages say interest estimates depend on domestic and international rates, and that global financial conditions did reach the Indian government-securities market during the year.

Net treasury-bill issuance as on 27 January 2026 was a negative ₹0.49 lakh crore. Short-term paper was being retired, not added. That is a separate signal from the dated curve: the cash position was not being leaned on T-bills at that date.

## Switches and buybacks

The statement treats switches and buybacks as debt-management tools, not as deficit financing in disguise. Switches of about ₹1.64 lakh crore had been completed by the date of the statement, moving redemptions along the curve. Buybacks of securities maturing in 2026-27 amounted to ₹86,775 crore during 2025-26. The stated aims are interest saving, a smoother redemption wall, and easier banking-system liquidity. A buyback changes which bond is outstanding. It does not, by itself, cut the 4.3 percent deficit target.

Most of the liability stock is domestic. External liabilities are 3.3 percent of total liabilities at book value, and the statement says most of that slice was contracted with multilateral agencies. A reader marking “foreign-currency risk” on the Union’s debt is marking a small share, not the main stock. Exchange-rate valuation still moves the external line inside the FRBM debt definition, which is why the debt ratio and the fiscal deficit are not the same object.

## How to use the signal

Three comparisons are clean, and one is not.

Clean: gross dated supply in 2026-27 versus the revised gross supply of 2025-26. Clean: net dated supply versus the fiscal deficit, to see how much of the deficit is securities versus small savings. Clean: the previous year’s weighted average yield and maturity, as a record of clearing conditions, not as a forecast.

Not clean: treating a single auction’s cut-off as proof that the fiscal path has broken. The statement’s own standard for the year is whether the programme is completed without disruption, and whether debt as a share of GDP is lower at the next revised estimates than the 56.1 percent recorded for 2025-26.

State development loans are a second supply pipe. They are not in these Union totals. The Reserve Bank’s study of state budgets is the document for that pipe; mixing it into the Centre’s ₹17.20 lakh crore double-counts nothing only if the two are kept in separate columns.

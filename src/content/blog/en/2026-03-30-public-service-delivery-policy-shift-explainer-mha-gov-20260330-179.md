# From citizen charters to shared service rails

India's citizen-service policy is a stack. A charter sets the promise. A grievance system records the miss. A state workflow issues the certificate. A document wallet and a mobile window carry the output. A payment rail moves the cash. No single circular replaced the others.

## The promise: charters, then Sevottam

The Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances records that citizen charters began after the Conference of Chief Ministers on 24 May 1997. Charters were required to include standards of service, time limits for delivery, and avenues for grievance redress, starting with sectors that had a large public interface, including Railways, Telecom, Posts, and the public distribution system. DARPG's [citizen charter page](https://darpg.gov.in/citizen-charter) says guidelines went to departments so charters would stay focused, and that a charter website, goicharters.nic.in, was launched on 31 May 2002.

[Sevottam](https://www.darpg.gov.in/relatedlinks/sevottam) is DARPG's generic framework for excellence in public service delivery. The page names three modules: the Citizen's Charter, the Grievance Redressal Mechanism, and Capability Building for Service Delivery. A charter without a redress path, or a redress path without a trained office, is outside that design.

## The miss: CPGRAMS

The [Centralised Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System](https://pgportal.gov.in/) is the online window for a grievance about service delivery. The portal says it is available 24x7, connected to ministries and departments of the Government of India and to the states, and also available through UMANG. A complainant receives a registration number, can track status, and can appeal after closure when the rating is "Poor." DARPG's role, described on the portal's FAQ, is policy, monitoring, and coordination. The ministry or department that owns the service does the redress.

## The cash: DBT Mission

The [DBT Mission about page](https://dbtbharat.gov.in/static-page-content/spagecont?id=1) dates Direct Benefit Transfer to 1 January 2013. The aim it states is a simpler and faster flow of information and funds, accurate targeting, de-duplication, and reduction of fraud. The Mission moved from the Planning Commission to the Department of Expenditure in July 2013, and to the Cabinet Secretariat under Secretary (Coordination and Public Grievances) with effect from 14 September 2015. Office memoranda of 13 February 2015 and 19 February 2015 set an electronic payment framework for Central Sector and Centrally Sponsored Schemes, and for schemes that transfer a cash component to an individual beneficiary. The same page says Aadhaar is preferred because it helps target the intended person, and that Aadhaar is not mandatory in DBT schemes.

## The certificate and the window: e-District, DigiLocker, UMANG

e-District is a Mission Mode Project under the National e-Governance Plan, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology and implemented by state governments. The [3 August 2022 Lok Sabha reply](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1847837) lists the services that design was built for: birth, caste, death, income, and local-resident certificates; old-age, disability, and widow pensions; and selected revenue, labour, and skilling services. That reply's count, 4,671 e-services in 709 districts, is a 2022 figure. The design point survives the date: the workflow sits with the state, at district and sub-district level.

DigiLocker, a MeitY flagship under Digital India and executed by the National e-Governance Division, is the wallet for documents fetched from issuers. UMANG, also NeGD and MeitY, is the mobile and web window. Its [landing page](https://web.umang.gov.in/landing/) describes one gateway for central, state, and local services, with documents such as Aadhaar, driving licence, PAN, and vehicle registration drawn from DigiLocker, on app, web, chatbot, and voice channels.

## What a policy reader should separate

A department that publishes a charter, ignores CPGRAMS, and hosts a PDF on a state site has met one module. A department that puts a form on UMANG and still prints the certificate only at a counter has a channel, not end-to-end delivery. A scheme that credits a bank account through DBT and never states who handles a failed credit has a payment rail without a redress owner. The policy change worth tracking is whether a named service uses all of the relevant rails, and which office remains accountable when one rail fails.

The May 2026 NeSDA cycle, covered in the [regulatory outlook](/blog/public-service-delivery-regulatory-outlook-mha-gov-20260330-459), is how DARPG now scores the online half of that question. It does not repeal Sevottam or the DBT framework.

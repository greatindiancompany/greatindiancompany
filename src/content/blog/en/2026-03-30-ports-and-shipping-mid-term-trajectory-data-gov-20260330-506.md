# Mid-term path for Indian ports: Sagarmala, 2030, and 2047

India's maritime plans are stacked on three clocks. Sagarmala is the programme already a decade old. Maritime India Vision 2030 is the medium horizon the Indian Ports Association still cites. Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047 is the far horizon the shipbuilding notes use. Mixing them produces a target with no owner.

## Sagarmala is the operating programme

The Union Cabinet approved Sagarmala on 25 March 2015. The National Perspective Plan was released on 14 April 2016 at the Maritime India Summit. The Sagarmala page states the aim as lower logistics cost for export, import, and domestic cargo, with limited infrastructure spend: a better modal mix for domestic cargo, bulk industry closer to the coast, manufacturing clusters near ports, and less time and cost on export-import containers.

The page lists five components: port modernisation, port connectivity, port-led industrialisation, coastal community development, and coastal shipping together with inland waterways. That fifth component is doing different work from the first. Modernisation adds berths and efficiency at ports the Centre or the states already run. Coastal shipping tries to move coal, cement, steel, foodgrain, fertiliser, and petroleum by sea instead of by road. The page says coastal traffic rose 118 percent over a decade and, writing against a 2025 horizon, estimates about 250 million metric tonnes a year of coastal traffic. It also says inland waterways were about 2 percent of freight as of 2025. Those sentences explain the pillar. They are not a fresh 2026 trajectory.

Connectivity is the constraint the page itself flags. Cargo is generated inland, lead distances are long, and a larger port that cannot evacuate is a larger queue. Indian Port Rail and Ropeway Corporation, from 2015, is the last-mile rail instrument. Port-led industry is narrower than "develop the coast": the page names coastal economic zones and units, and it singles out Kandla, Paradip, Ennore, and Visakhapatnam where land and deep water coincide.

The Indian Ports Association says its smart-port and green work lines up with Sagarmala, Maritime India Vision 2030, and Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047. Alignment is not the same as a published project scorecard. Monthly traffic notes on the association site are the near-term record. The vision documents are the direction.

## 2030 is a tug milestone; 2047 is a yard and fleet-energy milestone

The Ports Wing's Green Tug Transition Programme is one of the few mid-term targets with both years written down. At least half of tugs at major ports are to be green by 2030, on electricity, LNG, green methanol, or green hydrogen. All tugs are to be green by 2047. Harit Sagar, issued in May 2023, is the broader green-port frame. The page also says renewable energy's share at major ports is to rise to 60 percent, without a year on that line.

Shipyards use the far clock. The 27 December 2025 Press Information Bureau note says the assistance scheme and the development scheme remain valid until 31 March 2036, with an in-principle extension envisaged to 2047. It projects commercial shipbuilding capacity of about 4.5 million gross tonnage a year by 2047, and it creates an India Ship Technology Centre under the maritime university. The 2036 date is when the current scheme window closes. The 2047 date is the capacity language. A yard's bid in 2026 is inside the scheme window. It is not a claim that national output has reached the 2047 figure.

Trade dependence on the sea is the reason these clocks exist. The shipping division says about 95 percent of trade by volume and 68 percent by value moves by maritime transport. Sagarmala says more than 90 percent by volume. Either formulation means a mid-term port plan is a trade plan. It does not mean every coastal district industrialises on the same schedule. Community development, cruise circuits, and lighthouse tourism are on the Sagarmala page as livelihood work. They run on tourism demand, not on the tug calendar.

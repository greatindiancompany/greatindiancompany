# Where private investment sits once the rail is public

The investment question around India's digital public infrastructure is not whether the rails are busy. Several of them are, on the government's own counts. The question is who is allowed to earn a return, and which official accounts even try to show that return. They mostly do not. They show usage of a shared system, and they show what the shared system is forbidden to become.

## The public accounts are small next to the usage

On 29 July 2026 the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology gave the Lok Sabha the money it has put into two citizen platforms, beside user counts that run into the tens of crores.

DigiLocker, in that answer, had over 71.66 crore registered users and more than 936.03 crore documents accessible. Out of funds allocated since inception, amounting to ₹168.58 crore through the date of the answer, expenditure of ₹105.14 crore had been incurred. UMANG had over 11.85 crore registered users in the same answer, with ₹149.91 crore allocated since inception and ₹129.15 crore utilised.

Those are not the whole of digital public infrastructure. The same answer says the Digital India programme is an umbrella. Ministries and states have their own provisions. MeitY implements eight central-sector sub-schemes, and it published this allocation and expenditure for those schemes, in crore rupees:

| Financial year | Funds allocated | Expenditure |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2021-22 | 6,388.00 | 4,504.36 |
| 2022-23 | 5,400.50 | 3,863.13 |
| 2023-24 | 4,428.01 | 4,174.14 |
| 2024-25 | 4,173.00 | 3,784.93 |
| 2025-26 | 5,144.45 | 4,638.28 |

Read the scale, not a growth story. A document wallet used by more than 70 crore people was built and run, on this answer, with a low-hundreds-of-crores allocation over a decade. The MeitY umbrella is thousands of crores a year, which is material for a ministry and still not a proxy for the private businesses that send traffic across UPI or pull data through an Account Aggregator. NPCI's payment systems are not a line in this table. Neither is a private payments app.

The implication is structural. The cost of the shared layer, where the government publishes it, is not the pool of profit available to companies that use the layer. An investor who capitalises "DPI spend" as if it were the revenue pool of Indian fintech is mixing a public programme account with a private market.

## Some rails are incorporated so that they do not have owners in the usual sense

The Commerce Ministry's Lok Sabha reply of 4 August 2026 describes the Open Network for Digital Commerce as a section 8 company under the Companies Act, 2013, which is the not-for-profit form. The reply says ONDC is not a platform, an application, or a marketplace. As of July 2026 it had handled a cumulative total of over 500 million orders. The reply does not publish a take rate, a gross merchandise value, or a dividend.

That is the investment implication in one institutional fact. The network company is not the vehicle that captures commerce margin. Buyer applications, seller applications, logistics providers, and lenders that sit on the network might. The public answer does not show their economics. It shows participation: approximately 2 lakh or more active retail merchants, about 60 percent of them described as small sellers, and approximately 10 lakh or more service providers in mobility and logistics.

There is a public subsidy aimed at the edge, not at a listed network company. Under the MSME Trade Enablement and Marketing initiative, a sub-component of the World Bank-supported RAMP programme, a total outlay of ₹277.35 crore was approved for 2024-25 through 2026-27 to help micro and small enterprises join ONDC. The design targets 50 percent of beneficiary enterprises as women-owned. The money is for awareness, cataloguing, onboarding, logistics, and packaging support. It is not a valuation input.

## The Account Aggregator licence draws a hard line around the business

The Reserve Bank's Account Aggregator directions of 28 November 2025 are the clearest statement of what capital may not do.

An Account Aggregator is a non-banking financial company. No entity other than a company may do this business, and it needs a certificate of registration. The net owned fund requirement is not less than ₹2 crore, or a higher amount if the Reserve Bank specifies one. The leverage ratio must not exceed seven. The company stays in the base layer of the scale-based regulatory structure. It may not undertake any other business. It may not support customer transactions. Financial information it accesses must not reside with it. It may not use a third-party provider to carry out the aggregation business. It may not see the customer's login credentials at the bank or other provider. Information is shared only with the customer or with a financial information user the customer has authorised.

A financial information user, in these directions, is an entity registered with and regulated by a financial-sector regulator. The list of regulators named for this purpose is the Reserve Bank, SEBI, IRDAI, the Pension Fund regulator, and the Department of Revenue. A startup that is not in that perimeter cannot simply declare itself a user of bank statements.

The directions do leave a commercial surface. The board must approve a policy for pricing of services. An Account Aggregator may deploy investible surplus in instruments, not for trading. So a fee for the consent service is contemplated. The directions do not publish a fee schedule, and Sahamati's public dashboard does not report revenue. By July 2026 that dashboard showed 326.30 million cumulative linked accounts and 538.32 million cumulative consents fulfilled. Activity at that scale, with data forbidden to stay on the aggregator's servers, means the asset is the right to perform a consented pull, not a proprietary data warehouse.

For most investors the relevant company is therefore not the Account Aggregator. It is a regulated financial information user that becomes better at credit, insurance, or account opening because the pull is cheaper than collecting documents, or a financial information provider that has to answer those pulls. The public series stop before the unit economics of that second company.

## Health and documents: integration cost, not a concession

The health ministry's reply of 24 July 2026 says digital health applications are validated in the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission sandbox and undergo a web application security audit before they integrate. There is no centralised repository of health data. As of 20 July 2026 the mission reported 94.87 crore ABHA IDs, 5.36 lakh registered facilities, and 10.09 lakh registered professionals.

An investor underwriting a health-software company can treat those stocks as the size of the directory. The reply does not say what share of facility registrations convert into paid software, and it does not price the audit. The cost of admission is compliance work. The reply's third-party evaluation, conducted between June and August 2025, is summarised in qualitative language: reduced waiting times, cost savings, better access. The parliamentary text does not publish the study's magnitudes. Qualitative findings are not a revenue model.

DigiLocker is similar in structure and more extreme in the published budget. Tens of crores of registered users sit on a platform whose disclosed decade-long allocation is ₹168.58 crore. Issuers and relying parties build workflows around documents the platform can present. The platform's own account, as published, does not look like a toll road with a disclosed tariff.

## What an investment memo can honestly say

Three statements follow from the documents, and a fourth does not.

First, usage of the shared layers is large enough, on official counts, that distribution is no longer the scarce fact. UPI's August 2026 volume was 24,508.96 million transactions on NPCI's product statistics. DigiLocker users were reported above 72 crore at the end of July. ABHA IDs were reported at 94.87 crore in the same month.

Second, the published public money and the corporate form of ONDC say the centre of the rail is not where a typical equity return is designed to sit.

Third, regulation parks the return at the edge, and only for firms that accept a role: issuer, regulated information user, network participant, or health application that clears a sandbox and an audit. The Account Aggregator itself is a narrow utility with an explicit ban on transactions and on storing the data.

The statement that does not follow is a number for the private profit pool. None of the releases cited here report take rates, app-level UPI share, or credit disbursed through Account Aggregators. A memo that needs those figures has to get them from the company, not from the rail.

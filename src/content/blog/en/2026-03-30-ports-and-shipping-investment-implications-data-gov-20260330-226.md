# What the shipbuilding package implies for maritime investment

Public money in Indian maritime industry is no longer one "port budget." A concession at a major port, a grant to a shipyard, a maritime finance company, and a development fund pay for different assets and fail in different ways. The figures below are the ones the ministry and the Press Information Bureau have stated. They are not a forecast of returns.

## Three instruments inside one package

The Lok Sabha reply posted on 24 July 2026 states a shipbuilding package of ₹69,725 crore, in three parts.

The Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme, described as an extension of the earlier assistance policy, has an outlay of ₹24,736 crore. It exists because Indian yards face a cost disadvantage against yards elsewhere. The 27 December 2025 bureau note says assistance runs from 15 to 25 percent per vessel, by category, paid against milestones, with extra support for series orders. A ship owner who scraps a vessel at an Indian yard can receive a credit note equal to 40 percent of scrap value, tied to a new build. The same note says the scheme is expected to support projects worth about ₹96,000 crore over a decade. That is the ministry's expectation in the note, not contracts already signed. The July reply's figure for in-principle approvals over two years is a contract value of ₹15,234 crore.

The Maritime Development Fund has a corpus of ₹25,000 crore: a Maritime Investment Fund of ₹20,000 crore and an interest incentivisation fund of ₹5,000 crore. Guidelines were issued on 20 February 2026. SBI Ventures Limited was appointed fund manager on 26 May 2026. Until that fund reports what it has financed, the corpus is capacity to lend or invest, not a deployed book.

The Shipbuilding Development Scheme has an outlay of ₹19,989 crore. Greenfield clusters get full capital support for common maritime and internal infrastructure through a 50:50 Centre-state special purpose vehicle. Existing yards can seek 25 percent capital assistance for brownfield work such as dry docks, shiplifts, fabrication shops, and automation. The July reply says three clusters, in Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu, have in-principle approval, and four yards have in-principle approval for brownfield augmentation at an estimated cost of over ₹2,500 crore. Both schemes run to 31 March 2036, with an in-principle line of sight to 2047.

Infrastructure status, notified on 19 September 2025, sits beside the package. It covers Indian-flagged commercial vessels of 10,000 gross tonnage and above, and vessels built in India of 1,500 gross tonnage and above. Lenders will treat that classification under their own infrastructure rules. The notification does not itself price a ship.

## Port capital is still a concession, not this package

Major-port expansion remains a landlord problem. The Ports Wing promotes public-private partnerships and model concession agreements. Private capital in a berth, a mechanised terminal, or a tank farm is paid by the port's tariff and by volume, under a contract the Adjudicatory Board can now review if the project is stressed. That cash flow is not the shipyard subsidy.

Sagarmala Finance Corporation Limited is a third pocket. The Sagarmala page says the old Sagarmala Development Company, incorporated on 31 August 2016, was recast as this corporation and registered with the Reserve Bank of India as a non-banking financial company on 19 June 2025, as a Miniratna Category-I public enterprise. Its job is maritime finance. It is not a port authority and it is not the Maritime Investment Fund.

Last-mile rail is a fourth pocket. Indian Port Rail and Ropeway Corporation Limited, a 2015 joint venture of major ports and Rail Vikas Nigam, is the connectivity vehicle the Sagarmala page describes. A rail siding can decide whether a terminal earns its concession. It is easy to leave it out of a port model because it sits in a different company.

The shipping division counts 61 shipyards: eight central public sector, two state, and 51 private. Assistance, cluster grants, and the fund will not land evenly across that list. Demand aggregation through the ministry's technical committee is how central public enterprises are supposed to bunch orders. A private yard outside that pool still has to win a commercial contract.

# Why India's job figures are a poor fit for global league tables

India publishes more than one unemployment rate, and the gap between them is large enough to move a country around a league table. The honest comparison starts inside India's own releases. It does not start with a single number set next to another country's monthly jobless rate.

## Two rates, both official, not interchangeable

In the Periodic Labour Force Survey for July 2023–June 2024, the usual-status unemployment rate for persons aged 15 and above was 3.2 percent, the same for men and for women. Usual status looks back over the year. Principal activity is the status that occupied a person for a relatively long part of those 365 days. Subsidiary status picks up additional work. Someone who was employed for much of the year and unemployed in a particular week can still be a worker in this measure.

The calendar-year 2024 press note from the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation reports current weekly status, which uses the seven days before the interview. On that measure, all-India unemployment was 4.9 percent in January–December 2024. Urban weekly-status unemployment was 6.7 percent. Urban women's weekly-status unemployment was 8.2 percent. All-India weekly-status labour-force participation was 56.2 percent, unchanged from calendar 2023.

A Directorate General of Employment note on the employment situation records the April–June 2024 urban quarterly bulletin at a weekly-status unemployment rate of 6.6 percent for persons aged 15 and above. The July–September 2024 quarterly bulletin, which is urban and weekly, put labour-force participation at 50.4 percent, against 49.3 percent in the same quarter of 2023. Women's urban weekly participation in that quarter was 25.5 percent.

None of these is a correction of the others. A person reading "India's unemployment is about 3 percent" has chosen the easiest of the official concepts and dropped the urban week.

## What a foreign monthly rate is usually doing

Many national statistical offices publish a monthly or quarterly rate from a labour-force survey that classifies people by what they did in a recent reference week, and that treats a person as unemployed only if they were without work, available, and actively seeking it. India's weekly-status rate is the closer cousin of that object. India's usual-status rate is not. It will sit lower whenever work is seasonal, casual, or bunched into part of the year, which is a large part of rural employment.

The long usual-status improvement is still a fact about India. The same 2023–24 press note records rural unemployment falling from 5.3 percent in 2017–18 to 2.5 percent in 2023–24, and urban unemployment from 7.7 percent to 5.1 percent. The Directorate General of Employment summarises the all-India usual-status rate as declining from 6.0 percent in 2017–18 to 3.2 percent in 2023–24, with the worker population ratio rising from 46.8 percent to 58.2 percent. That is a domestic time series. It becomes a global rank only after someone has aligned the concept, the age cutoff, and the treatment of unpaid family workers. This page does not invent that alignment.

## Coverage and gig shares are also easy to mis-rank

On 21 November 2025 the Ministry of Labour and Employment said social-security coverage had risen from about 19 percent of the workforce in 2015 to more than 64 percent in 2025, and that the labour codes align India's framework with global standards. Both sentences are the Ministry's. Coverage in that usage is not the PLFS unemployment rate, and it is not a count of workers who received a contributory pension in the last month. Using 64 percent as if it were an International Labour Organization league-table cell, without the ILO report's own definition in front of you, overclaims what the press note contains.

NITI Aayog's 2022 report estimated that gig workers were 7.7 million in 2020–21, about 1.5 percent of India's total workforce and 2.6 percent of the non-agricultural workforce, and that they could be 23.5 million by 2029–30, about 4.1 percent of total livelihood and 6.7 percent of the non-agricultural workforce. Those are India estimates built from worker characteristics in survey data. They are not a measured share that can be subtracted from a US or European platform-economy share published on a different definition. The report's own skill split — about 47 percent medium-skilled, 22 percent high-skilled, and 31 percent low-skilled — is a description of that estimate, not a global skills ranking.

## The comparison that does hold

The useful benchmark is internal, and it is starker than most cross-country tables.

In July 2023–June 2024, usual-status participation was 78.8 percent for men and 41.7 percent for women. The women's figure is the one that moved: the press note records it at 23.3 percent in 2017–18. Urban weekly-status participation for women was still 25.5 percent in July–September 2024 and 25.8 percent across calendar 2024. Rural usual-status unemployment in 2023–24 was 2.5 percent; urban was 5.1 percent.

Anyone comparing India with another country should at least survive this test. Say whether the Indian number is usual status or current weekly status. Say whether it is all-India or urban. Say whether "coverage" means the Ministry's 2025 statement or a contributory membership count. If the sentence cannot do those three things, it is not a benchmark. It is a slogan with a flag next to it.

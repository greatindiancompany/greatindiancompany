For a household, the RBI’s repo rate shows up in three places: the interest rate on a floating-rate loan, the rate on a term deposit, and the prices of food, fuel and services that the inflation target is meant to stabilise. The August 2026 decision left the repo rate at 5.25 per cent. The household effect of that pause has to be read off the transmission table and the price data, not off the headline rate alone.

## Loans linked to the repo, and loans that are not

From February 2025 to June 2026 the policy repo rate fell 125 basis points. The August Bulletin says the external benchmark-based lending rate of 32 domestic banks fell by the same 125 basis points. Where a household loan resets off that benchmark, the benchmark itself fell one for one with the repo rate. The rate the household pays still includes the spread the bank sets on top. The Bulletin does not publish a separate housing or vehicle basis-point figure, so those products should not be assigned the 125 basis point move automatically.

Other benchmarks did not move one for one. Over the same cycle the median one-year MCLR fell 50 basis points. The weighted average lending rate on fresh rupee loans fell 80 basis points, and the rate on outstanding rupee loans fell 91 basis points. The Bulletin says this lending-rate pass-through was broad-based across major sectors. Those are economy-wide averages, not a housing-loan or vehicle-loan series.

The hold is already in the monthly data. In June 2026, with the repo rate unchanged, the fresh-loan weighted average rose 2 basis points, after a 1 basis point rise in May. The external benchmark rate was unchanged in both months. A borrower whose reset is purely external-benchmark will not see a new cut from the August pause, because there is no new cut to transmit. A borrower repricing in the fresh-loan pool was already seeing a small firming before the August meeting.

## Deposits lagged the cuts, then turned up

Households are also savers. Over the easing cycle the weighted average domestic term deposit rate fell 63 basis points on fresh deposits and 51 basis points on outstanding deposits, against the 125 basis point repo cut. In June alone, fresh deposit rates rose 16 basis points, and outstanding deposit rates rose 1 basis point.

The asymmetry is the point. Through the easing cycle, outstanding loan rates fell by more (91 basis points) than outstanding deposit rates (51). In the latest month of the table, fresh deposit rates rose faster than fresh loan rates. The Bulletin attributes the hardening to rising credit demand. Scheduled commercial bank credit was growing 19.3 per cent year on year as on 31 July 2026, and deposits 15.4 per cent.

## Where the stock of household credit actually grew

Personal loans, on the sectoral return for 30 June 2026, were up 15.8 per cent year on year. Inside that:

| Personal-loan category | Year-on-year growth, 30 June 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| Housing | 11.0 per cent |
| Vehicles | 17.3 per cent |
| Education | 13.3 per cent |
| Other personal loans | 14.2 per cent |
| Credit-card outstanding | 1.9 per cent |
| Loans against gold jewellery | 93.8 per cent |

The gold-jewellery line carries a classification warning in the table notes: since May 2024 one bank has reclassified a category of agricultural loan into loans against gold jewellery. The 93.8 per cent figure is not a clean reading of household gold-loan demand. Housing, at 11.0 per cent, was the slowest of the large retail categories and still a double-digit stock. The Bulletin’s narrative says personal loans picked up in June, supported by housing loans and loans against gold jewellery. NBFC retail loans, including housing loans from housing-finance companies, were also described as robust.

## The prices the target is aimed at

The August resolution’s inflation print is CPI at 4.4 per cent in June 2026, the first reading above the 4 per cent target after 16 months below it. The August Bulletin, which has one more month, puts headline CPI at 4.38 per cent in June and 4.45 per cent in July. Urban inflation was 4.0 per cent in July and rural inflation 4.8 per cent, against 3.9 and 4.7 per cent in June.

Fuel at the pump had already jumped before that. The June resolution says retail petrol and diesel prices were raised cumulatively by 7.4 per cent and 8.4 per cent from May, a direct impact of about 36 basis points on headline inflation, with further effects still to come. The August Bulletin says those retail prices then stayed unchanged in July and in August up to the 21st. Across four metros, the Indian Oil average was ₹108.7 a litre for petrol and ₹98.1 for diesel, and non-subsidised LPG was ₹952.3 a cylinder.

Food and restaurants are the other household channel. The Governor’s statement records food-and-beverages inflation at 5.1 per cent in June, and restaurant and accommodation services at 6.9 per cent, up from 4.2 per cent in April. Core inflation stayed at 3.9 per cent in July. Core excluding precious metals rose from 2.5 per cent in June to 2.7 per cent in July. The resolution’s own test is whether food and fuel stay in those categories or spread. As of these documents, the spread into core excluding precious metals was still limited.

Governor Sanjay Malhotra told the August minutes that average inflation was two per cent in the year the policy rate was brought down to 5.25 per cent. July’s 4.45 per cent reading is a different price world for the same nominal repo rate. The committee’s response, so far, has been to hold the rate and keep the stance neutral until it can see whether that gap persists.

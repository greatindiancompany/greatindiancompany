## The bottleneck moved

For a decade the obvious bottleneck on India's dedicated freight corridors was unfinished track. That bottleneck has a closing date in the official record. On 19 March 2025, 2,741 of 2,843 km were operational and the balance section was still under way. DFCCIL records trial runs on the JNPT–New Saphale (Vaitarna) section on 31 March 2026 as completion of the Western corridor. On 7 August 2026 the Railways Minister told the Rajya Sabha that both the Eastern corridor (1,337 km, Ludhiana–Sonnagar) and the Western corridor (1,506 km, Dadri–JNPT) have been commissioned.

The binding constraints now sit beside the track: the next corridor's paperwork, the port's last mile, park projects that several agencies share, and operators who are not on the digital platforms.

## A report is not a railway

Dankuni–Surat was announced in the Union Budget 2026. The August 2026 reply says preparation and updation of the detailed project report has been taken up. The 1 February 2026 briefing described 2,052 km, a path through Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra, a tie-in to the Western corridor, and nearly ₹1.20 lakh crore. None of those sentences is a commissioning date. The March 2025 reply had already shown how long the previous gate can stay shut: three other reports were under examination and unsanctioned, with viability, traffic, and funds named as the tests.

Train density is the operating bottleneck on the lines that do exist. The February 2026 briefing called the commissioned corridors near saturation at around 400 freight trains a day. The August reply put the average near 443. A shipper who assumes the new track automatically has room is reading the minister's saturation remark backwards. Terminals are the relief valve the same reply documents: 142 Gati Shakti Cargo Terminals, estimated capacity 224 million tonnes a year, 146 million tonnes handled in 2025-26. A terminal still has to be at the place the cargo originates. The policy locates them by industry demand and railway infrastructure, not by a uniform grid.

## Hinterland roads and parks

Port and coastal connectivity roads under Bharatmala are the published landside gap. As on 31 October 2024, 18 projects covering 424 km had been awarded and 189 km had been constructed, for major and minor ports in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and West Bengal. Awarded-but-unbuilt kilometres are an execution gap with a number.

Sagarmala's own design treats port-connectivity enhancement as a pillar, covering road, rail, and waterway links between the port and its hinterland. As of 19 March 2025, 272 of about 839 identified projects were complete. The unfinished book is the programme-level bottleneck. It is not a statement about any one port's gate.

Multimodal logistics parks are the transfer bottleneck, the place where rail, road, and containers are supposed to meet. The road ministry on 15 February 2022 identified 35 parks under Bharatmala Phase I, with Jogighopa under construction and bids invited for Nagpur (with JNPT at Sindi), Chennai (Mappedu, with the port and SIPCOT), and Bengaluru (with KIADB). DPIIT on 16 September 2025 said parks are being developed and that harmonising planning and implementation across agencies is still the work. The bottleneck named in 2025 is coordination, not a missing concept.

## Digital and cross-border gaps

DPIIT's September 2025 note says infrastructure gaps, regulatory harmonisation, and digital literacy among smaller logistics operators still limit seamless multimodal movement. The Unified Logistics Interface Platform and the Logistics Data Bank — more than 30 systems, over 160 crore transactions as of August 2025, more than 75 million containers across 101 inland container depots — are the systems a larger exporter can already use. The note's point is that smaller operators are not automatically on them.

On the international side, the 2014 dry run of the International North-South Transport Corridor, reported to the Lok Sabha on 20 March 2015, found irregular sailings from India to Iran, weak insurance cover for bills of lading, and a missing non-vessel-owning common-carrier layer. Those are execution gaps of service and paperwork. Because the corridor is a multilateral agreement, the reply said the fixes had to be negotiated with the other parties. File the list as 2015, and as the type of gap that track inside India does not close.

# India's ports and shipyards against the benchmark the ministry uses

India's maritime ministries do not publish one league table that says "here is the gap." They publish several comparisons, with different dates. Using the wrong one is how a global benchmark becomes a rumour. This page stays with comparisons the ministry or the Press Information Bureau has written down.

## The yard gap is a cost gap

The official reason for shipyard aid is cost, not a missing coastline. The 24 July 2026 Lok Sabha reply says the Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme, outlay ₹24,736 crore, addresses the cost disadvantage of Indian shipyards against global shipyards. The 27 December 2025 note turns that into a rate: 15 to 25 percent of vessel cost, by category, with milestone payments. That rate is the government's own measure of the gap it is willing to pay. It is not a measured cost delta for every ship type.

The same December note projects commercial shipbuilding capacity of about 4.5 million gross tonnage a year by 2047. The July reply states 4.5 million gross tonnage a year as the capacity the development scheme is meant to build, in line with Maritime India Vision 2030 and Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047. Read the year as 2047 when you are quoting the December note. Do not slide it forward into a 2030 delivery promise the reply does not make.

Output today is a different unit from that capacity aim. The July reply cites UN Trade and Development for a rise in India's shipbuilding output from 40,923 gross tonnage in 2024 to 57,637 gross tonnage in 2025, which the reply calls a 41 percent increase. This brief does not reopen the UN table. The comparison that matters is the one the reply itself sets up: recent output is in the tens of thousands of gross tonnes, and the capacity language is in the millions. Closing that distance is the point of clusters, brownfield docks, and the India Ship Technology Centre. It is not a result already booked.

## The fleet page is an old photograph

The shipping division says about 95 percent of India's trade by volume and 68 percent by value moves by sea. The Sagarmala page says more than 90 percent by volume and does not repeat the value share. Both sentences can be true as rounded programme language. Neither is a quarterly trade release.

On the same shipping page, India's cargo fleet is given as 1,411 vessels and 12.79 million gross tonnage, ranked 17th, average age 18.03 years, as on 30 April 2019, against 1.92 lakh gross tonnage at independence. Leave that ranking in 2019. A 2026 investment memo that still says "17th" is quoting a page the ministry has not redated.

## Waterways versus the developed-economy line

The Sagarmala page draws the modal comparison the coastal debate usually wants. It says that, as of 2025, inland waterways carry about 2 percent of India's freight, against more than 30 percent in developed economies. It says coastal shipping traffic rose 118 percent over the previous decade, and it estimates coastal traffic of about 250 million metric tonnes a year by 2025, with an inland-waterway cargo aim of 150 million metric tonnes a year by 2025. Those are the page's markers. Several of them are aimed at a year that has now passed. They explain why Sagarmala treats coastal shipping and inland waterways as a pillar. They are not a substitute for the Indian Ports Association's monthly major-port notes.

Port productivity is benchmarked inside India before it is benchmarked abroad. The Ports Wing ranks major ports on turnaround, berth output, detention, dwell, and evacuation under the Sagar Aankalan guidelines. A port that looks small in a global container list can still be the right benchmark for a bulk berth in Paradip or Deendayal. Container league tables and bulk productivity answer different trades.

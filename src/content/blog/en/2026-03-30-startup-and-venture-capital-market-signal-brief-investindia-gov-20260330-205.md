The number that travels furthest is the DPIIT recognition stock. The number that describes venture capital is much smaller, and it is published separately. Mixing them produces a market signal the government statistics do not support.

## Two official series

As on 30 June 2026, DPIIT had recognised 2,40,092 entities, according to a Lok Sabha reply. Recognition is a self-certified application against G.S.R. 108(E): entity type, age, turnover, and an innovation or employment claim, excluding businesses formed by a split or reconstruction. The Startup India page states that test. Clearing it puts an entity on a government list and opens the door to specified schemes. It does not record that an investor has priced equity.

The capital series in the 17 April 2026 PIB release is the Fund of Funds for Startups at the end of financial year 2025-26. More than ₹7,000 crore had been disbursed to over 135 SEBI-registered alternative investment funds. Those funds had invested more than ₹26,900 crore in over 1,420 startups. A Rajya Sabha reply dated 13 February 2026 gives an earlier precise cut: as on 31 December 2025, supported funds had invested ₹25,547.98 crore in 1,371 startups across 29 states and Union Territories.

Set 1,420 beside 2.23 lakh, the 31 March 2026 recognition stock in the same April PIB release. The fund-of-funds portfolio is a thin slice of the registry. That gap is the market signal. Most recognised entities are not, on these prints, portfolio companies of the government-backed fund programme.

## What a billion-dollar count is doing in the same conversation

PIB’s press note of 15 January 2026, written for a decade of Startup India, says something else again. It describes India’s high-value startup landscape as having grown from four privately held companies valued above $1 billion in 2014 to over 120 such firms, with a combined valuation above $350 billion. The same note says India had over 2 lakh DPIIT-recognised startups as of December 2025.

Those sentences answer different questions. One is a valuation description of privately held companies above a dollar threshold. The other is a count of entities that met a gazette test, most of them far below any billion-dollar conversation. PIB does not, in that note, name a private league-table vendor, and this brief does not import one. A reader who wants the dollar threshold should cite the 15 January 2026 note and keep it out of the DPIIT column.

The note also says around 50 percent of startups were emerging from Tier-II and Tier-III cities. It does not publish the city list or the classification rule behind that share. Treat “around 50 percent” as PIB’s summary, not as a table you can recompute.

## How a fund manager should read the tape

A rising recognition count says the application pipe is open and, after February 2026, that the pipe is wider. Financial year 2025-26 added more than 55,200 recognitions. April–June 2026 added 16,334 more. That is administrative throughput.

A rising fund-of-funds investment total says SEBI-registered managers who received government commitments found companies they would back. The December 2025 stock was 1,371 companies and ₹25,547.98 crore. The March 2026 description was over 1,420 companies and more than ₹26,900 crore. The step-up is real and still three orders of magnitude smaller than the registry.

Seed and credit sit in between, and they are also not the registry. By the end of 2025-26, Startup India Seed Fund Scheme incubators had approved over ₹605 crore to more than 3,400 startups, and the Credit Guarantee Scheme for Startups had guaranteed more than 410 loans amounting to over ₹1,250 crore. Useful, specific, and still a small fraction of 2.4 lakh names.

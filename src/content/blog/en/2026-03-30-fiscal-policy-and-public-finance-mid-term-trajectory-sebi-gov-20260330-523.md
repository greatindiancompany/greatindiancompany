Medium term, in the 2026-27 Budget, means a debt ratio the government wants lower by 2030-31, and a deficit it resets each year to stay on that slope. It does not mean a published table of deficits for 2027-28 and 2028-29. The fiscal statement says those rolling targets are not being provided, because the global environment is too uncertain.

## The path that is written down

The speech traces the aim to Budget 2025-26: Central government debt at 50±1 percent of GDP by 2030-31. The 2026-27 estimates put the ratio at 55.6 percent, against 56.1 percent in the 2025-26 revised estimates. The operational instrument is the fiscal deficit, estimated at 4.3 percent of GDP after 4.4 percent in the revised estimates. The speech says the older commitment, to be below 4.5 percent by 2025-26, has been met.

The statement places the decline since the pandemic consolidation began: 6.7 percent of GDP in 2021-22 to 4.4 percent in the 2025-26 revised estimates. From 2026-27 to 2030-31, it says, each year’s deficit is to be set so that debt keeps falling.

Arithmetic without a formula is still visible. Five years separate 55.6 percent from the middle of the 50±1 band. The step now being taken is 0.5 percentage point, from 56.1 to 55.6. Repeating a half-point decline each year would cover the distance to 50 percent. The documents do not promise that the step size stays at half a point. They explicitly decline to publish the next two years. A straight line drawn from 55.6 to 50 is a reader’s sketch, not a government table.

## What sits inside 55.6 percent

The FRBM definition used here includes external debt at current exchange rates, public-account liabilities, and NSSF special securities of the states. Those state securities are 0.5 percent of GDP in the 2026-27 estimates and 0.7 percent in the revised estimates. Debt excluding them is 55.1 percent and 55.5 percent. A trajectory quoted as 55.6 that later switches to the net figure has not improved by half a point. It has changed definition.

External liabilities are 3.3 percent of the stock at book value. The ratio can still move with the exchange rate. The statement treats that as a small share, mostly multilateral, not as the driver of the glide path.

Nominal GDP is assumed to grow 10.0 percent in 2026-27 over the first advance estimates for 2025-26. Debt-to-GDP falls when debt grows more slowly than nominal GDP. The first advance estimates themselves put nominal growth in 2025-26 at 8 percent. The Budget’s 10 percent is a step up from that estimate. If nominal growth comes in closer to 8 than to 10, the same rupee debt produces a higher ratio. The statement does not publish a sensitivity table. The dependence is still in the method.

## Interest is the reason the path is supposed to matter

The speech says a falling debt ratio gradually reduces the share of resources going to interest. The near-term estimates do not show that relief yet. Interest payments are ₹14.04 lakh crore in 2026-27, against a Budget estimate of ₹12.76 lakh crore for 2025-26. The primary deficit, which is the fiscal deficit minus interest, narrows only from 0.8 percent of GDP to 0.7 percent. Most of the consolidation in the table is not a collapse in the non-interest deficit. It is a small move, while the coupon bill in rupees is still rising.

The revenue deficit stays at 1.5 percent of GDP. A debt path that leaves the revenue deficit unchanged is leaning on capital spending and on nominal GDP, not on a sudden surplus in the revenue account. Receipts are estimated to cover 85.6 percent of revenue expenditure.

## What would confirm or break the story

Confirmation, inside the government’s own frame, is a revised debt ratio for 2026-27 that is not above 55.6 percent, and a following Budget that still shows a lower ratio. A fiscal deficit that stays near 4.3 percent can still miss the debt aim if GDP is weaker or if liabilities outside the year’s deficit, including the exchange-rate valuation of external debt, rise.

A break would be a return of the ratio toward 56 percent or higher in the revised estimates, or a statement that again withholds the path without showing the current year on the slope. The Act’s older endeavours — a 3 percent deficit and Central debt of 40 percent — remain unmet, and the deviation statement says so. The medium-term trajectory the government is actually asking to be judged on is the declining debt path, not a claim that those older ceilings have been reached.

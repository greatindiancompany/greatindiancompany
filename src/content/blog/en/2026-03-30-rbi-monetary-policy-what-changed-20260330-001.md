The Reserve Bank of India’s Monetary Policy Committee met for the 62nd time from 3 to 5 August 2026 and left the policy repo rate at 5.25 per cent. The standing deposit facility rate stayed at 5.00 per cent. The marginal standing facility rate and the Bank Rate stayed at 5.50 per cent. The stance stayed neutral. The vote was unanimous.

That is the same decision the committee took at its 61st meeting, from 3 to 5 June 2026. A reader who stops at the rate will miss the meeting. The August resolution revised the forecast around an unchanged instrument.

## The rate was already on hold

By the June meeting the easing cycle described in the Governor’s 5 August statement had already delivered a cumulative 125 basis point reduction in the repo rate, dated from February 2025. Both the June and August resolutions are pauses on top of that cycle. Neither meeting cut the rate, and neither meeting raised it.

The operating corridor did not move either. The gap from the standing deposit facility to the repo, and from the repo to the marginal standing facility, remains 25 basis points on each side.

## What the forecast did

The June resolution projected real GDP growth for 2026-27 at 6.6 per cent, with the first quarter at 6.6 per cent, the second at 6.3 per cent, the third at 6.5 per cent and the fourth at 6.8 per cent. It projected CPI inflation for the year at 5.1 per cent, with the first quarter at 4.2 per cent, the second at 5.1 per cent, the third at 5.9 per cent and the fourth at 5.4 per cent. Core inflation, CPI excluding food and fuel, was projected at 4.7 per cent.

The August resolution raised the 2026-27 growth projection to 6.7 per cent and set the quarterly path at 7.0, 6.4, 6.5 and 6.8 per cent. It projected growth in the first quarter of 2027-28 at 7.3 per cent, with risks evenly balanced. CPI inflation for 2026-27 was lowered to 5.0 per cent, with the second quarter at 4.7 per cent, the third at 5.9 per cent and the fourth at 5.5 per cent. Inflation in the first quarter of 2027-28 was projected at 5.3 per cent, again with risks evenly balanced. Core inflation for 2026-27 was projected at 4.3 per cent.

| | June resolution | August resolution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repo rate | 5.25 per cent | 5.25 per cent |
| Stance | Neutral | Neutral |
| GDP, 2026-27 | 6.6 per cent | 6.7 per cent |
| CPI, 2026-27 | 5.1 per cent | 5.0 per cent |
| Core CPI, 2026-27 | 4.7 per cent | 4.3 per cent |
| CPI peak quarter | Q3 at 5.9 per cent | Q3 at 5.9 per cent |

The year-ahead inflation peak did not move. Both resolutions still have headline inflation at 5.9 per cent in the third quarter of 2026-27. What softened was the full-year average, and the core projection.

## Why the committee rewrote the path

The August resolution records that CPI inflation rose to 4.4 per cent in June 2026 after 16 consecutive months below the target. The Governor’s statement of the same day gives the quarterly comparison: realised headline inflation in the first quarter of 2026-27 was 3.9 per cent, against the 4.2 per cent projected in the June policy, a shortfall of 30 basis points. The resolution attributes the June rise mainly to food and fuel, and says core inflation was unchanged at 3.9 per cent in May and June. Core excluding precious metals was lower still, at 2.3 to 2.5 per cent in that period.

In the minutes published on 19 August, Deputy Governor Poonam Gupta ties the forecast revision to the oil assumption used in successive projections. She states that the April policy assumed oil at US$85 a barrel, the June policy at US$95, and that a reasonable reading of oil futures at the August meeting was about US$90 for the year. She describes the August numbers as a 10 basis point upward revision to growth and a 10 basis point downward revision to inflation relative to June.

Governor Sanjay Malhotra’s statement in those minutes puts the pause in a longer frame. He says average inflation was only two per cent in the year when the policy rate was brought down to 5.25 per cent, while headline inflation has already averaged 3.93 per cent in the current year, and that this normalisation may in time call for a recalibration. He also says the evidence so far does not show a supply shock turning into generalised inflation, unanchored expectations, or persistent inflation, which is the test he sets for a monetary response.

## What to carry into October

The next meeting is scheduled for 5 to 7 October 2026. The August text is explicit that the committee wants more clarity on the inflation path and its composition before it moves the rate, and that a neutral stance is how it keeps that choice open. The rate is the same as in June. The story that changed is the forecast: slightly firmer growth, a slightly lower inflation average, a lower core projection, and an unchanged third-quarter peak of 5.9 per cent.

# Remittances, the rupee, and the household side of India's trade gap

India's merchandise deficit is a national accounts fact. A household meets the external sector in three narrower places: money sent home from abroad, the rupee rate on the day cash is converted, and the imported-energy bill that sits inside the goods deficit. The Reserve Bank publishes the first and the third. It displays the second. It does not publish a household budget.

## Remittances are large enough to show up in the quarter

In April–June 2026, personal transfer receipts were **US$42.9 billion**. A year earlier they were US$33.2 billion. The RBI describes these receipts as mainly remittances by Indians employed overseas. They sit inside the secondary-income account. The net of that whole account was US$40.8 billion in the quarter, so personal transfers are the bulk of it and not the entire line.

In July 2026, net transfers were US$13.2 billion, against US$12.6 billion in July 2025. For April–July 2026 they were US$53.9 billion, against US$43.4 billion a year earlier. These are the monthly balance-of-payments lines released on 15 September 2026. They are preliminary.

Set next to the goods gap, the scale is easier to see. The April–June goods deficit was US$86.1 billion. Net services receipts were US$51.6 billion. Personal transfers of US$42.9 billion, together with services, are why a goods deficit of that size left a current account deficit of US$4.2 billion, or 0.5 per cent of GDP. The household transfer is not a rounding item in that arithmetic. The RBI table already contains the result: current-account credits of US$294.9 billion and debits of US$299.2 billion.

## The rate a family actually sees

At 1:00 pm on 24 September 2026 the RBI homepage showed FBIL rates of:

- **Rs 95.9099 per US dollar**
- Rs 127.0295 per pound sterling
- Rs 109.1912 per euro
- **Rs 26.1130 per UAE dirham**
- Rs 60.6200 per 100 Japanese yen

A family receiving dollars or dirhams converts at a bank or money-changer rate that tracks this class of benchmark. Gulf remittances are the reason the dirham line belongs on a household page. The homepage names FBIL as the source. The rate is for that hour. It is not the average rate at which the quarter's US$42.9 billion was converted, and the RBI release does not publish that average here.

A higher rupee-per-dollar rate raises the rupees received per dollar of remittance. The same move raises the rupee cost of a dollar-priced payment: a foreign university invoice, a dollar subscription, an imported device. Both statements follow from the quote. Neither statement is a measured change in any particular family's income. The releases used here do not report median remittance size, the number of recipient households, or retail fuel prices.

## Oil is the goods line with a household path

Inside the April–June goods account, petroleum, oil and lubricants exports were US$23.0 billion and imports US$60.6 billion. The POL deficit was **US$37.6 billion**, against US$32.2 billion a year earlier, when imports were US$49.2 billion. That is the external oil bill on a payments basis for the quarter: US$37.6 billion inside a goods deficit of US$86.1 billion. The releases used here do not convert that bill into a retail fuel price.

Commerce customs data tell a related shipment story without being the same series. In the provisional DGCIS quick estimates, petroleum-product *exports* in August 2026 were US$6,807.86 million, up 63.27 per cent from US$4,169.70 million in August 2025. Exporting refined products and importing crude are different lines. A household does not receive the export number. The POL deficit in the RBI table is the closer description of the imported-energy pressure on the external account.

## What the trade gap does not tell a family

The April–July merchandise trade deficit on the Commerce basis was US$118.60 billion. That number measures goods crossing the customs frontier. It does not arrive as a bill in a household. Three published offsets sit between that gap and the current account: services, transfers, and income.

For April–July 2026 the RBI puts the merchandise deficit at US$117.8 billion on a payments basis, the services surplus at US$69.3 billion, net transfers at US$53.9 billion, and net income at −US$16.6 billion. The current account deficit for those four months is US$11.2 billion. The income line is mostly investment income paid to non-residents. It is not a household expense line either.

Reserves are the country's buffer, not a household balance. As on 11 September 2026 they stood at US$780,782 million. A week-to-week move of a few billion dollars, such as the US$4,924 million decline in that print, does not change a remittance. A sustained move in the rupee benchmark does.

## How to read the next print

When the RBI releases the next month, look first at personal transfers or the transfers line, then at the POL deficit if the quarter is being published, then at the homepage dollar and dirham rates. The merchandise deficit is context. It becomes a household fact only through those channels.

# The new test for a served airport, and who may bid

Two regulatory shifts are now in public text. One decides which regional routes the next UDAN round will subsidise. The other decides how a lessor gets an aircraft back. They come from different documents, and they bind different companies.

## The route rules

The Press Information Bureau’s 16 July 2026 comparison of Modified UDAN with the earlier scheme is the operative description until the scheme document itself is the file on a bidder’s desk.

An airline that does not hold an air operator permit cannot bid. The award is supposed to follow the business plan and the aircraft the airline has. Missing the start window means the route is re-bid and the first bidder is barred. That replaces a design in which bidding without a permit was allowed in order to attract new entrants.

The definition of a served airport is now a three-part test on the year before the scheme is published: more than 14 scheduled departures a week on average, connections to more than two city pairs, and more than 40,000 passengers in that year. Priority areas keep the city-pair and passenger tests and raise the departure test above 21 a week. The old test was a departure count alone — more than seven a week, or more than 14 in priority areas — taken from the last DGCA schedule.

Slots are a softer commitment. The comparison says efforts will be made to allocate slots at metro and major airports for regional flights at usable times. “Efforts will be made” is not a slot entitlement.

Fares stay regulated on the supported seats. Reduced fares still apply to 50 percent of seats. The old ceiling of 40 such seats per flight is removed when the aircraft has more than 80 seats. Viability-gap funding can last five years, at 75 percent, 50 percent, and 25 percent of the awarded amount in years three, four, and five.

On 30 July 2026 the ministry also restated a limit that has not changed: the government does not pick an airline’s aircraft type, market, or network. International flying still sits inside bilateral air-service agreements and the capacity those agreements allow. A regulatory outlook that treats Modified UDAN as a fleet plan for IndiGo or Air India has the wrong instrument.

## The safety oversight that already runs

The 6 August reply on airport expansion describes DGCA’s existing safety role in one paragraph, next to the passenger-amenity systems. Oversight is routine surveillance, spot checks, regulatory audits, and special audits against the Aircraft Rules and Civil Aviation Requirements. The reply does not announce a new audit programme. It records the one that applies while terminals are being rebuilt at the airports named in that answer, from Chennai and Kolkata to Leh and Pakyong.

## The lessor rules

On 8 May 2026, at the India Aircraft Leasing and Financing Summit in GIFT City, the civil aviation minister pointed to the Protection of Interests in Aircraft Objects Act, 2025, as the law that aligns India with the Cape Town Convention. His account of the problem it addresses is specific: lessors had faced long delays exercising IDERA deregistration rights, which raised costs for airlines and, in his telling, fares for passengers. He also said a high-level stakeholder committee on leasing, promised at the first summit, had been constituted.

DGCA’s role at that summit, as the PIB note records it, was to explain the framework for leasing, financing, and asset recovery, including Cape Town implementation. That is a financing regulation. It does not change the served-airport test, and the served-airport test does not repossess an aircraft.

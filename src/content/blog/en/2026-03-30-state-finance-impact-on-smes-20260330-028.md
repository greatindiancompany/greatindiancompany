# Own-tax revenue and the small firm's fiscal bill

A small firm meets state finance on two clocks. One is the challan: taxes the state assesses on the firm or on a transaction the firm signs. The other is the state's receipt mix: whether salaries, subsidies, and capital projects are funded by the state's own taxes, by Union transfers, or by borrowing. The second clock decides tender volume and how fast a vendor is paid. The Reserve Bank's *State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025-26* (23 January 2026) is the consolidated map of that second clock. It does not publish a firm-level tax invoice.

## What "own tax" includes

Own-tax revenue is money raised under the state's own taxing powers. It is distinct from the state's share of Union taxes and from grants. Chart II.4 of the study is the own-tax series. Statement 14 is tax revenue. Statement 15 is non-tax revenue, which includes fees, royalties, and interest receipts.

The heads a firm is most likely to recognise are these:

- State GST, on supplies that fall inside GST, administered with the rest of the GST system.
- Stamp duty and registration fees, when the firm takes a lease, buys a plot, or mortgages property. Box II.1 of the study is specifically about the revenue efficiency of stamp duty and registration fees across states.
- State excise, where the business is in a commodity the state still taxes, including alcoholic liquor for human consumption, which stayed with the states under GST.
- Value added tax on petroleum products that remain outside GST, which enters freight and generator cost.
- Motor vehicle tax on the fleet, and electricity duty on power drawn from the distribution company.
- Profession tax, in states that levy it, which is a small per-person charge with a compliance file of its own.

Rates and exemptions differ by state and by the year's finance act. This brief does not quote a rate. The state's latest budget and the tax department's notification are the rate sheet. The Reserve Bank table tells you whether that head is a large part of the state's own receipts.

## What the firm does not see on a challan

The Sixteenth Finance Commission memorandum, published with the Union Budget, keeps the states' share of the divisible pool at 41 per cent for the award period 2026-27 to 2030-31. The government accepted that recommendation, and accepted a related one: the Union should disclose the net proceeds certified by the Comptroller and Auditor General under Article 279. Devolution arrives as a book transfer into the state's consolidated fund. A firm pays it only in the indirect sense that Union taxes fund the pool.

Grants move the same way. Chart II.3 of the study tracks components of grants-in-aid. A grant for a centrally sponsored scheme can fund a project a small contractor builds, and the state's matching share can tighten the same budget that pays the bill.

Borrowing is the third receipt that never appears as a tax on the firm and often appears as a work order. The January study says the consolidated gross fiscal deficit, 3.3 per cent of GDP in 2024-25 and budgeted at 3.3 per cent for 2025-26, mainly reflects 50-year interest-free capital loans above the normal borrowing ceiling. Capital expenditure was 2.7 per cent of GDP in the two preceding years and is budgeted at 3.2 per cent for 2025-26. For a vendor, that capital line is the relevant one. The Department of Expenditure's 27 March 2026 guidelines allocate Rs 2,00,000 crore under the same loan scheme for 2026-27.

## Where a firm should look before it generalises

The all-state own-tax chart hides prohibition states, mining states, and states whose stamp duty is a large share of receipts. Box II.1 is the stamp-duty comparison the study chose to highlight. Statement 14 is the state-wise tax table. After that, the state's own budget speech and the Comptroller and Auditor General's finance accounts for that state are the documents that show arrears, guarantees, and whether capital bills were left unpaid. Those state documents are the place to stop. A national average will not tell a founder in one city what the local stamp duty or the local capital budget is doing.

Someone opening a school, affiliating a college, or issuing a skill certificate in India is not facing one regulator. The National Education Policy 2020 wants a simpler higher-education map. The map an operator files against today is the one that still has statutes. Confusing the two is how projects lose a year.

## Schools stay with boards, the school department, and a standard-setter

School recognition, affiliation, and examinations sit with state boards and with the central boards, under the Department of School Education and Literacy. Samagra Shiksha is the scheme that finances the school system from pre-school to senior secondary. It is not itself the licensing authority for a private school. State governments still recognise schools.

The policy proposes PARAKH, a National Assessment Centre, as a standard-setting body for student assessment across recognised school boards, for guiding state achievement surveys, and for the National Achievement Survey. Standard-setting is not the same job as granting affiliation. A board can be pushed toward a new assessment pattern and still be the body that declares a result.

NIPUN Bharat, the foundational literacy and numeracy mission launched on 5 July 2021, sits under Samagra Shiksha. Its norms are mission guidelines for a learning goal, aimed at Grade 3 by 2026-27. They do not replace the Right to Education recognition norms for a school building and a teacher.

Vocational exposure inside schools, which the policy wants from the middle and secondary stages, has to satisfy two regimes at once: the school board's curricular rules, and, where the course leads to a vocational certificate, the qualification rules below.

## Vocational certificates have a regulator of their own

The National Council for Vocational Education and Training was established by gazette notification on 5 December 2018 and has been operational since 1 August 2020. It subsumed the work of the National Skill Development Agency and the former National Council for Vocational Training. Its published mandate is to recognise and monitor awarding bodies, assessment agencies, and skill information providers, and to anchor the National Skills Qualifications Framework and the National Qualification Register. Training institutes are regulated indirectly, through the awarding bodies that affiliate them, rather than by NCVET licensing every classroom.

An awarding body, in NCVET's own description, certifies learners in qualifications it has developed or adopted, once those qualifications are NSQF-aligned and approved. It sets affiliation norms for training partners, onboards assessment agencies, and monitors both. A training company that prints a certificate without a recognised awarding body behind it is not inside this system, whatever the marketing says.

The Directorate General of Training, inside the skill ministry, runs the long-term side: the Craftsmen Training Scheme in ITIs, instructor training, and the National Skill Training Institutes. A Rajya Sabha reply of 1 April 2026 records two CTS courses built for newer industries, a Semiconductor Technician course at NSQF level 4.5 for students who have passed Class 12 with physics and mathematics, and a Green Hydrogen Production Technician course at NSQF level 3.5 for students who have passed Class 10. NSQF level is the regulatory label that lets those courses sit next to other qualifications. The Sector Skill Councils and the National Skill Development Corporation develop and roll out much of the short-term ecosystem. NCVET's recognition function is what is supposed to stop that ecosystem fragmenting into incomparable certificates. The Cabinet note that created NCVET said fragmentation was the problem it was set up to fix.

Apprenticeships are a contract regime as well as a qualification regime. Designated trades and optional trades under the National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme are not the same list. Optional trades are where an establishment can engage apprentices in roles beyond the designated list. The apprenticeship portal records the contract. A course approval from an awarding body does not, by itself, create an apprentice contract.

## Higher education: the proposal and the present tense

The policy's regulatory chapter proposes the Higher Education Commission of India as one umbrella with four independent verticals: a regulatory council, an accreditation council, a grants council, and a body for academic standards. The regulatory council is meant to be the single point for higher education, including teacher education, excluding medical and legal education. The text says this needs existing Acts to be reworked and repealed, and describes the intended style as "light but tight."

Until that repeal happens, the bodies named on the AISHE portal remain the operating ones: the University Grants Commission for universities and the All India Council for Technical Education for technical education, alongside the National Assessment and Accreditation Council. Professional courses in medicine and law stay outside the proposed single council even in the policy text. A private university project that waits for HECI before talking to UGC is waiting on a statute that the policy itself says must still be passed.

Multiple entry and exit, the Academic Bank of Credits, and the four-year multidisciplinary bachelor's degree are academic architecture from the same policy. They change how a student moves. They do not abolish the regulator that approves the institution.

The practical outlook, as of this update, is stable institutional split. Schools: state and board recognition, Samagra Shiksha money, PARAKH as the policy's assessment standard-setter. Vocational awards: NCVET, NSQF, awarding bodies, with DGT for ITIs. Apprenticeships: a separate contract. Colleges and universities: UGC, AICTE, and the accreditation system, with HECI still a proposal that requires Parliament to retire the current Acts. Any circular that claims one login now covers all four should be checked against that split before a team rebuilds its compliance calendar around it.

## Use the benchmark India wrote down

A country ranking for healthcare access is easy to paste and hard to defend. This brief does not assign India a 2026 place on a World Health Organization league table. The comparison that official documents actually support is the one India set for itself in the National Health Policy, 2017, and the one the National Health Authority draws between PM-JAY and the ceilings that came before it.

The Prime Minister’s Office note on Cabinet approval says the policy proposes raising public health expenditure to 2.5 percent of GDP in a time-bound way, putting up to two-thirds or more of resources into primary care, and providing most secondary care at district level. The policy text states the starting point it was using: government health expenditure at 1.15 percent of GDP, with 2.5 percent as the 2025 mark. It also asks states for health spending above 8 percent of their budgets by 2020, and a 25 percent cut in the proportion of households facing catastrophic health spending by 2025, measured from the levels then current. The health minister’s statement at approval adds two beds per 1,000 population, placed so that emergency care is reachable in the golden hour, and free drugs, diagnostics, and emergency and essential services in public hospitals.

Those sentences are the benchmark. A later brief can compare a health-accounts release with 2.5 percent. This one does not invent that comparison. By 24 September 2026 the policy’s 2025 date has passed. Passing a date is not the same fact as missing or hitting the number.

## The scheme’s own “before” picture

The Authority’s PM-JAY page uses a different and more concrete comparison. It says government-funded insurance before PM-JAY used annual family ceilings from ₹30,000 to ₹3,00,000, and that this fragmentation is what the ₹5 lakh family floater was built to replace. Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana, launched in 2008 and later subsumed, is described as a ₹30,000 cover with a five-member family cap. PM-JAY removes the family-size cap, covers pre-existing disease from day one, adds three days before admission and 15 days after, and makes the benefit portable across empanelled hospitals in India.

That is an internal benchmark: a higher ceiling, a wider family definition, and a national hospital network instead of a state-bounded one. It still covers listed secondary and tertiary hospitalization for eligible families. It is not a comprehensive benefit for every resident and every outpatient visit. Comprehensive primary care is the other Ayushman Bharat component, aimed at the whole catchment of a Health and Wellness Centre. Universal health coverage, which both the policy and the Authority invoke, is the horizon. The instruments are partial on purpose.

## What the 2015 accounts were doing on the page

The same PM-JAY page anchors its background in National Health Accounts 2015: government health spending about 1.2 percent of GDP, 21 percent of health spending from government revenue, and 62 percent out of pocket. Those are the figures the scheme’s authors put next to the word “underfunding.” They are twenty-fifteen figures printed on a living programme page. Quoting them as the current distance from 2.5 percent of GDP misstates the clock. The honest use is historical: this is the gap the 2017 policy and the 2018 scheme said they were answering.

Ayushman Bharat’s own text also ties the design to the Sustainable Development Goals’ line, “leave no one behind.” That is a commitment statement. It does not convert a SECC 2011 deprivation list into coverage of every household. States may add households at their own cost. People aged 70 and above were later brought in irrespective of income, by the Cabinet decision of 12 September 2024, on a separate ₹5 lakh family rule described in the Authority’s FAQ. Income-blind cover for that age group is a real widening. It is still not the whole population at every age.

## How to make a fair external comparison later

A serious external comparison needs a named series, a year, and a definition that matches the Indian target. Public spending as a share of GDP is comparable only if the other country’s series counts the same governments and the same services. Beds per 1,000 are comparable only if day-care and critical-care beds are classified the same way. Catastrophic spending needs the same threshold. Until those series are opened, the usable benchmarks are the ones in this note: ₹5 lakh against the older ₹30,000 to ₹3,00,000 band, primary care’s two-thirds claim, district secondary care, and the dated spending targets.

## The policy named a different kind of access

India’s National Health Policy, 2017, is the document Ayushman Bharat cites as its reason for existing. The Prime Minister’s Office, recording Cabinet approval, said the policy aims at universal health coverage and a move from selective primary care to a comprehensive package. That package, in the PMO note, includes geriatric, palliative, and rehabilitative care. The same note says the policy allocates a major share, up to two-thirds or more, of resources to primary care, and that it wants most secondary care delivered at district level rather than only at a medical college.

The policy text, as posted for public reading, sets a public-expenditure path from the then 1.15 percent of GDP to 2.5 percent by 2025, asks states to take health spending above 8 percent of their budgets by 2020, and seeks a 25 percent reduction from then-prevailing levels of catastrophic health spending by 2025. A statement by the health minister at approval also records an aspiration of two beds per 1,000 people and of free drugs, free diagnostics, and free emergency and essential care in public hospitals. Those are targets with dates and definitions. This page does not score them. Whether public spending reached 2.5 percent of GDP is a later national-accounts question, answered in a health-accounts release, not by repeating the target.

## What selective primary care had left out

The Ayushman Arogya Mandir about-page, maintained under the health ministry, describes the National Health Mission years as a period that strengthened reproductive and child health and selected communicable-disease programmes. It then says the service range at primary care did not match the rise in chronic disease. That is the shift in plain language. A sub-centre built for maternal care and a short list of infections was being asked to become the place where hypertension, diabetes, common cancers, mental-health screening, oral care, eye and ENT complaints, and palliative care also start.

The mandir page ties that idea to a design rule: convert sub-centres serving about 3,000 to 5,000 people, and primary health centres, so that time to care is no more than 30 minutes. The expanded list on that page has twelve service groups, from pregnancy and newborn care through emergency care and basic mental-health management. Free essential drugs and diagnostics are part of the same description. The user-facing price at that level is written as zero.

## The second half of the shift is a hospital assurance

The National Health Authority’s PM-JAY page says earlier government cover was a patchwork of ceilings from ₹30,000 to ₹3,00,000 a family, and that the Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana, launched in 2008, had a family cap of five. PM-JAY, launched on 23 September 2018 in Ranchi, is described there as ₹5 lakh a year on a family-floater basis, with no cap on family size, cashless at the hospital, and linked in design to the primary-care centres. The Authority’s own background text says the older schemes had no link to primary care. That sentence is the policy point. Hospital cover without a clinic nearby treats the admission. It does not treat the blood pressure that preceded it.

The two components are not interchangeable. A Health and Wellness Centre, now called an Ayushman Arogya Mandir, is for the whole population in its catchment. PM-JAY, on the Authority’s page, is for eligible families, described as the bottom 40 percent on SECC 2011 criteria, plus older RSBY families missing from that list. States may add families, and they pay for the addition. A resident can be entitled to the clinic and outside the hospital scheme. Confusing the two produces bad operating plans, which is the subject of the [execution note](/blog/healthcare-access-execution-bottleneck-review-education-gov-20260330-593).

## What the shift asks of a district

If most secondary care is supposed to sit at the district, the district hospital is a policy object, not a leftover. Indian Public Health Standards, revised in 2022, are the ministry’s benchmark for that public facility and for the centres below it. PM-JAY then pays empanelled public and private hospitals for listed admissions, and the Authority’s page says public hospitals are reimbursed at par with private ones. The policy therefore has a public delivery path and a purchased-care path at the same time. An operator who reads only one path will misread the district.

The 2025 dates in the 2017 policy have passed relative to this update. The programmes built in response are still the ones to read: mandirs for comprehensive primary care, PM-JAY for hospitalization cover, and the infrastructure mission for buildings and labs. How those three look after the target year is the [mid-term note](/blog/healthcare-access-mid-term-trajectory-education-gov-20260330-513).

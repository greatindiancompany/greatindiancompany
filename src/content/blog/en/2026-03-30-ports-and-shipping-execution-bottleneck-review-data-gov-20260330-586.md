# Where Indian port execution actually gets stuck

Indian port execution rarely fails because nobody announced a project. It fails in the hours a ship waits, in the hours a box sits, and in the kilometres between a factory and a gate. The ministry already measures those hours. The bottleneck review is a reading of its own indicators, not a new audit.

## The indicators are the bottleneck list

The Ports Wing says it tracks average turnaround time, output per ship berth day, pre-berthing detention, and port dwell time. Sagar Aankalan uses that family of measures, plus evacuation speed, to rank major ports. Each one points at a different stuck point.

Pre-berthing detention is the roadstead. The ship has arrived and does not have a berth, a tide, a pilot, or a free hook. Turnaround time is the whole visit. Output per ship berth day is whether the gear and the labour on the quay match the ship's size. Dwell time is the land side after the hook: the box or the bulk pile waiting on documents, a rake, or a truck. A port can improve the quay and still fail the trader if dwell does not move. The Indian Ports Association's monthly traffic notes say how much cargo came. They do not, by themselves, say which of these four hours got worse. Read both.

Digitisation is the ministry's proposed solvent for part of the dwell problem. The association lists the Port Community System, the National Logistics Portal Marine (Sagar Setu), and an enterprise business system among its programmes. A portal removes a queue only if customs, the port, the railway, and the transporter are on it. The existence of the portal is not a measured dwell time.

## Evacuation is the bottleneck Sagarmala writes in prose

The Sagarmala page is blunt: adding capacity does not handle more traffic if evacuation is restricted. Hinterland connectivity is mostly road and rail. Pipelines matter for petroleum and gas, not for a container. Cargo-generating centres sit inland, so the lead is long and the delivery window is uneven. The programme's answer is last-mile rail through Indian Port Rail and Ropeway Corporation, plus a larger role for coastal shipping and inland waterways.

That modal shift is itself a bottleneck. The page says inland waterways carry about 2 percent of freight as of 2025, and it treats coastal shipping as the mode that has grown, by 118 percent over a decade on that page's account, without becoming the default. Dedicated coastal berths, bunkering, and the road or rail at the other end are the missing pieces it names. A coastal movement of cement or steel that still needs a long truck haul at both ends has not escaped the bottleneck. It has added a port call.

The shipping division's coastal paragraph makes the same economic claim and then prints a cost comparison that confuses waterways with rail. Ignore that sentence. Use the operational test instead: is there a coastal berth, a return cargo, and a receiver who can take a ship-sized parcel.

## Institutional seams slow the project after the sanction

A major port's board can approve a berth under the 2021 Act and still wait on a state road, a railway window, or a concessionaire's financing. Stressed concessions now go to the Adjudicatory Board in Mumbai, not back to a tariff authority that no longer exists. Referral is a remedy. It is also evidence that the contract and the trade diverged.

Non-major ports add a second seam. About 65 of them handle cargo under state boards. A last-mile road to a state jetty is not in the major-port ranking. Shipbuilding has a parallel version of the same problem: three greenfield clusters are at in-principle stage in the 24 July 2026 reply. In-principle approval is not a dock. Common infrastructure under a Centre-state special purpose vehicle has to be built before a yard's 25 percent brownfield grant, or a 15 to 25 percent vessel subsidy, has a modern place to land.

# What India’s external trade account implies for investment

An export headline is a poor underwriting memo. The number that sits next to a plant, a services contract, or a rupee cost of capital is the external account: how large the goods deficit is, what services and remittances offset, and whether domestic investment is rising with exports or instead of them.

## The closed quarter is April–June, not August

On 1 September 2026 the Reserve Bank released preliminary balance-of-payments data for April–June 2026–27. The current account deficit was US$ 4.2 billion, 0.5 percent of GDP, against US$ 3.4 billion, or 0.4 percent of GDP, a year earlier.

The goods deficit was US$ 86.1 billion, wider than US$ 68.9 billion a year earlier. Goods credits were US$ 132.0 billion and debits US$ 218.0 billion. Net services receipts were US$ 51.6 billion, up from US$ 47.9 billion, on services exports of US$ 106.2 billion. The RBI said services exports rose in computer services, other business services, and transportation.

Personal transfer receipts, mainly remittances, were US$ 42.9 billion, up from US$ 33.2 billion. Net outgo on primary income, mainly investment income, was US$ 10.5 billion, smaller than US$ 13.3 billion a year earlier.

On the financial account, net FDI was an inflow of US$ 6.1 billion, against US$ 5.2 billion. Foreign portfolio investment was a net outflow of US$ 9.6 billion, against a net inflow of US$ 1.6 billion. Foreign exchange reserves fell by US$ 8.1 billion on a BoP basis, against an accretion of US$ 4.5 billion a year earlier.

Read that as a structure, not a slogan. The merchandise deficit was an order of magnitude larger than the current account deficit because services and secondary income financed most of it. A wider goods gap did not, in this quarter, produce a wide current account gap. It also did not produce a reserve build.

## Do not mix this with the customs quick estimate

Customs merchandise exports for a month and BoP goods credits for a quarter are different series. The RBI’s own footnotes have shown customs revisions feeding back into the current account. In the 8 June 2026 release for 2025–26, the Bank revised the October–December current account deficit to US$ 15.5 billion, 1.5 percent of GDP, from US$ 13.2 billion, because merchandise imports in customs data were revised up. An investment note that freezes a quick estimate as “the” external deficit will be wrong when that revision lands.

## What MoSPI said about real exports and capital formation

A [1 September 2026 PIB note](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2305422) reporting Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation estimates put real GDP growth at 7.8 percent in April–June 2026–27. Real exports of goods and services grew 12.0 percent, against 6.0 percent a year earlier. Gross fixed capital formation grew 11.9 percent, against 5.8 percent. Those are volume-style national-accounts rates, not dollar customs growth.

The investment implication that survives contact with both releases: domestic capital formation accelerated in the same quarter that real exports accelerated, while the dollar goods deficit widened and services plus remittances kept the current account deficit near half a percent of GDP. That supports a case for capacity in lines that can sell abroad and at home. It does not support treating merchandise export growth as if the external account had swung into surplus.

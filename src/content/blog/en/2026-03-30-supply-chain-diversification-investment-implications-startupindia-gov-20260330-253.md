# Investment rules for a manufacturing plant in India's trade diversification push

A second factory in India is an investment decision before it is a logistics story. The binding text is DPIIT's Consolidated FDI Policy Circular of 2020, effective 15 October 2020. It is a policy document, not a term sheet. This brief is a reading of that document for operators. It is not a legal opinion.

## Manufacturing capital is on the automatic route

Paragraph 5.2.5.1 says foreign investment in manufacturing is under the automatic route, subject to the rest of the policy. The activity can be the company's own manufacturing, or contract manufacturing in India through a legally tenable contract, on a principal-to-principal or principal-to-agent basis. A manufacturer may sell products made in India by wholesale or retail, including e-commerce, without government approval.

That clause is why a brand can place a contract manufacturer in India and still treat the Indian entity as the manufacturer for FDI purposes, provided the contract is real. Press Note 4 of 2019, recorded by PIB on 18 September 2019, is the amendment that put contract manufacturing into that paragraph. A later DPIIT clarification of 9 December 2019 said the foreign principal is not excused from the FDI conditions: compliance stays with the manufacturing entity.

Food is the exception inside the same section. Paragraph 5.2.5.2 allows 100 percent FDI under the government route for retail trading, including e-commerce, of food products manufactured or produced in India. A food plant that also wants to retail is not in the same bucket as an electronics contract manufacturer.

## The land-border screen is the China-plus-one catch

Paragraph 3.1.1(a) is the sentence investment committees miss. An entity of a country that shares a land border with India, or a case where the beneficial owner of the investment is situated in or is a citizen of any such country, can invest only under the government route. The circular also routes those applications to the administrative ministry or department identified by DPIIT, and it points back to Press Note 3 of 2020.

The note does not say "China" and stop. It covers every country that shares a land border. A fund incorporated in a third country can still be caught if the beneficial owner sits in a land-border country. For a buyer building a second source, the operating question is who owns the Indian vehicle, not which flag is on the slide.

Pakistan has a tighter rule in the same paragraph: investment only on the government route, and not in defence, space, atomic energy, or prohibited sectors. Do not collapse that into the general land-border sentence.

## Incentives are not the same as entry

PLI is an incentive after the investment is eligible, and only in notified sectors. The 2 August 2023 PIB note lists 14 sectors and an outlay of Rs 1.97 lakh crore. It also says anchor plants are expected to create a vendor base, with MSMEs among the selected applicants (176 of 733 at that date). An investor outside those sectors does not inherit the incentive by calling the project diversification. An investor inside a sector still has to meet that ministry's guidelines. DPIIT is not the implementing ministry for every scheme.

Trade access is a third gate. Even a fully approved plant earns a tariff preference only if the relevant agreement is in force and the shipment qualifies for a certificate of origin under DGFT. The India-UAE CEPA, in force from 1 May 2022, is an example of a preference that already has a start date. A concluded text that is still awaiting ratification is not.

## What to underwrite

Underwrite four things separately: automatic-route eligibility for the manufacturing activity, beneficial ownership against the land-border rule, whether the product is in a notified PLI sector, and whether the export lane has an agreement in force.

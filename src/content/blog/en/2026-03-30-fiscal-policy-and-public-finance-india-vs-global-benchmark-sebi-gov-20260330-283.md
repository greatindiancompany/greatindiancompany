A comparison of India’s deficit with “the world” is only as good as the table underneath it. The Union’s 2026-27 fiscal papers do cite a global backdrop. They do not publish a league table of fiscal deficits. This note stays inside what those papers actually print, and says plainly what they leave out.

## The global sentences the Budget uses

The Macro-economic Framework Statement quotes the International Monetary Fund’s World Economic Outlook of January 2026. Global growth is projected at 3.3 percent in calendar 2026 and 3.2 percent in 2027, matching the 3.3 percent outturn the Fund recorded for 2025, and below a long-term average of 3.7 percent. The statement says global headline inflation is expected to moderate at different speeds, and that the balance between sectoral investment and shifting trade policy is precarious. Geopolitical escalation and renewed trade tensions are named as risks to flows.

That is a growth-and-risk backdrop. It is not a statement that India’s fiscal deficit is above or below the G20 median. Anyone who attaches a peer-country deficit to this paragraph is adding a number the document does not contain.

## The India sentences next to that backdrop

The same framework uses the National Statistics Office’s first advance estimates: real GDP growth of 7.4 percent in 2025-26, nominal growth of 8 percent. Services are estimated at 9.1 percent, manufacturing and construction at 7 percent, agriculture at 3.1 percent. Private final consumption is projected to grow 7 percent and to account for 61.5 percent of GDP. Gross fixed capital formation is estimated to rise 7.8 percent, with its share near 30 percent of GDP for three years.

On the fiscal side, the framework says the Union deficit was 54.5 percent of the 2025-26 Budget estimate by December 2025, and that capital expenditure grew 15 percent year on year in April–December 2025. Those are Controller General of Accounts figures reproduced in the Budget paper, for the previous financial year, not for 2026-27.

The medium-term statement then sets the home anchors. The fiscal deficit moves from 6.7 percent of GDP in 2021-22 to 4.4 percent in the 2025-26 revised estimates and 4.3 percent in the 2026-27 Budget estimates. Central government debt is 56.1 percent and then 55.6 percent. The working aim, carried from the previous Budget, is a debt ratio of 50±1 percent by 2030-31.

## Why the statutory benchmarks are not foreign benchmarks

The FRBM Act, as restated in the preface, tells the Centre to limit the fiscal deficit to 3 percent of GDP by 31 March 2021, and to endeavour to keep general government debt within 60 percent of GDP and Central debt within 40 percent by 31 March 2025. Those are India’s legal endeavours. They are not the IMF’s debt threshold and they are not an OECD average.

Two mistakes follow when they are used as a global score.

First, 4.3 percent is the Centre’s deficit, not general government. States have their own deficits, published in the Reserve Bank’s study of state budgets, and the Centre’s transfers sit in both sets of books. Adding 4.3 to a state deficit double-counts what the Centre gave the states and still misses what local bodies do.

Second, 55.6 percent is Central debt on the FRBM definition, including public-account liabilities and NSSF special securities issued for the states. The statement itself shows that stripping those state securities takes the ratio to 55.1 percent in the 2026-27 estimates. A foreign series that counts only marketable central debt will not match 55.6, and the gap is a definition, not a hidden miss.

## What the papers say the global setting changes

The statement withholds rolling deficit targets for the next two years because of that global uncertainty. The deviation note says global stability rests on a fragile trade-policy balance, and that the government will still try to keep debt on a declining path from 2026-27 through 2030-31. One domestic risk it names is high tariffs on exports to the United States, with effects it lists as weaker sentiment, restrained capital inflows, and pressure on the currency. It also says free-trade agreements and trade diversification are a partial offset. None of those sentences quantify a fiscal-deficit gap versus another country.

The honest benchmark available today is internal. Is 4.3 percent below 4.4 percent? Is 55.6 percent below 56.1 percent? Is the revenue deficit, unchanged at 1.5 percent, improving or not? Those are the comparisons the document computes. A cross-country fiscal table would have to come from a later official compilation that uses one debt definition on purpose. This Budget set does not contain it.

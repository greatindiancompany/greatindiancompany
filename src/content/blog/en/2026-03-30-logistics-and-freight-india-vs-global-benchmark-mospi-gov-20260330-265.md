India’s public logistics benchmark is two numbers and a deadline, and they do not all come from the same document. Mixing them is how a serious comparison turns into a slogan.

## The rank the government is willing to cite

On 11 August 2026, answering Lok Sabha Starred Question 327, the Ministry of Commerce and Industry said India’s position in the latest World Bank Logistics Performance Index had improved to 38th, an advance of six places. The [reply](https://sansad.in/getFile/lsapps/loksabhaquestions/annex/188/AS327_tYLfn2.pdf) does not reprint the Bank’s pillar scores. Customs, infrastructure, international shipments, competence, tracking, and timeliness may have moved by different amounts. Anyone who needs the pillar split should open the Bank’s own edition, name that edition, and stop treating “the latest” as a moving label.

The target beside that rank is older. The [cabinet note of 21 September 2022](https://www.pmindia.gov.in/en/news_updates/policy-introduces-unified-logistics-interface-platform-standardization-monitoring-framework-and-skill-development-for-greater-efficiency-in-logistics-services/) says the National Logistics Policy aims to put India among the top 25 countries on that index by 2030. Thirty-eighth, as cited in August 2026, is not the top 25. The gap is the benchmark story. This brief does not forecast whether the remaining places will be closed. The government has not published that path in the reply.

## The cost ratio, and the speech it gets paired with

The same August 2026 reply says a DPIIT–NCAER assessment estimates logistics cost at about 7.9 percent of GDP, and calls that comparable to advanced economies. It does not list those economies or their ratios. “At par” and “comparable” are the reply’s words. They are not a table.

On [17 September 2022](https://www.pmindia.gov.in/en/news_updates/pm-launches-national-logistics-policy/), the Prime Minister told the launch audience that the aim was to bring logistics cost from 13–14 percent to single digits. The 2022 cabinet note turns that into a 2030 comparison with global benchmarks, without repeating 13–14 as a measured baseline.

Do not subtract 7.9 from 14 and call the difference a reform dividend. The August reply never says the NCAER method reproduces the 13–14 percent framing. A lower ratio can be a better system. It can also be a different boundary: what counts as logistics, which year of GDP is the denominator, whether inventory and the empty backhaul are inside the line. Until the assessment’s method is read next to the speech, the honest sentence is that the government now cites about 7.9 percent, and that in 2022 it asked the country to aim for single digits from a 13–14 percent description.

## What a global benchmark will not settle

The index and the ratio are national. A shipper in a landlocked cluster cares about the port-to-inland leg, the rail slot, and the state border process. Those can be poor in a country whose national rank has improved, and tolerable in a country whose rank has not. The Logistics Ease Across Different States exercise is the domestic comparison the August reply says has been institutionalized. It is not a substitute for the World Bank rank, and the reply does not print state scores.

Road-corridor quality is a third benchmark, and it belongs to the roads ministry. [Bharatmala](https://morth.nic.in/en/bharatmala) is the programme page. Kilometres constructed, which the August reply does report for a three-year window, measure network addition. They do not measure India’s place on a global logistics index.

Use the rank to see how the government wants to be compared. Use the 7.9 percent figure as its current macro claim, with the method unread until you open it. Use the top-25-by-2030 line as a 2022 target that August 2026 had not declared met.

[Great Indian Company](https://greatindiancompany.com/) would rather show the gap between 38th and the top 25 than invent a peer table the reply left out.

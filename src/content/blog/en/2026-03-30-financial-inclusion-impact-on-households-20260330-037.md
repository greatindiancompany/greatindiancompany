For a household, financial inclusion in India is a specific product, not a slogan. The [PMJDY scheme page](https://www.pmjdy.gov.in/scheme) says a person without another account can open a basic savings bank deposit account at a bank branch or a business correspondent called a Bank Mitra. There is no minimum balance. The balance earns interest. The account can receive government transfers. A RuPay debit card is part of the offer. Accident cover on that card is ₹1 lakh, and ₹2 lakh for PMJDY accounts opened after 28 August 2018. An overdraft of up to ₹10,000 is available to eligible account holders. The Department of Financial Services describes the same bouquet: a basic account, the RuPay card with ₹2 lakh accident cover, the overdraft subject to eligibility, and Bank Mitras for rural access.

The account is also the on-ramp DFS names for Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana, Pradhan Mantri Suraksha Bima Yojana, and Atal Pension Yojana. Those are separate schemes. Holding a Jan Dhan account makes a person eligible to be enrolled. It does not mean the person already holds the insurance or the pension.

## Who is on the list

As on 25 March 2026, the [country progress report](https://www.pmjdy.gov.in/account-statistics-country.aspx) shows 57.93 crore beneficiaries and 32.29 crore women beneficiaries. Women are the majority of the named stock. RuPay cards issued were 40.27 crore, so the card count sits below the account count. Deposits were ₹3,05,244.71 crore. Dividing those two published totals produces about ₹5,270 per account. A household living on a benefit transfer can hold a much smaller working balance than that average; the average is only a reading of the national stock.

The column “rural/semi-urban centre bank branches” accounts for 45.27 crore of the 57.93 crore. That is the location of the branch centre that holds the account. It is not a census of where the customer sleeps.

## Where the survey still sees a household gap

The Global Findex 2025 finds that women and men in India are equally likely to have an account, and that the gender gap in account ownership is not statistically significant. The same report’s spotlight on inactivity says more men than women own active accounts, and that the gender gap in active accounts, measured among account owners, narrowed from 12 percentage points in 2021 to 7 percentage points in 2024. It links that narrowing to government-to-person payments: the share of women receiving those payments digitally rose from 13 percent in 2021 to 24 percent in 2024.

Among adults who still have no account, the report’s most common reason in India is that a family member already has one. The chapter says this share is higher than the share who cite a lack of money, which it reads as unmet demand for a personal account. Distance and cost are real, and about half of unbanked adults in India cited each of lack of money, cost, and distance. The leading reason is sharing inside the household.

That is the household problem left after mass account opening: a personal account that is actually used, a card that has been issued, and an overdraft that exists only after the bank judges the account eligible. The [risk brief](/blog/financial-inclusion-risk-watch-20260330-077) separates a quiet account from an account the Reserve Bank classifies as inoperative. Great Indian Company treats the household product as its own subject so it is not averaged away into a national index.

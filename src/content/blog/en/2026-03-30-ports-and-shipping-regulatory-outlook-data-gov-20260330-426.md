# Regulatory outlook for major ports, coastal ships, and shipyards

Indian maritime regulation is a set of offices that do not substitute for each other. A tariff dispute at a major port, a seafarer's certificate, a jetty licence at a state port, and a subsidy claim at a shipyard land on different desks. The outlook from September 2026 is that the major-port desk has already changed, and the shipyard desk is now a scheme manual rather than a speech.

## Major ports: the board prices, the board in Mumbai judges

The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 has been in force since 3 November 2021. It repealed the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963. The port authority board regulates, operates, and plans the major port. It sets the tariff for its services and for access to port assets, including land. The rules that make this operational are already listed by the Ports Wing: scale of rates and fees, master plan and application of funds, accounts and audit, corporate social responsibility, sinking fund, and the chairperson's conditions.

The adjudicator is no longer the Tariff Authority for Major Ports. That body ceased on 13 August 2025, when the Adjudicatory Board for Major Ports was constituted. The board's presiding officer, named on the ministry page, is Justice Ashish Jitendra Desai. Its other members are Shri Chirravuri Viswanath and Dr Sanjeev Ranjan. It sits in Mumbai. Its work is disputes with concessionaires and captive berth users, stressed-project reviews, user complaints, and leftover matters from older tariff orders. It does not restore central tariff-setting.

Guidelines around that core are easy to mistake for statutes. The Ports Wing publishes Harit Sagar green-port guidelines (May 2023), Sagar Aankalan performance guidelines, a berthing policy, land policy, a waterfront policy for port-dependent industry, and a standard operating note for the green tug transition. They shape how a port spends and how it is ranked. They are not the Act.

The same page lists the Indian Ports Act, 2025 beside the 2021 Act. Non-major ports, about 200 of them, remain under state maritime boards or state governments on that page's own description. This brief does not summarise the 2025 Act's sections. Anyone licensing a non-major jetty still starts with the state board.

## Ships: the Directorate, not the port

The Directorate General of Shipping is an attached office, created in September 1949 and based in Mumbai. It handles executive matters of merchant shipping. A coastal operator's certificates, manning, and ship safety sit there even when the cargo is only moving from one Indian port to another. The shipping division describes coastal shipping as a mode that can take load off road and rail. The cost-per-tonne sentence on that page mixes waterways, rail, and road in a way that does not parse, so it is not used here as a regulatory fact.

Inland waterway incentives are another instrument. The shipping division says the Jal Vahak scheme, announced on 15 December 2024, is implemented by Inland and Coastal Shipping Limited, a Shipping Corporation subsidiary, and provides a 35 percent incentive to cargo owners who use inland waterways. That incentive is not a major-port tariff.

## Yards: scheme rules, domestic content, and recycling law

Shipyard money now has a manual. The 27 December 2025 Press Information Bureau note says assistance of 15 to 25 percent is milestone-based, that independent valuation is mandatory, and that a shipbreaking credit note equals 40 percent of scrap value for recycling in India. The schemes run to 31 March 2036. The ministry has published the assistance guidelines on its site. Eligibility turns on contract dates, vessel type, and domestic-content conditions in those guidelines. A yard should read the guideline schedules, not a summary, before pricing a bid.

Recycling is regulated separately. The shipping division points to the Ship Recycling Act, 2019 and to Hong Kong Convention compliance at Alang-Sosiya, where it says 115 of 131 allotted plots meet that status. A credit note for scrapping and a compliant plot are linked in policy and still administered as recycling law plus a scheme.

Infrastructure status, notified on 19 September 2025 for large Indian-flagged ships and for smaller ships built in India, is a financing classification used by lenders. It does not replace the Directorate's ship certificates or the port's berthing rules.

# How India describes digital public infrastructure, in its own documents

India's digital public infrastructure is easier to misuse as a slogan than to read as a policy. The official documents describe a specific shift: stop treating each department's software as a closed system, and publish a few shared functions that many agencies and firms can use.

That shift has a date of origin in the programme sense, and a later date when India asked other governments to use the same language.

## The programme, then the phrase

The Press Information Bureau, reporting a government account on 29 July 2026, says the Digital India programme was launched in July 2015. The same release says the government has adopted a digital public infrastructure approach so that digital services can be delivered at population scale through open, interoperable, and scalable platforms.

The release then names the functions in plain words. Foundational digital public infrastructure, it says, has been implemented at population scale for identity (Aadhaar), for payment (UPI), and for data exchange (DigiLocker and API Setu). That sentence is the domestic policy statement. It is more concrete than a general claim about "digitisation."

Around those three functions, the same release lists systems that are shared plumbing rather than a single scheme: Meri Pehchaan as a single sign-on, API Setu as a catalogue of government APIs (8,904 APIs published, in that account), eSign as an online signature service, and Common Service Centres as assisted access where a person does not complete the transaction alone.

## What the G20 agreed to call it

During India's 2023 presidency, the G20 wrote the idea down for a wider group of governments. The New Delhi Leaders' Declaration of 9 September 2023 calls digital public infrastructure an evolving concept: a set of shared digital systems, built and used by both the public and private sectors, based on secure and resilient infrastructure, that can be built on open standards and specifications as well as open-source software, and that can enable delivery of services at societal scale.

The declaration welcomes a voluntary, suggested G20 Framework for Systems of Digital Public Infrastructure. The Digital Economy Ministers' framework, dated 19 August 2023, is explicit that members approach digital transformation differently, and that the concept can be tailored and even named differently. "Public," in that text, can mean public benefit and access, subject to governance and oversight by public authorities. It does not mean that every component must be a government department.

The ministers' annex names three basic functions:

- Identification: people and businesses can securely verify identity, with complementary trust services such as electronic signatures and verifiable credentials.
- Payments: easy and instant transfer of money between people, businesses, and governments.
- Data sharing with consent where that applies: personal data can move across public and private sectors, with safeguards under the applicable data-governance framework.

India's stack maps onto that list without much translation. Aadhaar is the identity system. UPI is the payment system. DigiLocker, API Setu, and the Account Aggregator framework are different answers to data sharing. They are not the same answer. DigiLocker holds or fetches documents issued by an authority. An Account Aggregator, under the Reserve Bank's directions, retrieves financial information and must not keep it.

## What "open" means in the Indian systems

Open does not mean unregulated, and it does not mean a government marketplace.

The Account Aggregator design is the clearest legal statement. The Reserve Bank of India (Non-Banking Financial Companies – Account Aggregator) Directions, 2025, issued on 28 November 2025, define the business as retrieving or collecting specified financial information and presenting it to the customer or to a financial information user. The customer's financial information is not the property of the Account Aggregator and must not be used in any other manner. The directions say an Account Aggregator shall provide services only on explicit consent, shall not support transactions, and shall not let financial information reside with it. Consent has to travel in a standardised artefact that states purpose, recipients, and expiry.

The commerce version of the same idea is institutional rather than a payment rule. On 4 August 2026 the Ministry of Commerce and Industry told the Lok Sabha that the Open Network for Digital Commerce is an open, interoperable network that lets buyers and sellers transact across applications without being tied to one application. It is a section 8 company under the Companies Act, 2013. The answer says it is not a platform, an application, or a marketplace. Seller-side and buyer-side applications implement a common specification. The network operator is not the shop.

Health uses yet another pattern. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission site describes a mission launched in September 2021 to build an integrated, citizen-centric digital health ecosystem. The health ministry's 24 July 2026 reply says privacy by design is a guiding principle and that there is no centralised repository of health data. Exchange happens after the patient's consent. Applications are validated in a sandbox and go through a web application security audit before they join.

Education is federated on purpose. DIKSHA, the National Council of Educational Research and Training's Digital Infrastructure for Knowledge Sharing, says each state and Union Territory uses the platform in its own way. The national layer supplies building blocks, including those associated with the National Digital Education Architecture launched on 29 July 2021. The state decides the programme.

## What the shift replaced

The older pattern was a department portal: one scheme, one login, one database, one vendor. The releases above still contain portals. UMANG is a mobile front door to many services. myScheme is a discovery site for schemes. eOffice is a workplace system for files. Those are useful, and the Lok Sabha has published operating counts for them. They are not the policy shift by themselves.

The shift is the part a second organisation can call. A bank can send a UPI payment without rebuilding identity. A state board can issue a document into DigiLocker without building a national wallet. A regulated lender can ask an Account Aggregator for a statement the customer has consented to, instead of collecting PDFs. A seller app can be discovered by a buyer app it does not own, if both speak ONDC's specification.

## What the policy does not settle

The G20 text is voluntary. It does not require India, or any other member, to open every data set. India's own data-protection statute is on a phased clock, which is a different brief from this one. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified in November 2025. Most of the operative duties were given a later commencement date. A shared rail and a complete privacy code are not the same project, even when the same ministry speaks for both.

The policy also does not promise that every resident is on every rail. UIDAI's saturation report as on 30 June 2026 puts live Aadhaar penetration at 94.59 percent nationally, and much lower in several north-eastern states. A design that assumes universal identity will fail in the places the identity report itself flags.

Read the policy as three public functions plus a rule about who may hold the data. Identity is a number and an authentication service. Payments are a message between banks. Data moves when a person agrees, and the intermediary that carries the agreement is often forbidden to keep the payload. That is the shift. The apps on top of it are a later, private choice.

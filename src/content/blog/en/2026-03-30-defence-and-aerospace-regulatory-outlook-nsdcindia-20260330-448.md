# A defence firm needs a licence, a control-list check, and an export permission

## Gate one: permission to manufacture

Press Note 4 of 2020 defines the defence industry that sits under the foreign-investment rule as industry needing a licence under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951, plus manufacture of small arms and ammunition under the Arms Act, 1959. DPIIT considers licence applications in consultation with the Ministry of Defence and the Ministry of External Affairs.

A PIB note of 20 November 2025 says 788 industrial licences have been issued to 462 companies. That is a stock of permissions, not a statement that every defence supplier needs a fresh licence, and not a statement that a licence is an order. The same note says the Defence Procurement Manual 2025, effective 1 November 2025, removed the old no-objection certificate from the erstwhile Ordnance Factory Board. Dropping one certificate is a procedural change. It does not retire the industrial-licence statute.

Foreign ownership, if any, is a second question inside this gate. New licensees can take foreign investment up to 74 percent on the automatic route. The rest of Press Note 4, including Home Ministry security clearance and the national-security review, still applies.

## Gate two: whether the item is on SCOMET

The Directorate General of Foreign Trade publishes the SCOMET list: Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies. It is Appendix 3 to the export schedule. The DGFT SCOMET page lists an updated list as on 23 September 2025. A firm checking status on a later day should take the file the page currently marks as updated, because the page keeps older lists beside it.

SCOMET covers dual-use items, munitions, and nuclear-related items, including software and technology. Being absent from a positive indigenisation list does not mean an item is absent from SCOMET. The lists answer different questions. One is about who should make a part for an Indian buyer. The other is about whether a cross-border movement is controlled.

## Gate three: defence export authorisation

The Department of Defence Production reported that it issued 1,762 export authorisations in FY 2024-25, against 1,507 the year before. Those authorisations are how the Department counts permission to export defence items. They are not DGFT's entire SCOMET workload, and they are not the industrial licences counted in the November note.

DGFT's handbook on India's strategic trade control system describes Category 6, the munitions list, as licensed through the Department of Defence Production's procedure. A reader should confirm the current handbook and the current standard operating procedure before filing, rather than treat a 2017 delegation note as the only text that matters. The practical point is stable: munitions-list exports are a Defence Production permission, while the control list itself is published by DGFT.

## How the gates stack

A company can hold an industrial licence and still need an authorisation for a shipment. It can win a SRIJAN development effort and still be unable to export the result. It can receive foreign investment within the cap and still fail a control-list check. Space activities add a further authorisation from IN-SPACe, which is not any of these three.

The November 2025 note also describes revenue procurement under the Defence Procurement Manual 2025, covering goods and services of about Rs 1 lakh crore, with lower liquidated damages of 0.1 percent per week on indigenisation projects and guaranteed orders for indigenous products for up to five years. That manual changes how a domestic revenue buyer contracts. It is not an export licence.

Great Indian Company lists the gates in the order a firm actually meets them: make, classify, then export. The credible path is the notice for the gate in front of you.

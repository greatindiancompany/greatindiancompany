India's education policy does contain an international comparison. It is easy to misuse, because it is old, and because fresher domestic numbers are now available for a different question. This brief separates the comparison the policy actually printed from the benchmarks a reader can update without inventing a ranking.

## The comparison inside the policy

Chapter 16 of the [National Education Policy 2020](https://www.education.gov.in/nep/nep2020_final_eng.pdf) quotes the 12th Five Year Plan, covering 2012–2017. That plan estimated that less than 5% of the Indian workforce aged 19–24 had received formal vocational education, against 52% in the United States, 75% in Germany, and 96% in South Korea. The policy uses those figures to argue for speed. It does not present them as a survey conducted for the policy year, and it does not update them.

Treat that paragraph as a statement of why the 2020 policy wanted vocational education pulled into mainstream schools and colleges. Do not put "India 5%, Germany 75%" on a 2026 slide as if it were a current census. No official series cited here has republished those four percentages for a recent year. Inventing a fresh league table would be worse than keeping the dated one and labelling it.

The policy ties its own vocational target to a global commitment India had already adopted. Sustainable Development Goal 4, which the introduction recalls from the 2030 Agenda, seeks inclusive and equitable quality education and lifelong learning. Paragraph 16.5 says the aim that at least 50% of learners in school and higher education have vocational exposure by 2025 is aligned with SDG 4.4. That is a domestic target placed next to a global goal. It is not a rank.

## Benchmarks you can actually refresh

Three domestic benchmarks in the same policy can be set beside later official numbers, if you keep the definitions straight.

First, higher-education gross enrolment. The policy wants 50% by 2035, including vocational education, from 26.3% in 2018. AISHE 2023-24, released on 8 July 2026, reports a gross enrolment ratio of 30 for ages 18–23 on the 2011 population projection. The policy's "including vocational education" clause and the survey's age-group definition are not guaranteed to be identical. The honest sentence is that the latest official higher-education ratio is 30, and the policy aim is 50 by 2035. The gap is large either way. Calling 30 "halfway to the OECD" would be a comparison this page does not have a source for.

Second, school completion through the secondary stage. The policy, using the data then available, said the gross enrolment ratio was 90.9% in Grades 6–8, 79.3% in Grades 9–10, and 56.5% in Grades 11–12, and it set a goal of 100% gross enrolment from preschool to secondary by 2030. UDISE+ 2024-25, on the new stage structure, reports middle-stage gross enrolment of 90.3% and secondary-stage gross enrolment of 68.5%. Secondary-stage retention in that release is 47.2%. Retention and gross enrolment are different indicators. A country can raise the enrolment ratio and still lose a large share of a cohort before the stage is finished. That domestic gap is a sharper benchmark, for an operator or a state team, than a stale comparison with South Korea.

Third, public spending. The policy says combined government expenditure on education was around 4.43% of GDP in the Analysis of Budgeted Expenditure 2017-18, against a long-standing aim of 6%. This brief does not supply a newer ratio. Until a later budget analysis from an official source is in hand, the live benchmark is the aim, and the sourced level is the 2017-18 one. Do not fill the blank with an unsourced "current" percentage.

## What a serious comparison looks like

A usable comparison names the indicator, the age or grade, the year, and the publisher. "Vocationally trained youth aged 15–29" from the skill ministry, rising from 7.1% in 2017-18 to 26.1% in 2023-24, can sit beside the policy's exposure target only with a warning that the populations differ: workforce youth with vocational training, versus learners inside school and college who have had vocational exposure. The first can rise while the second is still unmet, or the reverse.

Foundational learning is the other place rankings go wrong. The policy and NIPUN Bharat define a national mission for reading and numeracy by the end of Grade 3. They do not, in the texts used here, publish a current international test score for India. The National Assessment Centre, PARAKH, is the policy's proposed standard-setter for school boards and for the National Achievement Survey. A PARAKH or NAS release is the right domestic benchmark for learning. A borrowed PISA rank is not a substitute if you have not opened that release.

If you need one sentence for a board: India's own policy still judges the country against a 50% higher-education enrolment aim by 2035, a universal school-enrolment aim by 2030, and a 6% of GDP public-spending aim. The latest official higher-education ratio is 30. The latest official secondary-stage retention is 47.2%. Those two domestic facts do more work than the 12th Plan's international vocational percentages, which remain in the policy as history.

The August 2026 pause is a domestic vote taken against an external shock. The Monetary Policy Committee kept the repo rate at 5.25 per cent and the stance neutral. The resolution’s global paragraph is short and concrete: conflict in West Asia resumed in July after a ceasefire, several central banks have raised rates while others are watching, the US dollar has strengthened, and oil, inflation expectations and public finances are the downside risks it names.

## What the committee says the world is doing

The 5 August resolution describes 2026 so far as a year of sharp market swings, persistent inflation concerns and shifting policy expectations. Persistent inflation has prompted several central banks to raise rates. The dollar’s rise is tied, in that paragraph, to elevated yields, a hawkish Federal Reserve tone, and a US economy the resolution associates with productivity gains from artificial intelligence. Equity markets have been volatile as investors reprice AI-related stocks.

The June resolution, six weeks earlier, had already said major advanced-economy central banks were likely to pivot toward tightening, and that sovereign yields had hardened on fiscal concerns. The August text is the update: the pivot is no longer only a forecast. Some central banks have moved. The Reserve Bank has not. India’s repo rate is on hold after a 125 basis point easing cycle from February 2025 to June 2026, while the resolution’s picture of the rest of the world is one of vigilance and, in places, higher rates.

Deputy Governor Poonam Gupta’s 22 May 2026 speech, published in the Bulletin, gives the inflation contrast that makes the divergence intelligible. On the World Economic Outlook figures in that speech, India’s headline inflation in 2025 was 2.2 per cent, against 5.2 per cent for emerging-market and developing economies and 4.1 per cent for the world. That low 2025 print is the backdrop to the easing cycle. The August meeting is about what followed: headline inflation back above 4 per cent, and a projection of 5.0 per cent for 2026-27.

## Oil is the channel that rewrites the forecast

The Indian basket of crude averaged US$106.23 a barrel in May 2026 and US$83.22 in June. Hostilities resumed in early July. By 31 July, Brent was 34.5 per cent above its end-June level and the Indian basket was 32.5 per cent higher. The August Bulletin then records US$89.7 a barrel for the Indian basket in August up to the 20th. In the minutes, Gupta says the April projection assumed US$85, the June projection US$95, and the August projection about US$90. Each MPC forecast in this cycle has been an oil forecast as much as a demand forecast.

The domestic pass-through has been partial and then frozen at the pump. Retail petrol and diesel were raised 7.4 per cent and 8.4 per cent in May, which the June resolution said would add about 36 basis points directly to headline CPI. The August Bulletin says those metro retail prices were unchanged in July and through 21 August. Fuel inflation in the CPI still edged up, from 4.5 per cent in June to 4.6 per cent in July. Wholesale fuel and power inflation was 20.0 per cent in July, down from 30.8 per cent in May, and still a cost shock for any firm that buys energy off the wholesale index rather than the retail pump.

## The external accounts the Governor put next to the rate

The Governor’s 5 August statement keeps the external balance in the same speech as the rate decision. The current account deficit was 0.6 per cent of GDP in 2025-26, US$25.4 billion, the same ratio as the year before. April–May 2026 recorded a current-account surplus of US$2.8 billion, led by services and remittances. The merchandise trade deficit in the first quarter of 2026-27 widened to US$86.6 billion from US$68.7 billion a year earlier, on crude oil, electronic goods and gold. Merchandise exports in that quarter were US$129.3 billion, up 15.9 per cent, and imports US$216.2 billion, up 19.9 per cent.

The IMF’s July 2026 World Economic Outlook, as cited in that statement, expects world goods and services trade volume to grow 3.5 per cent in 2026, down from 5.0 per cent in 2025. Services exports and remittances are the offsets the statement relies on. The services surplus was US$34.3 billion in April–May 2026, against US$31.7 billion a year earlier. Net transfers, mainly remittances, were US$29.6 billion in those two months, against US$20.0 billion.

Financing has been stronger than the trade gap alone would suggest. Gross foreign direct investment was US$30.7 billion in April–June 2026, against US$26.7 billion a year earlier. Net FDI was US$7.9 billion in the Governor’s figures for that quarter, against US$4.8 billion. Foreign portfolio investment turned to a net inflow of US$7.1 billion in June–July, led by debt, after outflows in April–May. Counted from April through July, portfolio flows were still a net outflow of US$4.3 billion, with equity outflows of US$11.6 billion and debt inflows of US$7.3 billion. The August Bulletin’s later window, through 20 August, puts the year’s portfolio outflow at US$2.3 billion. The sign depends on the window. The statement does not claim a settled inflow.

Reserves on 31 July were US$692.9 billion. The statement says that stock covered more than 10 months of imports and 90.8 per cent of external debt. The August Bulletin’s later reading, as on 14 August, is 11.0 months of goods imports and, separately, 8.7 months of goods and services. The exchange-rate policy stated alongside those numbers is unchanged: the rate is market-determined, and the Reserve Bank acts against excessive volatility and disorderly moves.

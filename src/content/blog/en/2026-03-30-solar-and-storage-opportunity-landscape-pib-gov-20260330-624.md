# Four Solar Buyers and the Contract Each One Signs

"Solar opportunity in India" is four contracts. The buyer, the payment, and the ministry instrument are different in each. A firm that uses one pitch for all four will mis-state who pays.

## Households, on a capped assistance formula

PIB release 2010133 of 29 February 2024 approves PM Surya Ghar for one crore households, with an outlay of Rs 75,021 crore and free electricity up to 300 units a month. The buyer is the household. Central financial assistance is 60 percent of system cost for 2 kW systems and 40 percent of the additional cost from 2 to 3 kW, capped at 3 kW. At the benchmark prices in that note, assistance was Rs 30,000, Rs 60,000, and Rs 78,000 for 1 kW, 2 kW, and 3 kW or higher. The household applies on the national portal, picks a vendor, and can sell surplus to the distribution company. The cabinet note said a 3 kW system would generate more than 300 units a month on average.

The commercial opportunity is installation and service under that cap, plus vendor rating and domestic-content checks described in the later progress note. It is not a 25-year power purchase agreement.

## Farmers, under three components

PIB release 2042069 of 6 August 2024 describes PM-KUSUM, launched in March 2019 and scaled up in January 2024. Component A lets farmers or their organisations, or a developer on their land, build up to 2 MW. The distribution company buys power at a pre-fixed levelised tariff and may pass on a central incentive of Rs 0.40 per unit or Rs 6.6 lakh per MW, whichever is less, for five years. Component B is a standalone pump, with 30 percent central assistance, or 50 percent in the northeast, hills, and islands. Component C solarises grid-connected pumps or whole agricultural feeders, at the same assistance rates.

The buyer is a farmer, a farmer group, or a distribution company, depending on the component. As on 30 June 2024 the reply counted 4,11,222 farmers benefited nationally. That is a reach figure, not a tender pipeline.

## Distribution companies, through SECI

SECI's what-we-do page describes the utility contract. SECI awards developers by reverse bid, signs a power purchase agreement with the developer, and a power sale agreement with the distribution company. As of 1 May 2025 it had awarded over 73.8 GW, including 45.9 GW of solar, 16.4 GW of wind, and 11.5 GW of hybrid. Products include plain renewable energy, round-the-clock supply, firm and dispatchable renewable energy, and utility-scale batteries.

The opportunity for a developer is a bid SECI will accept and a sale agreement a distribution company will sign. The opportunity for a storage firm is a tender that asks for energy at a defined time, which SECI says it runs. Payment security is part of SECI's stated role as intermediary. It is not the same as the household subsidy.

## Factories, paid on modules sold

MNRE's PLI page is a manufacturer contract, not a power contract. The outlay is Rs 24,000 crore. Three bidders received 8,737 MW of fully integrated awards under tranche I through IREDA. Eleven bidders received 39,600 MW of fully or partially integrated awards under tranche II through SECI in April 2023. Incentive runs for five years after commissioning, on manufacture and sale, with higher performance favoured. The buyer of the module may be a developer, a rooftop vendor, or an exporter. The government counterparty on the incentive is MNRE through IREDA or SECI, and only after the factory is up and selling.

List eligibility sits beside this contract. A module that cannot go onto a government scheme, open-access plant, or net-metered roof is a smaller market even if the factory holds a PLI award. That gate is the ALMM order, described on MNRE's list page.

## Choose the contract before the capacity slide

A credible landscape slide has four rows: household assistance, farm component, SECI power sale, and post-commissioning module incentive. Storage appears inside the SECI product list and inside the separate viability-gap scheme, not as a fifth copy of rooftop solar.

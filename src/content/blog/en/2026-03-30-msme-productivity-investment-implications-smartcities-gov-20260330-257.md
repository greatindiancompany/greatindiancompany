# MSME credit, ceilings, and what investors can underwrite

An investment case for an Indian micro, small, or medium enterprise now has three official constraints: how large the firm may become and remain classified, whether a bank loan can be guaranteed, and whether receivables can be financed. Each constraint is written down. None of them is a forecast of returns.

## Headroom inside the definition

Since 1 April 2025, S.O. 1364(E) allows investment in plant and machinery or equipment up to ₹2.5 crore for micro, ₹25 crore for small, and ₹125 crore for medium, with turnover ceilings of ₹10 crore, ₹100 crore, and ₹500 crore. The Press Information Bureau tied that widening to scale, technology upgradation, and access to capital.

For an investor, the underwriting point is the composite test. Either ceiling can push the firm into the next category. A term loan that funds equipment can change classification even when revenue is unchanged. Models that assume the firm "stays micro" through a capacity project need the post-project investment number, not the pre-project one.

The 14 May 2026 PIB note records that non-tax benefits of the earlier category can continue for three years after an upward move. Build that as a transition, with an end date, and keep tax advice with the company's tax counsel.

## Guarantee is an MSE instrument

CGTMSE is a trust of the Ministry of MSME and SIDBI. It guarantees credit that member lending institutions extend to micro and small enterprises without collateral or third-party guarantee. The Development Commissioner's scheme page states that, with effect from 1 April 2025, the scheme facilitates guarantees for credit support up to ₹10 crore to those enterprises. CGTMSE's circular of 18 March 2025 applies the higher ceiling to guarantees approved on or after 1 April 2025, including enhancement of working capital on covered accounts, for banks and select financial institutions. The standard annual guarantee fee on the new upper slabs is 1.10 percent above ₹5 crore up to ₹8 crore, and 1.20 percent above ₹8 crore up to ₹10 crore.

The Ministry's 26 June 2026 note says 29.03 lakh guarantees amounting to ₹3.77 lakh crore were approved between 1 January and 30 November 2025. That is an approval stock for those eleven months. It is not a disbursement figure, and it is not a claim about default.

Medium enterprises sit inside the MSME definition and outside this guarantee. A deal that depends on CGTMSE after the firm crosses the small-enterprise ceiling is a different credit.

The same May 2026 note describes the Self-Reliant India Fund as ₹10,000 crore from the government and ₹40,000 crore to be raised through private equity and venture capital, in a mother-fund and daughter-fund structure managed by NSIC Venture Capital Fund Limited. Budget 2026-27, as that note records it, adds ₹2,000 crore to the fund and names a ₹10,000 crore SME Growth Fund. Public notes differ on how many firms the SRI Fund had financed by late 2025 and by May 2026, and on the rupee amount. Until those statements are reconciled on one SIDBI or Ministry table, underwrite the fund's design, not a blended total.

## Receivables and the bank file

The Reserve Bank's Master Direction on lending to the MSME sector, updated as on 9 February 2026, points banks to the priority-sector directions of 24 March 2025. All bank loans to MSMEs qualify as priority sector. For that classification, banks follow the category on the Udyam Registration Certificate or the Udyam Assist Certificate. Factoring of MSME receivables on the Trade Receivables Discounting System is eligible for priority sector. The 14 May 2026 PIB note says Budget 2026-27 mandates TReDS for central public sector enterprises.

A guarantee raises a bank's willingness to lend without collateral. It does not collect the invoice. Productivity that is locked in receivables still needs the TReDS path or the payment timelines in the MSMED Act.

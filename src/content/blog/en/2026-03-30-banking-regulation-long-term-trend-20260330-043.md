# How India's bank capital rule stacked buffers on a 9 percent floor

The long pattern in Indian commercial-bank capital is not a new percentage every year. It is a minimum total-capital ratio of 9 percent of risk-weighted assets, with common-equity buffers stacked on top, now written as one directions document.

## One floor, restated rather than reset

On 1 April 2025 the Reserve Bank was still publishing a [Master Circular on Basel III Capital Regulations](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasterCirculars.aspx?Id=12815&Mode=0) for scheduled commercial banks other than small finance banks, payments banks, and regional rural banks. That circular consolidated prudential guidelines issued up to that date.

On 28 November 2025 the [Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks - Prudential Norms on Capital Adequacy) Directions, 2025](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=13159&Mode=0) took over the subject for commercial banks as the Directions define them: banking companies other than small finance banks, payment banks, and local area banks, plus corresponding new banks and the State Bank of India. The Directions, updated as on 1 July 2026, say that existing directions, instructions, and guidelines on prudential capital adequacy for commercial banks stand repealed, as communicated by the circular of 28 November 2025. Actions already taken under the repealed instructions continue to be governed by those instructions.

The minimums in the new Directions are the familiar stack. Common Equity Tier 1 of at least 5.5 percent of risk-weighted assets. Tier 1 of at least 7 percent. Total capital of at least 9 percent, before buffers. A capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percent, held as common equity, so common equity plus the buffer is 8 percent and total capital plus the buffer is 11.5 percent.

The trend to read is continuity of the floor, and a change in the legal form: an annual master circular gave way to directions issued under section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, with a repeal-and-saving clause.

## Buffers restrict distributions before they force a breach of the minimum

The conservation buffer is designed to be drawn in stress and rebuilt by limiting dividends, share buybacks, and other distributions when common equity sits inside the buffer range. The Directions set that conservation as a percentage of earnings, rising as the common-equity ratio falls toward the 5.5 percent minimum. The buffer binds at both solo and consolidated level, and the tighter of the two ratios governs.

Domestic systemically important banks carry a further common-equity surcharge. The framework, which the Directions trace to the Reserve Bank's press release of 22 July 2014, scores size, interconnectedness, substitutability, and complexity, places a bank in a bucket, and requires extra common equity. The table in the Directions runs from 0.20 percent of risk-weighted assets in bucket 1 to 1.00 percent in bucket 5, which the table marks empty. Bucket 4 is 0.80 percent, bucket 3 is 0.60 percent, and bucket 2 is 0.40 percent. The surcharge applies at solo and consolidated level. It works as an extension of the conservation buffer. The Directions' own example: a bucket-1 bank needs common equity of 8.2 percent of risk-weighted assets if it wants to avoid the distribution limits of the buffer regime. The Reserve Bank reviews the assessment method; the Directions point to a review announced on 28 December 2023. This brief does not name the banks currently in each bucket, because that list is a separate annual disclosure.

A countercyclical buffer can be switched on by the Reserve Bank. The Directions treat it as a requirement "as may be mandated," not as a standing add-on in Table 1. Foreign banks that are globally systemic, and that operate in India as branches, hold an additional common-equity surcharge in India scaled to their India risk-weighted assets relative to the global group's.

## What stayed outside this consolidation

Small finance banks, payment banks, and local area banks are not in these Directions. Leverage ratio, at 4 percent for a domestic systemically important bank and 3.5 percent for other banks covered here, sits beside the risk-weighted stack as a non-risk-based floor. Pillar 2 still lets the Reserve Bank demand more capital bank by bank. The long trend is a stable 9 percent total-capital minimum, equity buffers that bite through distribution limits, and a 2025 rewrite that put that stack in one directions text.

# State-wise pattern of India's major ports and coastal yards

India's coastline is not one port system. The Centre administers the operating major ports. State maritime boards administer non-major ports. Shipyards sit in a third pattern, mostly private, with public yards scattered through the same states. A state ranking by "maritime potential" that ignores that split will mis-assign both cargo and permits.

## The twelve operating major ports, by coast

The Ports Wing says 14 major ports exist and 12 are operational. It names the twelve. The Indian Ports Association's member list is the same set. The two that are not operating are not named on that page, so this brief does not guess them.

On the west coast, Gujarat has Deendayal Port Authority, the Kandla system. Maharashtra has two Centre-run ports, Mumbai Port Authority and Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority, which serve overlapping hinterlands and different cargo mixes. Goa has Mormugao. Karnataka has New Mangalore. Kerala has Cochin.

On the east and south-east coast, Tamil Nadu has three: V.O. Chidambaranar at Thoothukudi, Chennai, and Kamarajar at Ennore. Kamarajar is listed by the association as Kamarajar Port Limited, a company, while the others on this list are port authorities. Andhra Pradesh has Visakhapatnam. Odisha has Paradip. West Bengal has Syama Prasad Mookerjee Port, the Kolkata system. The ministry page spells the Thoothukudi port "Chidambarnar"; the association uses "Chidambaranar." The port is the same.

## What the state actually controls

Non-major ports are under the state maritime board or the state government. The Ports Wing counts about 200 of them and says around 65 handle cargo. The rest are port limits used by fishing boats and small ferries. Gujarat, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Tamil Nadu are the states where a large non-major estate sits beside a major port. A factory, a captive jetty, or a coastal berth in those states may never appear in a major-port statistic.

Sagarmala's industrial language follows the same map without ranking states. The programme page points to coastal economic units where land sits near a deep-draught port, and it names Kandla, Paradip, Ennore, and Visakhapatnam as places to look first. That is a land-and-depth screen, not a state scoreboard.

## Where ships are built and broken

The shipping division counts 61 shipyards in the country: eight under the central public sector, two under state governments, and 51 private. It does not, on that page, give a state-wise yard census. The location signal that is dated is the 24 July 2026 reply: in-principle approval for greenfield shipbuilding clusters in Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu. Those three states already host major ports. A cluster approval is not a statement that the other coastal states lack yards.

Ship recycling is concentrated even more tightly. The shipping division says nearly 98 percent of ship recycling in India happens at Alang-Sosiya in Gujarat, on about 10 kilometres of seafront on the Gulf of Cambay, with 153 plots and a stated capacity of 4.5 million metric tonnes a year. It says 131 plots are allotted and 115 have Hong Kong Convention compliance. Any state comparison of "maritime jobs" that omits Alang is describing ports and forgetting the end of a ship's life.

Inland and coastal waterways cut across this state list. The Sagarmala page treats coastal shipping and inland waterways as one pillar, and the Shipping Corporation's inland subsidiary runs services on national waterways that are not the same asset as a state maritime board's jetty. A state government can promote a non-major port and still have little say over a national waterway.

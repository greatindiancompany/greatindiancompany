# How Modified UDAN changes the rules on a regional route

Modified UDAN is a change in the contract between the ministry, the state, and the airline. The public text of that change is the comparison Minister Ram Mohan Naidu presented on 16 July 2026, published the same day by the Press Information Bureau, plus the Lok Sabha statement of 6 August that fixes the outlay and the ten-year target.

The scheme was launched on 4 July 2026 at Jodhpur. It runs from 2026-27 to 2035-36. The outlay is ₹28,840 crore. The target named in the House is 120 new destinations and 4 crore passengers over ten years.

## Who may bid

Under the earlier scheme an airline could bid without an air operator permit, which the minister described as a way to draw new entrants. He also said some of those airlines bid networks they could not fly. Modified UDAN requires an air operator permit before a bid. Award is meant to follow the business plan and the aircraft the airline actually has. If the airline misses the start window, the route is re-bid and the original bidder is barred.

That single rule changes who a state should talk to. A slide deck from a company still seeking a permit is not a bid the new scheme will score.

## How long the support lasts

Viability-gap funding was limited to three years. It can now run five, and it tapers: year three at 75 percent of the awarded amount, year four at 50 percent, year five at 25 percent. The 23 July Lok Sabha reply uses the same idea in plainer words: a longer support period, tapered, so that a route is supposed to stand without permanent subsidy, while fares stay capped on the seats that the support covers.

The cap itself moves. Earlier, reduced fares applied to 50 percent of seats and to no more than 40 seats on a flight. On aircraft with more than 80 seats, the 40-seat ceiling is removed. The 50 percent rule stays.

## What “served” means

The old test treated an aerodrome as served once it had more than seven actual departures a week in the last DGCA schedule, or more than 14 in priority areas. The new test looks at the year before the scheme is published and asks for three things together: more than 14 scheduled departures a week on average, connectivity to more than two city pairs, and more than 40,000 passengers in that year. In priority areas the departure test rises to more than 21 a week. The city-pair and passenger tests stay.

A strip with a few weekly flights and almost no passengers can still be unserved under the new definition. A strip that already has a modest network and 40,000 passengers is served, and it is not the scheme’s target.

## Who chooses the aerodrome

Previously, airlines picked routes from a pool and the chosen aerodromes were then developed, inside a fixed ₹5,500 crore support for aerodromes and heliports since 2017. The new sequence puts development first. Aerodromes are prioritised after a challenge, and airlines then choose among aerodromes that are ready to bid. States can put routes on a priority list that the ministry includes in the award.

The 6 August statement names the challenge tests: encumbrance-free land, infrastructure readiness, technical and operational feasibility, traffic potential, statutory clearances, and the state’s own support.

## What the Centre will buy itself

There is a small fleet line inside the scheme, separate from commercial airline orders. The Cabinet has approved two HAL Dhruv helicopters for regional and difficult-terrain work and two HAL Dornier aircraft. The 16 July note assigns the helicopters to Pawan Hans and the Dorniers to Alliance Air. The point of the line, as the 6 August reply puts it, is the shortage of small aircraft and helicopters on regional routes, and a domestic aerospace purchase under the Atmanirbhar Bharat heading.

Operation-and-maintenance support is also new. For three years, manpower costs at RCS aerodromes can be supported at up to ₹3.06 crore a year per airport, and up to ₹0.90 crore for a heliport or water aerodrome. Aerodrome development money in the ten-year envelope is about ₹12,159 crore for 100 airports, described as inflation-adjusted and aligned at ₹100 crore per airport, and ₹3,661 crore for 200 helipads at ₹15 crore each in hilly, north-eastern, and aspirational districts.

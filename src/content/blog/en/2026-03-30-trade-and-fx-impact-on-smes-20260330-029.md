# What the export numbers mean for India's smaller exporters

A national export surge is a weak brief for a smaller firm. The firm needs three separate facts: how large the MSME-related slice of merchandise exports is, which commodity lines are actually moving, and which public instruments pay for finance versus paperwork. Those facts come from a December 2025 PIB note, the August 2026 DGCIS quick estimates, and the RBI's software-export survey. They do not describe the same businesses.

## The share is a product basket, not a headcount of firms

On 11 December 2025 the Press Information Bureau reported a written reply by the Minister of State for MSME. The contribution of MSME-related products to India's merchandise exports, in US dollar terms, rose from **45.74 per cent in 2023-24 to 48.55 per cent in 2024-25**.

That sentence is about specified products associated with the MSME sector. It is not a census of how many micro, small, and medium firms filed a shipping bill, and it is not a profit figure. A large firm shipping a product that sits in an MSME-related basket can be inside the share. A small software exporter is largely outside it, because the share is merchandise.

The same reply is the latest PIB statement of that share used here. It stops at 2024-25. It should not be stretched forward to the 2026-27 customs prints.

## The Export Promotion Mission splits money from market-readiness

The same PIB note records that the government approved the Export Promotion Mission on 12 November 2025. The outlay is **Rs 25,060 crore for 2025-26 through 2030-31**. Support runs through two windows.

**Niryat Protsahan** is trade-finance facilitation for MSME exporters. **Niryat Disha** is the non-financial window: export quality and compliance, international branding, trade fairs, export warehousing and logistics, inland transport reimbursement, and trade intelligence and capacity building.

Alongside that, the International Cooperation Scheme pays for exhibition visits, buyer-seller meets, and, for first-time micro and small exporters, registration-cum-membership certification with an export promotion council, export insurance premium, and testing and quality certification. Over 2020-21 to 2024-25, **1,361 MSMEs** benefited. That is a count of scheme users over five years. It is a small number next to the merchandise-export economy, which is the useful reading: the scheme is a facility a firm opts into, not a description of the sector.

The cluster programme in the same note is about common facilities and industrial estates, not about the exchange rate. Over those five years, 190 projects were approved, including 82 common facility centres and 108 infrastructure projects.

## August's customs print was not a garment print

DGCIS quick estimates for August 2026, provisional, in million US dollars:

| Commodity | August 2025 | August 2026 | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Engineering goods | 9,867.17 | 12,319.81 | +24.86% |
| Electronic goods | 2,925.52 | 5,553.09 | +89.82% |
| Petroleum products | 4,169.70 | 6,807.86 | +63.27% |
| Drugs and pharmaceuticals | 2,510.63 | 2,606.95 | +3.84% |
| Gems and jewellery | 2,311.85 | 2,327.83 | +0.69% |
| Readymade garments of all textiles | 1,234.51 | 1,200.73 | −2.74% |

For April–August 2026-27, engineering goods were US$58,704.38 million, up 19.55 per cent. Electronic goods were US$26,663.56 million, up 39.72 per cent. Readymade garments were US$6,149.66 million, **down 9.10 per cent**.

A job-working garment exporter and a firm supplying an electronics line are inside the same national export total and on opposite sides of this table. Merchandise exports as a whole were US$43,810.92 million in August, up 26.12 per cent. That headline is real. It is a poor proxy for the garment line.

July's Commerce narrative, released on 13 August 2026, had already pointed at the same mix for that month: petroleum-product exports from US$4.13 billion to US$6.92 billion, electronic goods from US$3.76 billion to US$5.92 billion, and engineering goods from US$10.40 billion to US$12.24 billion. July and August are separate months. The pattern across both is that engineering, electronics, and petroleum products carried the growth the releases chose to name.

## The rupee matters on the invoice, and software is a special case

At 1:00 pm on 24 September 2026 the RBI homepage showed an FBIL rate of **Rs 95.9099 per US dollar**, Rs 109.1912 per euro, and Rs 127.0295 per pound. A smaller goods exporter invoicing in dollars meets that rate on conversion. The rate is a benchmark at that hour. It does not, by itself, say whether the firm's rupee margin rose or fell, because input costs and the buyer's currency are outside the homepage.

The RBI's software survey for 2025-26 is the right currency map for software and IT-enabled firms, and the wrong map for a garment unit. Software services exports were US$221.4 billion. The US dollar invoiced 72.6 per cent, the euro 9.6 per cent, the rupee 6.3 per cent, and the pound 6.0 per cent. Off-site delivery was 91.7 per cent. Private limited companies accounted for 60.8 per cent of those exports. The United States took 54.1 per cent. A small software firm is statistically closer to that survey than to the MSME merchandise share. A small goods firm is closer to the DGCIS commodity line and to Niryat Disha's compliance and logistics window.

## What a founder can do with this

Match the firm to one basket before quoting a national growth rate. If the product is garments, the April–August decline is the relevant customs line. If the product is in engineering or electronics, the relevant line is the one that rose. If the firm needs working capital against export orders, the named instrument is Niryat Protsahan. If the firm needs a certificate, a fair, or a test report, the named instruments are Niryat Disha and the International Cooperation Scheme.

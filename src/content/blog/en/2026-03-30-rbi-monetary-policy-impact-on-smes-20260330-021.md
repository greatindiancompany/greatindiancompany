A small firm in India does not borrow at the repo rate. It borrows at whatever benchmark its bank or NBFC actually uses, and it pays that rate on top of a spread. The August 2026 pause matters for MSMEs because the repo rate is no longer falling, credit to micro, small and medium industry was still expanding quickly in June, and fresh lending rates had started to harden.

## Which benchmark moved, and which did not

The August 2026 Bulletin table on transmission covers February 2025 to June 2026. Over that easing cycle the policy repo rate fell 125 basis points. The external benchmark-based lending rate at 32 domestic banks fell by the same 125 basis points. The median one-year marginal cost of funds-based lending rate fell 50 basis points. The weighted average lending rate on fresh rupee loans fell 80 basis points, and the rate on outstanding rupee loans fell 91 basis points.

That split is the operating fact for a borrower. The external benchmark fell one for one with the repo rate. The median one-year MCLR fell 50 basis points, which is less than half of the 125 basis point cut. The Bulletin says pass-through to fresh and outstanding rupee loans was broad-based across major sectors. It does not publish a separate MSME weighted average in that table. The quantity of MSME credit is in the sectoral return below; the rate pass-through has to stay with these economy-wide benchmarks.

The pause is already visible in the monthly rows. With the repo rate unchanged, the fresh-loan weighted average rose 1 basis point in May 2026 and 2 basis points in June. The fresh term-deposit rate rose 4 basis points in May and 16 basis points in June. The Bulletin’s text says fresh deposit and lending rates hardened with rising credit demand. The external benchmark rate was flat in both months. The median one-year MCLR rose 10 basis points in May and fell 15 basis points in June, so the June firming in the fresh-loan average was not a move in the external benchmark.

Private-sector banks recorded stronger pass-through to lending rates than public-sector banks over the easing cycle. Public-sector banks recorded relatively higher transmission to deposit rates. An MSME loan at a public-sector bank therefore sat on the slower side of the lending-rate pass-through the Bulletin describes.

## Credit quantities were not the constraint

The Bulletin’s sectoral return, covering select banks that account for about 95 per cent of non-food credit, puts industry credit at the end of June 2026 in three size classes:

| Industry segment | Year-on-year growth, 30 June 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| Micro and small | 23.0 per cent |
| Medium | 30.3 per cent |
| Large | 16.6 per cent |
| Industry, all three | 19.2 per cent |

The priority-sector memo in the same table is a wider net. Micro and small enterprises under priority sector, which include industry and services and also priority-sector lending certificates, grew 24.5 per cent. Medium enterprises under that memo grew 27.5 per cent. Those percentages should not be added to the industry rows.

The Governor’s 5 August statement describes the same picture in words: industrial credit strengthened, helped by sustained MSME credit and a pickup in credit to large industry. Non-food bank credit grew 17.4 per cent year on year as on 15 July 2026, against 9.7 per cent a year earlier. By 31 July, the Bulletin puts scheduled commercial bank credit growth at 19.3 per cent and deposit growth at 15.4 per cent.

NBFC credit is a second pipe, and it did not behave like bank credit to industry. The Bulletin says NBFC credit grew at a double-digit pace across major sectors in June except industry, where growth moderated largely because infrastructure credit slowed. An MSME that borrows from an NBFC is in a different book from an MSME that borrows from a bank.

Market rates for larger borrowers moved the other way from bank loan growth. In the Bulletin’s corporate-bond table, the three-year AA yield averaged 8.34 per cent in August up to the 20th, 8 basis points higher than in July, with a spread of 198 basis points over the corresponding risk-free rate. The three-year BBB-minus yield averaged 11.92 per cent. The Governor’s statement puts the three-month commercial-paper rate at an average of 7.07 per cent since the June policy, against 7.52 per cent between the April and June policies. Short-term market paper eased. The lower-rated bond curve did not.

## Costs are a second channel

The August resolution says headline inflation is rising mostly because of food and fuel, with limited signs that price pressure has become general. For a firm, wholesale costs are the nearer signal. The Bulletin reports wholesale price inflation at 9.8 per cent in July 2026, from 9.9 per cent in June. Fuel and power inflation in that index was 20.0 per cent in July, down from a recent peak of 30.8 per cent in May. A staff measure of industrial input-cost inflation moderated to 15.0 per cent in July from 16.2 per cent in June, and was still elevated. Farm input-cost inflation moderated to 10.9 per cent from 11.7 per cent.

The June resolution had already noted government measures to support MSMEs and exporters in the face of the external shock. Those are fiscal and administrative steps. They are not a change in the repo rate. The monetary fact, as of the August documents, is that bank credit to micro, small and medium industry was growing faster than credit to large industry, while the internal benchmark and fresh lending rates had stopped falling.

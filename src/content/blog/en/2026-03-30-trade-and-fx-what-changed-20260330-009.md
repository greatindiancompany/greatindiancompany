# What changed in India's external accounts through July 2026

India's external account in the first months of 2026-27 is a goods gap that widened, a services surplus and a remittance inflow that covered most of it, and a July banking inflow that rebuilt reserves after a weak June quarter. The Reserve Bank of India published that sequence in three releases. They answer different questions, and they should be read in date order.

## The June quarter left a small current-account deficit

On 1 September 2026 the RBI released preliminary balance-of-payments figures for April–June 2026. The current account deficit was US$4.2 billion, or 0.5 per cent of GDP. A year earlier, in April–June 2025, it was US$3.4 billion, or 0.4 per cent of GDP.

The goods line did the widening. Merchandise exports were US$132.0 billion and imports US$218.0 billion, so the goods deficit was US$86.1 billion. In the same quarter of 2025 the goods deficit was US$68.9 billion, on exports of US$112.7 billion and imports of US$181.6 billion. Inside that, the petroleum, oil and lubricants deficit was US$37.6 billion, against US$32.2 billion a year earlier.

Services and household transfers did the offsetting. Net services receipts were US$51.6 billion, up from US$47.9 billion. Services exports were US$106.2 billion and services imports US$54.6 billion. Personal transfer receipts, mainly remittances, were US$42.9 billion, up from US$33.2 billion. Net outgo on primary income, mainly investment income, was US$10.5 billion, smaller than the US$13.3 billion outgo a year earlier.

Financing was the uncomfortable part of the quarter. Foreign direct investment recorded a net inflow of US$6.1 billion, above US$5.2 billion a year earlier. Foreign portfolio investment recorded a net outflow of US$9.6 billion, against a net inflow of US$1.6 billion. Non-resident deposits brought in US$2.8 billion, down from US$3.6 billion. External commercial borrowings to India were a net US$3.3 billion, down from US$4.4 billion. On a balance-of-payments basis, foreign-exchange reserves fell by US$8.1 billion. A year earlier they had risen by US$4.5 billion.

The same day's companion note separates valuation from transactions. In nominal terms, including valuation, reserves fell by US$22.5 billion in April–June 2026. The valuation loss was US$14.4 billion. The RBI describes that loss as mainly a lower gold price and a stronger US dollar against major currencies. A year earlier, valuation had added US$25.3 billion and nominal reserves had risen by US$29.8 billion. That note uses an older balance-of-payments format. Its capital-account lines are not the same lines as the BPM6 statement published the same day. Use the BPM6 statement for the current account. Use the companion note for the valuation split.

## July reversed the reserve drawdown

On 15 September 2026 the RBI released a preliminary monthly balance of payments for July 2026. The current account deficit was US$7.0 billion, against US$3.2 billion in July 2025. For April–July 2026 it was US$11.2 billion, against US$6.6 billion a year earlier.

The merchandise deficit in July was US$31.7 billion, on exports of US$45.1 billion and imports of US$76.8 billion. For April–July the merchandise deficit was US$117.8 billion, against US$97.1 billion a year earlier. Services still produced a surplus: US$17.6 billion in July, and US$69.3 billion for April–July, on services exports of US$38.3 billion and US$144.5 billion respectively. Net transfers were US$13.2 billion in July and US$53.9 billion for April–July.

The financing line changed character. Net foreign portfolio investment was an inflow of US$4.1 billion in July, after an April–July outflow that was still US$5.5 billion. Net FDI was US$7.3 billion in the month. The striking print is non-resident deposits: a net inflow of US$33.5 billion in July, and US$36.2 billion for April–July, against US$4.7 billion in April–July 2025. Banking capital as a whole was a net US$18.4 billion in July.

The overall balance in July was a surplus of US$20.8 billion. In the RBI's sign convention, monetary movements of −US$20.8 billion mean reserves rose by that amount on a payments basis. For April–July the overall surplus was US$12.7 billion. The July accretion more than covered the US$8.1 billion payments-basis decline of April–June. Those two published figures add to the April–July result.

Treat the July deposit print as a preliminary observation, not a new monthly run-rate. The quarterly balance of payments is where a one-month spike is either confirmed or revised.

## The reserve stock by mid-September

The weekly statistical supplement dated 18 September 2026 puts foreign-exchange reserves at US$780,782 million as on 11 September 2026. That is US$4,924 million below the previous week, US$89,674 million above end-March 2026, and US$77,816 million above a year earlier.

The stock split that week was foreign-currency assets of US$645,796 million, gold of US$111,225 million, SDRs of US$18,845 million, and a reserve position in the IMF of US$4,916 million. The week-earlier supplement, dated 11 September, had put the stock at US$785,706 million as on 4 September, after a one-week rise of US$44,903 million.

A reserve headline can rise while the current account is in deficit. July shows that. It can also fall in a week when nothing of consequence has happened to trade, because gold prices and the dollar move the valuation. The April–June valuation loss is the clean example.

## Customs data are a different clock

The Commerce quick estimates are the shipment record. The RBI balance of payments is the payment record. July merchandise exports are US$44.24 billion in the [13 August 2026 Commerce release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2298878) and US$45.1 billion in the RBI's July balance of payments. Both figures are real. They are not a revision of each other.

That Commerce release also estimated July services exports at US$35.89 billion, and it said so because the latest RBI services data it had were for June. The RBI later put July services exports at US$38,253 million in its [31 August services release](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63480), and at US$38.3 billion in the monthly balance of payments. Anyone comparing "exports" across those dates has to name the release.

The DGCIS quick estimates for August 2026, provisional and published with the mid-September tables, put merchandise exports at US$43,810.92 million and merchandise imports at US$70,670.34 million. That month belongs to the next customs print. It is not yet inside the RBI's July balance of payments.

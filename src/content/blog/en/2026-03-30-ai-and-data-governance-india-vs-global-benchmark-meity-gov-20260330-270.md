India has not published an official league table that scores the Digital Personal Data Protection Act against the GDPR or any other statute. A rank quoted from a foreign index is not a MeitY finding about this Act. The comparison that the Indian instruments themselves support is structural, and it is easy to get wrong.

## Three transfer rules, three clocks

**Payment systems, already in force.** On 6 April 2018 the Reserve Bank directed that the entire data relating to payment systems be stored in a system only in India. The data includes the full end-to-end transaction details. The foreign leg of a transaction may also be stored in the foreign country if required. The circular is issued under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007. The Reserve Bank's FAQ then says there is no bar on processing a payment abroad, but the data must afterwards be stored only in India, and if processing was done abroad the data should be deleted from systems abroad and brought back not later than one business day or 24 hours from payment processing, whichever is earlier. A system audit by a CERT-In empanelled auditor was part of the 2018 compliance path. This rule binds payment-system providers. It does not, by its own text, bind every company that holds personal data.

**Incident logs, already in force.** CERT-In's directions of 28 April 2022, under section 70B of the IT Act, require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporate, and government organisations to keep logs of their ICT systems for a rolling 180 days, within Indian jurisdiction. That is a log rule for a defined class of entities. It is not a rule that all personal data must reside in India. The directions took effect 60 days after issue. A 27 June 2022 note extended some timelines for MSMEs and for subscriber validation by data centres, VPS, cloud, and VPN providers. Use that note before assuming every clause started on the sixtieth day.

**Personal data under the DPDP Act, not yet the live transfer duty.** Section 16(1) says the Central Government may, by notification, restrict a Data Fiduciary from transferring personal data for processing to a country or territory it notifies. That is a negative list the government has the power to write. The Act does not itself name the countries. Section 16(2) says nothing in section 16 cuts down any other Indian law that imposes a higher degree of protection or a tighter restriction on transfer. That is the statutory reason the Reserve Bank's payment rule is not dissolved when section 16 comes into force.

Section 16 is in the eighteen-month commencement list. As of 24 September 2026 it is due on 13 May 2027, with the rest of sections 11 to 17. Quoting section 16 as today's general localisation statute is ahead of the gazette.

## What the Rules add, on the same later date

Rule 13(4) of the DPDP Rules, 2025 tells a Significant Data Fiduciary to ensure that personal data specified by the Central Government, on the recommendation of a committee, is processed subject to a restriction: that personal data, and the traffic data of its flow, is not transferred outside India. The committee includes officials from MeitY and may include officials from other ministries. Rule 15 says personal data processed under the Act may be transferred outside India subject to requirements the Central Government specifies, by general or special order, for making that data available to a foreign State or to a person or entity under that State's control.

Both rules are in the eighteen-month set. Even after 13 May 2027, Rule 13(4) does nothing to a particular dataset until the government specifies the data and a Significant Data Fiduciary has been notified under section 10. Section 10 is on the same eighteen-month list. PIB's note of 14 November 2025 summarises this as "localisation where required" for Significant Data Fiduciaries. "Where required" is doing the work. It is not a blanket in-country rule for every fiduciary.

Consent Managers are a narrower localisation of the entity, not of all data. The First Schedule requires the applicant to be a company incorporated in India, with net worth not less than two crore rupees. Rule 4, which opens registration, starts on 13 November 2026, not on 13 May 2027.

## How to use this instead of a rank

A global slide that says "India mandates data localisation" is mixing the payment rule, the log rule, and a future, conditional personal-data power. A slide that says "India is GDPR-equivalent" is not a sentence in the Act. The Act's own comparison tool is section 16(2): stricter Indian law still applies.

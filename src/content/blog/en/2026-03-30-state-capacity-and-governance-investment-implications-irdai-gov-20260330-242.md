# State Finance in the 2025-26 Budgets: What an Investor Should Read

An investor looking at a state-backed project, a municipal bond, or a supplier whose receivables sit with a state department needs the fiscal capacity of that state, not a national growth slogan. The Reserve Bank's study of state budgets is the primary public account of that capacity. The edition that covers 2025-26 was released on 23 January 2026. Its theme is demographic transition and what it does to state finance.

## The deficit number has a named cause

RBI reports that the consolidated gross fiscal deficit of states rose to 3.3 percent of GDP in 2024-25, after staying below 3.0 percent in the previous three years. States budgeted the same 3.3 percent for 2025-26. The Bank's own explanation of the breach of 3 percent is specific: it mainly reflects 50-year interest-free loans from the Centre under Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment, and those loans sit over and above the normal net borrowing ceiling.

That sentence changes the investment reading. A deficit that widens because a state drew an interest-free capital loan is a different risk from a deficit that widens because revenue collapsed or subsidies jumped. It is still a deficit. It still adds to the liability stock. It is not, on RBI's account, evidence that the revenue account suddenly broke. The Department of Expenditure's 27 March 2026 guidelines continue the same loan into 2026-27, at Rs 2,00,000 crore, again above the net borrowing ceiling. Anyone comparing a 2024-25 outcome with a 2026-27 borrowing plan should keep that loan inside the comparison.

Capital expenditure, on the same RBI release, held at 2.7 percent of GDP in both 2023-24 and 2024-25, and is budgeted at 3.2 percent of GDP in 2025-26. The capex line is the asset side of the story the deficit line is telling. SASCI's Part III, in the March 2026 guidelines, pays a further incentive only for growth in capital expenditure from the state's own resources, and it excludes SASCI releases from the growth calculation. An investor who wants to know whether a state is adding its own money, or recycling the Centre's interest-free loan, has an official definition to ask for.

## The debt stock is the number that does not explain itself away

The same release says consolidated outstanding liabilities of states stayed elevated after the pandemic, with a budget estimate of 29.2 percent of GDP at end-March 2026. The interest-free loan explains why the annual deficit can print above 3 percent while the borrowing ceiling is respected. It does not retire the stock. A project with a fifteen-year offtake from a state is exposed to that stock, to interest, and to whatever else is committed before the supplier is paid.

RBI's theme chapter is the forward-looking part. States are at different stages of demographic transition, and the Bank says that stage is already shaping their finances. Youthful states have a wider window because the working-age population and revenue mobilisation are still expanding, and the Bank points that window at human-capital investment. Ageing states face a narrowing window: a shrinking tax base and rising committed expenditure, which pushes them toward revenue capacity and reforms in healthcare, pensions and workforce policy. Intermediate states are told to fund growth and prepare for ageing at the same time. An investor underwriting a state for a decade should know which of those three sentences the state sits in. The January 2026 release states the typology. It does not publish, in the press note, a list that assigns every state to a box. That assignment belongs in the full study, read state by state.

## Where the transfer system still sits

The Fifteenth Finance Commission, writing for 2021-26, recommended that 41 percent of the divisible pool go to states. PIB's 1 February 2021 summary put that share at Rs 42.2 lakh crore over the five years, inside estimated aggregate transfers of about 50.9 percent of the divisible pool once grants were included. Horizontal shares used income distance at 45 percent, population and area at 15 percent each, demographic performance at 12.5 percent, forest and ecology at 10 percent, and tax and fiscal effort at 2.5 percent. The 2026-27 SASCI untied slice for states, Rs 67,000 crore inside a Rs 75,000 crore Part I, is allocated on Sixteenth Finance Commission shares. The investment point is continuity of formula plus a growing capital-loan overlay, not a guess about next year's devolution percentage.

The Department of Expenditure's Public Finance (States) Division is the office that approves state borrowings under Article 293(3), enforces the Finance Commission fiscal path, and coordinates debt monitoring with RBI. That is the counterparty behind the ceiling the SASCI loan sits above.

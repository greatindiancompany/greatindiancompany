# The Rules That Decide Metro Assistance and Rail Cargo Terminals

The regulatory outlook for Indian transit is not a bill waiting in Parliament. It is two rulebooks already in force, plus a fare practice the railway has described in the Lok Sabha.

## Central metro money has entry conditions

The Metro Rail Policy, 2017, of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, ties central assistance to city institutions. States are to constitute a Unified Metropolitan Transport Authority as a statutory body that prepares the Comprehensive Mobility Plan, organises urban-transport investment, and coordinates agencies. For projects with central assistance, the state commits to set up and operationalise that authority within a year. The equity-sharing model uses a jointly owned special-purpose vehicle. The managing director is a state nominee, with prior approval of the Centre. The chair is a nominee of the urban affairs ministry. Private participation in some form — construction, operations, fares, or another unbundled piece — is part of that model.

A PIB note on 9 August 2025 states the numeric tests associated with central financial assistance: a minimum economic internal rate of return of 14 percent, and mandatory private participation. It also records domestic procurement of at least 75 percent of metro cars and 25 percent of key equipment and subsystems. A city team that has a detailed project report and does not have the authority, the return threshold, or a private role is outside the assistance rule as published. The 14 percent figure is an economic hurdle for assistance. It is not a reported equity return.

## Cargo terminals have a clock and a set of waivers

The Gati Shakti Multi-Modal Cargo Terminal policy, launched by the Ministry of Railways on 15 December 2021, is the freight rule. Approvals are meant to be simple. Departmental charges, land licence fees on railway land used for connectivity, and commercial-staff costs are not charged to the applicant. The railway maintains common-user facilities and, outside the yard and loading lines, the track, signalling, and overhead equipment. Terminals with at least one million tonnes of outward traffic can receive the cost of a mid-section block station back as a 10 percent freight rebate. Construction is to be completed within 24 months of approval.

The 7 August 2026 reply shows how far that rule has been used: 142 terminals commissioned, about ₹10,000 crore of private investment, 224 million tonnes a year of estimated capacity, and 146 million tonnes handled in 2025-26. In-principle approvals for further locations were reported at 310 in the ministry’s 23 July 2026 account. An approval is not a commissioned terminal. The 24-month clock is the regulatory fact that sits between the two.

Wagon investment schemes are a parallel permission: special-purpose wagons for cement, oil, steel, and fly ash, general-purpose rakes, and a separate automobile scheme. They are permissions to bring rolling stock, not a concession on the dedicated freight corridor itself. Both commissioned corridors remain ministry projects. Dankuni–Surat is a detailed project report, not a terminal licence.

## Fares are a published railway decision

On 11 March 2026 the ministry said it does not treat ticket fares as a trade secret, and that fare changes are published. Fares are set with reference to cost of service, competition from other modes, and affordability. The same reply disclosed a passenger subsidy of ₹60,239 crore in 2024-25. Any reading of “regulatory risk” on passenger yield has to start from that subsidy, which the railway has said is continuing, rather than from an assumed move to cost-reflective fares.

# Why 679 operationalised UDAN routes are not 679 routes still flying

The execution fact in regional aviation is a gap the Ministry of Civil Aviation has already measured. Since the Regional Connectivity Scheme began, 679 routes have been operationalised. On 23 July 2026 the ministry told the Lok Sabha that 348 of them were still operational. A week later, dated 10 July, the same ministry said those 679 routes connected 95 airports, including 17 heliports and 2 water aerodromes, had benefited 168 lakh passengers, and had produced 3.58 lakh flights.

The passenger and flight totals are cumulative successes. They do not restore a route that has stopped. Anyone using 168 lakh passengers as evidence that the network is intact is reading a stock of trips, not a count of city pairs still served.

## Where the design itself was the bottleneck

The 16 July 2026 scheme comparison says the quiet part in the government’s own words. Airlines could bid without an air operator permit. Some bid networks beyond the aircraft they could field. Aerodromes were developed after airlines chose them, so the strip and the operator were sequenced the wrong way round. Support lasted three years and then stopped. Slots at metro airports for regional flights were not part of the scheme.

The discontinued-before-tenure annexure shows the result is not evenly spread. Of the 165 routes that stopped before the support period ended, 34 are in Uttar Pradesh, 17 in Uttarakhand, 15 in Assam, 14 in Madhya Pradesh, and 12 in Karnataka. The bottleneck is not “the North-East” or “the Hindi belt” as a single story. Assam and Uttarakhand both sit high on the early-exit list, and so does Uttar Pradesh, which is also the country’s largest domestic-visit state.

Routes were still being added while others dropped. A separate reply on 6 August says 188 RCS routes were made operational in the last three financial years. Addition and attrition are simultaneous. A dashboard with only “routes operationalised this year” will look like execution. The 348 figure is the one that survives attrition.

## What the new scheme changes in the sequence

Modified UDAN, launched on 4 July 2026, reorders the work. Aerodromes are supposed to be prioritised through a state challenge before airlines bid. Bidders need a permit, a business plan, and aircraft. Missing the commencement window triggers a re-bid and bars the first bidder. Support can run five years, tapering to 75, 50, and 25 percent. The ministry says it will try to find usable metro slots. None of that has a published scoreboard yet. The scoreboard that exists is the old scheme’s 679 and 348.

Tamil Nadu is a small, concrete picture of the difference between “identified” and “operationalised.” The 30 July reply says four airports — Salem, Vellore, Neyveli, and Tanjore — have been identified for RCS development and operation. The annexure of operationalised routes lists Salem–Bengaluru, Salem–Cochin, Salem–Hyderabad, and several Chennai spokes, and it lists Salem–Bengaluru twice. The paper does not explain the repeated line. A state cell should not turn “four airports identified” into “four airports with a durable network.”

## What is still scarce

The 6 August reply on indigenous aircraft is explicit about one physical bottleneck: a shortage of small aircraft and helicopters for regional and difficult-terrain flying. The Cabinet response inside the scheme is two HAL Dhruv helicopters and two HAL Dornier aircraft. That is a four-airframe decision. It does not fill the gap between 348 routes flying and the 120 new destinations the ten-year target names.

Funding has not been the piece that ran out. Provisional viability-gap funding in 2025-26 was ₹738.13 crore, between the two previous years of ₹807.02 crore and ₹628.42 crore. The constraint the ministry describes is commencement and staying power, not an empty subsidy account.

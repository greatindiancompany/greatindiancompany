# Uptime, Permission, and the Last Drop Slow BharatNet

BharatNet's public story has a large numerator. The execution story is the gap between that numerator and a connection a household can use on a Tuesday. Three official gaps describe the bottleneck. They are permission time, uptime, and the last drop into the home.

## Permission is fast on average and slow in named states

The Telecommunications (Right of Way) Rules, 2024 took effect on 1 January 2025 and, in the government's account, put uniform charges in place. By 12 February 2026, average disposal time had fallen from 455 days to 30.4 days, which the minister treated as the 2030 ease target already met. Thirty-three of 36 states and union territories had implemented the rules. Tamil Nadu and West Bengal were named among those where compliance was pending. Tamil Nadu's disposal time was about 85 days, and 84.9 days in the written statement for 2025–26. A national average of a month does not clear a trench in a state that still takes about three months. The amended programme's ring-fibre design assumes the trench gets dug.

## Online, and up, are different counts

PIB's February 2026 reply says nearly 2.14 lakh gram panchayats out of 2.56 lakh had been made online under Phases I and II, at an approximate cost of Rs 42,000 crore. The National Broadband Mission 2.0 line in the same reply says optical fibre with 95 percent uptime covered 42,000 villages as of December 2025, toward 2.7 lakh villages by 2030. The Amended BharatNet design, priced at Rs 1,39,579 crore in the December 2024 note, exists because the first build was not the end state: it specifies ring topology, ten years of maintenance against a service-level agreement, a central network operating centre, remote fibre monitoring, and power backup. Power backup is in the design because a dark site is a known failure mode, not an afterthought.

In Tamil Nadu the same reply is more local. Operational fibre at 95 percent uptime was in 4,325 villages as of January 2026. Service-ready gram panchayats, excluding block headquarters, were 10,869 of 12,525 as on 31 December 2025. Anchor institutions in the state were already at 84.74 percent, above the national 68.8 percent. The bottleneck in that state is less "is there a panchayat on the map" and more "does the fibre stay up, and does the state permission match the national clock."

## The last drop is still thin

Tamil Nadu had 808 fibre-to-the-home connections through BharatNet, against a plan under the amended programme for 5,27,506 households over ten years, implemented by the state. Facility providers had laid 3,08,907 route kilometres in the state, about 55,000 of them under BharatNet. Route kilometres and household connections move on different clocks. Nationally, TRAI's July 2026 note puts fixed wired broadband at 48.14 million and rural wireline tele-density at 0.57 percent. Urban areas hold 89.19 percent of wireline subscribers. The mobile network, with rural tele-density of 61.05 percent, is how most rural users are online. A gram-panchayat fibre that stops at the panchayat office does not change that.

5G fixed wireless, 13.21 million lines at end-July and slightly more rural than urban, is the bypass some operators are already selling where the last drop of fibre is late. It does not retire the uptime target. The mission still scores villages on fibre at 95 percent uptime.

Where that bypass, and the anchor-institution gap, leave room for a product is the [opportunity brief](https://greatindiancompany.com/blog/telecom-broadband-opportunity-landscape-mohfw-gov-20260330-632).

Cross-country startup rankings usually mix private databases, each with its own rule for what counts as a startup, a round, or a “unicorn”. Indian official statistics do not publish that kind of table. What they publish is large, precise, and easy to misuse in an international slide.

## The comparison PIB actually makes

On 15 January 2026, the Press Information Bureau marked a decade of Startup India. The note says that with over 2 lakh DPIIT-recognised startups as of December 2025, India “stands firmly as one of the world’s largest startup ecosystems”. A Rajya Sabha reply dated 13 February 2026 makes the December figure exact: 2,07,135 recognised entities as on 31 December 2025. By 30 June 2026 a Lok Sabha reply put the stock at 2,40,092.

“One of the world’s largest” in that note is a claim about this registry. The registry’s rule is Indian and administrative: private limited company, partnership, LLP, or cooperative; age and turnover ceilings under G.S.R. 108(E); an innovation or wealth claim; exclusion of split-ups. Another country’s company register, venture-deal database, or unicorn list uses a different rule. A larger Indian number can be a wider net. A smaller foreign number can be a narrower one. Neither fact, by itself, ranks ecosystems.

This brief does not add a private global ranking to fill the gap. Where PIB offers a global phrase, the phrase is quoted and the denominator is named.

## The other official sentence is a valuation slice

In the same 15 January note, PIB describes a high-value landscape: from four privately held companies valued above $1 billion in 2014 to over 120 such firms, with a combined valuation exceeding $350 billion. That is the government’s published contrast between 2014 and the date of the note. It is not a DPIIT field. DPIIT does not, in the releases used here, certify a dollar valuation for each recognised entity.

Over 120 firms against 2,07,135 recognised entities at December 2025 is the scale difference. The billion-dollar description and the recognition stock can both be true because they count different objects. A slide that labels 2 lakh Indian “startups” as comparable to a few hundred “unicorns” in another country has changed the unit.

PIB does not name a commercial data vendor for the “over 120” sentence. This page does not supply one, and it does not update the sentence past 15 January 2026.

## What can be compared without a private dataset

Some official facts travel better across borders because the institution is named.

SEBI’s Alternative Investment Funds Regulations, 2012, last amended on 18 April 2026, are the domestic rulebook for pooled venture and private-equity capital. Category I is the bucket aimed at startups, early-stage ventures, and related socially or economically desired sectors, and it includes venture capital funds. Fund of Funds 2.0 may commit only to Category I and Category II funds. A global limited partner comparing India with another market can compare regulatory categories and government commitment rules. That is a cleaner comparison than comparing recognition headcount with a foreign deal count.

Inside India, the comparable capital figures are also official and modest next to the registry. At the end of 2025-26, funds backed by the first Fund of Funds had invested more than ₹26,900 crore in over 1,420 startups. Seed-fund incubators had approved over ₹605 crore to more than 3,400 startups. The credit guarantee covered over ₹1,250 crore across more than 410 loans. Those are programme outcomes. They are not a statement of India’s share of world venture capital.

Jobs need the same care. More than 23.36 lakh direct jobs as on 31 March 2026 come from startups’ own reports to DPIIT, a point the parliamentary annexure states in those words. They are not an International Labour Organization series, and they should not be lined up against another country’s employer survey.

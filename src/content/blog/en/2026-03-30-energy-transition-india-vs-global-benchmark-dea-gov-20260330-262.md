# India's Energy Transition Against the Global Numbers

International rankings are useful when you know the unit. India's official global comparison for renewable energy is a comparison of installed capacity, dated, and published by the International Renewable Energy Agency. It is not a ranking of emissions, of electricity actually generated, or of progress toward net zero.

## The ranking is a capacity table

On 8 April 2026 the Union Minister for New and Renewable Energy said India had moved to third place in renewable-energy installed capacity in IRENA's Renewable Energy Statistics 2026, ahead of Brazil, on data as of December 2025. The briefing reproduced the IRENA capacity table in gigawatts:

- China: 2,258.02
- United States: 467.92
- India: 250.52
- Brazil: 228.20
- Germany: 199.92
- Japan: 134.53
- Canada: 110.51
- World: 5,149.28

Third place is real on that list. The distance to first place is also real. China's listed renewable capacity is about nine times India's. The United States is a little under twice India's figure. A rank without those neighbours turns a stock comparison into a medal.

IRENA's yearbook says capacity is the maximum net generating capacity installed and, for most countries, connected at the end of the calendar year. Generation statistics in the same publication run through 2024, a year behind the capacity table. Anyone comparing "India is third" with a generation chart from another agency is comparing two different years and two different units.

## India's own later stock is a different cut

MNRE's figure for 31 March 2026 is not the IRENA December 2025 number, and it should not be forced to match. The Ministry reported 274.68 GW of renewable capacity on that date: 150.26 GW solar, 56.09 GW wind, 51.41 GW large hydro, 11.75 GW bio-energy, and 5.17 GW small hydro. Adding 8.78 GW of nuclear, non-fossil capacity was 283.46 GW. By 31 July 2026, MNRE's sheet shows 291,725.64 MW of renewable capacity including large hydro, and 300,505.64 MW of non-fossil capacity.

Large hydro is inside MNRE's renewable total. International series sometimes treat pumped storage and large hydro differently from solar and wind. The honest use of the two publications is sequential. IRENA places India third on a December 2025 renewable-capacity definition. MNRE then shows how the domestic stock moved through March and July 2026, on the Ministry's own rows.

Wind has a separate rank in the same April briefing: fourth in global installed wind-turbine capacity, after the domestic stock crossed 56 GW. Solar's global claim in the 13 August 2026 PIB factsheet is about growth in 2025, not about the all-time stock: India was described as the world's second-largest solar growth market that year, adding more than 37 GW, compared with 34 GW added by the United States. A growth rank and a stock rank answer different questions. India can add solar quickly and still hold far less renewable capacity than China.

## The domestic benchmark that matters more

The comparison that changes a business decision is inside India, between capacity and generation.

PIB said India met the updated NDC mark of 50 percent non-fossil installed capacity in June 2025, five years before 2030. By 31 July 2026 the share was over 54 percent of a system PIB described as around 552 GW. In the same April 2026 briefing, non-fossil sources were 29.2 percent of electricity generated in FY 2025-26: 538.97 billion units out of 1,845.921 billion units. In July 2025, the Minister said, renewables met 51.5 percent of electricity demand of 203 GW, which the briefing called the highest renewable share in generation the country had reached. A high month and a 29.2 percent year can both be published. The year is the figure to use when judging how much of India's electrical energy has actually moved.

The updated NDC is built for this kind of comparison. It commits India to a 45 percent cut in the emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 from 2005, and to about 50 percent non-fossil installed capacity, with the capacity goal written as dependent on technology and low-cost finance. It does not commit India to an absolute emissions cap of the kind some other Paris agreements use. Ranking India against a country that has an absolute cap, using only a renewable-capacity league table, skips the unit India's own pledge uses.

## How to cite the rank without overworking it

Say: as of IRENA's December 2025 statistics, India was third in renewable installed capacity, behind China and the United States and ahead of Brazil. Then say what the domestic generation accounts show. The capacity rank explains India's place in the global build-out. The 29.2 percent generation share explains how far the power system has moved. Both belong in a serious brief. Only one of them is a podium.

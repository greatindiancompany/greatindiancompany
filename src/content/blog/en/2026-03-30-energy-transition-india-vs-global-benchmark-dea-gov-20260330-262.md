# India's Energy Transition Beside The Global Pattern

Comparing India's energy transition with a global chart is useful only if the chart holds demand still. In many high-income systems, electricity demand is flat or growing slowly, and the policy argument is about retiring coal and replacing it. In India, demand is still growing, electricity use is still spreading across more hours and more appliances, and new renewable capacity is added on top of a coal fleet that continues to generate. A benchmark that ignores that difference will call India slow at retiring plants and miss why the plants are still running.

## Addition under rising demand

Solar and wind are the bulk of new capacity. That part of the story resembles other large markets that found utility solar cheap enough to scale. The generation mix does not resemble a country that has already flattened demand and closed mine-mouth coal. Coal remains central to electrical energy and to the evening, when solar has left the system. Hydro and, where it is being added, storage are the flexibility India can actually call. Gas has not played the bridging role it plays in some Atlantic systems, because fuel availability and price do not make it the default peaker.

The fair question is whether non-fossil capacity is being absorbed while demand rises, not whether India's retirement schedule copies a European one.

## Manufacturing and deployment are different races

India is both a deployment market and a late builder of solar manufacturing. Module lists, production incentives, and import dependence have all been part of that second race. A country can install a large solar fleet with imported modules and still treat domestic manufacturing as unfinished. Benchmarks that mix "capacity installed" with "share of modules made at home" are two rankings. Publish them separately.

Hydrogen, rooftop solar, and agricultural solar are programme tracks under the renewable ministry. They are not evidence that India has the same household-electrification problem, or the same industrial-fuel problem, as every peer. Rooftop progress depends on distribution utilities and roofs. Green hydrogen depends on cheap clean electricity that the grid does not yet have in surplus in every hour.

## Grid shape is the real comparison

Other large renewable builders have hit the same wall India is hitting: midday surplus, evening scarcity, transmission between resource and load, and buyers that are not always creditworthy. Ministry of Power reforms on access, deviation, and distribution are the Indian version of that wall. They should be compared with other countries' connection queues and utility finances, not with a single global "percent renewable" badge.

This brief does not assign India a rank or a megawatt gap to a peer. Any numerical comparison should cite the year, the fuel definition, and whether the figure is capacity or generation. Those three choices change the result.

# SEBI's 2026 mutual fund regulations, in plain language

The policy shift in India's household equity market is not a new IPO chapter. It is the replacement of the 1996 mutual fund regulations. SEBI notified the Mutual Funds Regulations, 2026 on 14 January 2026. The legal page now shows that text last amended on 7 July 2026. The Annual Report for 2025-26, Chapter 5, explains why the rewrite happened and which investor-facing rules moved with it.

## Why the 1996 book was retired

Chapter 5 says three decades of amendments had made the 1996 regulations long and layered. The 2026 text keeps the older safeguards and rewrites the form. Valuation rules that used to sit in several places are gathered into one chapter. Scheme documents, the scheme information document, the statement of additional information, and the key information memorandum, move to prescribed formats. Newspaper-advertisement rules are cut back in favour of website, SMS, and email disclosure. Provisions that are operational rather than statutory sit in the master circular, which SEBI says was reviewed so it matches the new regulations.

That is a drafting reform. It does not, by itself, change a scheme's portfolio. It does change which document a trustee, a distributor, or a fund analyst should treat as current.

## The investor rules Chapter 5 actually names

Several conduct rules moved in the same review. They are specific enough to use.

The maximum exit load falls from 5 per cent to 3 per cent. The load is still credited back to the scheme, so remaining investors are not paying the leaver. SEBI notes that industry practice was already around 1 to 2 per cent. The cap is a ceiling, not a new charge.

Transaction charges and upfront commissions paid by the investor are discontinued. SEBI's principle, as stated in the chapter, is that the distributor is the agent of the asset management company and is paid by the AMC.

The beyond-top-30-cities incentive is narrowed. Extra pay is only for a new individual investor, identified by a new PAN, at industry level. The cap is 1 per cent of the first lump-sum application, or of SIP investments in the first year, and ₹2,000 per investor overall. The money comes from the investor-education fund, not from a higher scheme expense. A separate incentive, on the same 1 per cent and ₹2,000 pattern, is aimed at new women investors.

Specialized investment funds, introduced by a February 2025 circular under the old regulations, sit between a plain mutual fund and portfolio management. SEBI describes a minimum investment of ₹10 lakh per investor, and standard forms for the strategy document and for trustee reports.

Equity index funds and equity exchange-traded funds received an enabling borrowing provision so they can take part in the closing auction session. Physical gold and silver in schemes are valued off domestic exchange spot prices rather than LBMA prices. The cut-off for online repurchase of overnight schemes used to upstream client funds moves from 3 pm to 7 pm. AMCs no longer file 52 specified reports.

Scheme categories were adjusted as well. Chapter 5 mentions sectoral debt funds, tighter overlap rules for sector and thematic equity schemes, public disclosure of that overlap, and life-cycle funds with a pre-set glide path. Those are product-architecture rules. A new scheme name is not, on its own, a new asset class.

## What the money looked like while the rule changed

The rewrite landed in a large industry. Chapter 5 reports mutual fund assets of ₹73.7 lakh crore at March 2026, up from ₹31.43 lakh crore in March 2021 and up 12.2 per cent in 2025-26. Unique investors rose 13.2 per cent to 6.1 crore. SIP accounts rose from 10.05 crore to 10.45 crore, while 7.2 crore new SIPs were registered and 6.8 crore were discontinued or matured. Average net monthly SIP contribution rose from ₹13,052 crore to ₹16,413 crore. Gross mobilisation was ₹157.5 lakh crore. Net inflows were ₹7.4 lakh crore, down 9.7 per cent because redemptions rose faster than sales.

Fifty-four asset management companies were registered at 31 March 2026, of which five are public sector, plus three inactive funds. Individuals held 97.7 per cent of folios and 58.3 per cent of assets. Institutions held 0.6 per cent of folios and 37.8 per cent of assets.

## What this does, and does not, change for an IPO

A mutual fund is a buyer of both the secondary market and the anchor or institutional book of an IPO. The 2026 regulations do not set the retail application size in an IPO. That remains an ICDR question. They do shape the pool of domestic money that has been the stabiliser when foreign portfolio investors sell. Chapter 4 of the same annual report says mutual funds contributed about three quarters of domestic institutional net equity buying in 2025-26, ₹6.4 trillion against ₹4.9 trillion the year before.

For a founder, the practical point is distribution. The people who fill an anchor reservation, which the primary-market chapter sets at 33.33 per cent of the anchor book for domestic mutual funds, now operate under the 2026 fund regulations and the July consolidation. For a household, the practical point is the document: the scheme's key information memorandum should be read against the 7 July 2026 text, and the exit-load cap, the ban on investor-paid upfront commission, and the SIP churn numbers above are the parts that change the experience of staying invested.

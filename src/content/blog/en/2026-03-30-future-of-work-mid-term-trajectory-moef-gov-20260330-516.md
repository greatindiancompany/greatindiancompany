# The medium-term path for participation, gig work, and unfinished rules

A medium-term view of work in India has to keep three paths apart. One is a 2022 scenario for gig headcount through 2029–30. One is the measured rise in labour-force participation, especially among women, from 2017–18 to 2023–24. One is the legal path from a code that took effect in November 2025 to rules and contribution dates that were still described as forthcoming in the Economic Survey presented in early 2026. Blending them produces a single upward line that none of the sources draws.

## The gig scenario, labelled as a scenario

NITI Aayog's June 2022 report estimated 68 lakh gig workers in 2019–20, using principal and subsidiary status, or 2.4 percent of the non-farm workforce and 1.3 percent of all workers. For 2020–21 it estimated 77 lakh, or 2.6 percent of the non-agricultural workforce and 1.5 percent of the total workforce. It then extended the series to 2.35 crore workers by 2029–30, which it expected would be 6.7 percent of the non-agricultural workforce or 4.1 percent of total livelihood.

The report was clear about the method. Gig workers were identified from characteristics — urban location, age, education, and household consumption — rather than from a question that asks "are you a gig worker." The employment elasticity of that estimated group was above one from 2011–12 to 2019–20, and above the elasticity for employment overall. The authors read that as growth creating more demand for this kind of work than for other work, and as some non-gig work converting into gig work. That reading is an interpretation of the historical elasticity. It is not a law that elasticity stays above one after 2020.

On composition, the estimate put about 47 percent of gig work in medium-skilled jobs, 22 percent in high-skilled, and 31 percent in low-skilled, with the medium share drifting down and the other two rising. The report expected medium-skilled work to remain the largest block through 2030. Retail trade and sales accounted for about 26.6 lakh of the estimated workers, transportation for about 13 lakh. A medium-term plan that staffs only for app-based driving, or only for high-end freelance professionals, misses the middle the report itself said would still dominate.

The Press Information Bureau note of the report's launch, on 27 June 2022, repeats the 77 lakh and 2.35 crore figures. It does not update them. No later official estimate is used in this brief. Until one appears, 2029–30 remains a scenario with a published method, not a target the government has scored itself against.

## The participation path that is already measured

The path with observations behind it is in the Periodic Labour Force Survey. Usual-status participation for persons aged 15 and above rose from 49.8 percent in 2017–18 to 60.1 percent in 2023–24, on the Directorate General of Employment's summary. The worker population ratio rose from 46.8 percent to 58.2 percent. Unemployment on the same usual-status concept fell from 6.0 percent to 3.2 percent.

Women account for most of the participation gain. The 2023–24 press note records women's usual-status participation at 23.3 percent in 2017–18 and 41.7 percent in 2023–24, and at 37.0 percent as recently as July 2022–June 2023. Men's rate was already 75.8 percent in 2017–18 and 78.8 percent in 2023–24. Rural participation rose from 50.7 percent to 63.7 percent; urban participation from 47.6 percent to 52.0 percent.

A medium-term trajectory that talks only about platforms is talking about a slice NITI put at 1.5 percent of workers in 2020–21. A trajectory that ignores women, rural work, and the gap between a 41.7 percent usual-status rate and an urban weekly rate still near 25 percent in 2024 is ignoring the movement the survey actually recorded. Both belong in the picture. They are not the same movement.

The Economic Survey 2025–26 adds a growth-accounting view. Labour input, on KLEMS estimates, grew about 2.3 percent a year on average from 2013–14 to 2019–20. After the pandemic the Survey describes the series as volatile, with a mix of disruption and faster formalisation. Its medium-term judgment is that consolidation of labour law, lower compliance friction, state regulatory reform, and continued spending on education, skilling, and apprenticeships should allow labour-input growth to settle above the pre-pandemic pace. That sentence is the Survey's, and it is conditional. It is the right kind of medium-term statement: a direction tied to institutions, not a headcount for a year.

## What has to happen for the legal path to catch the labour path

The codes have been effective since 21 November 2025. The Survey's early-2026 preface still expected the rules "in the next few months." Section 114(5) of the Code on Social Security withholds the start of aggregator contributions until the Centre notifies a date. Karnataka's fee is a notified percent inside 1 to 5 percent of payout. Rajasthan's Act starts on a notified date.

If those notifications arrive and schemes are funded, the 2029–30 gig scenario sits inside a contributory frame, and the participation rise sits inside a wage code and an appointment-letter rule. If they do not, the participation numbers can keep moving while gig work remains a definition without a levy. The medium-term risk is not that one official forecast will be missed by a lakh of workers. It is that readers will treat the NITI scenario, the PLFS trend, and the code's commencement as a single completed reform. They are three clocks, and in September 2026 only the PLFS trend has a long run of observations behind it.

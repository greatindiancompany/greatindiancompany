# How the Reserve Bank sets a commercial bank's capital rule

India's commercial-bank capital rule is a directions document issued under section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949. The current text is the [Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks - Prudential Norms on Capital Adequacy) Directions, 2025](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=13159&Mode=0), dated 28 November 2025 and updated as on 1 July 2026. Reading it as "Basel, applied in India" is fair only if the scope and the three pillars stay attached to that sentence.

## Who is inside

The Directions define a commercial bank as a banking company other than a small finance bank, a payment bank, and a local area bank, together with corresponding new banks and the State Bank of India, using the definitions in section 5 of the Banking Regulation Act. A reader who applies the 9 percent minimum, or the leverage ratio in these Directions, to a small finance bank or a payments bank is using the wrong instruction. Those banks are pointed to their licensing and operating guidelines.

Capital adequacy is applied at group and consolidated level as well, including the treatment of a non-operative financial holding company and, for a foreign bank in branch mode, specified non-bank affiliates under management control. The detail is in the scope chapter. The point for a non-specialist is that solo and consolidated ratios can differ, and distribution limits follow the tighter one.

## Three pillars, in the Reserve Bank's words

The Directions say the framework has three components. Pillar 1 is the minimum capital requirement. Pillar 2 is the supervisory review and evaluation process. Pillar 3 is market discipline.

Under Pillar 1, a bank maintains a minimum capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratio of 9 percent on an ongoing basis, other than capital buffers. Risk-weighted assets add credit risk, market risk, and operational risk. Inside the 9 percent, common equity must be at least 5.5 percent and Tier 1 at least 7 percent. The capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percent is additional common equity. A leverage ratio of 4 percent for a domestic systemically important bank, and 3.5 percent for other banks under these Directions, sits next to the risk-weighted minimum and uses Tier 1 capital over an exposure measure.

Pillar 2 is where the minimum stops being uniform. The Reserve Bank looks at the bank's risks, including interest-rate risk in the banking book, liquidity risk, concentration risk, and residual risk, and at the quality of the systems that measure them. It may prescribe a higher minimum ratio for that bank. Banks are expected to run their own internal capital adequacy assessment and to operate well above the regulatory minimum. The Directions also attach stress-testing guidance: a bank runs the prescribed shocks at least half-yearly, and those results feed the supervisory review, which can include additional capital or liquidity buffers.

Pillar 3 is disclosure. The Directions set templates for capital composition, reconciliation, and the leverage ratio, reported so that a reader outside the bank can see the elements Pillar 1 requires. Disclosure is quarterly for the leverage ratio. It is not a substitute for the Reserve Bank's own supervisory assessment.

## What the Directions still leave to other texts

Prompt corrective action, priority-sector targets, conduct rules on digital lending, and the ombudsman scheme are separate instructions. A capital ratio above the table does not, by itself, answer whether a loan is priority sector or whether a complaint is maintainable. The Reserve Bank may also clarify these Directions, and that clarification is final on matters the Directions cover. Other laws continue to apply. The Directions are in addition to them.

# Who has to be in the room for an Indian issue

An IPO is often described as a founder event. The rulebook staffs it as a chain of registered intermediaries. SEBI's Annual Report 2025-26 does not publish a headcount of bankers, lawyers, or compliance analysts. It does publish who must be registered, how merchant bankers are now tiered, and how many firms held those permissions on 31 March 2026. That is the jobs map the regulator actually drew.

## Merchant bankers are tiered by net worth and by mandate

Chapter 3 says the merchant banker regulations were amended to split the business. Non-SEBI work has to sit in a separate unit, behind a Chinese wall, and the client must be told that SEBI's investor-protection machinery does not cover it. Net worth of the regulated business is ring-fenced from that unit.

The permission itself is now two categories. Category 1 needs a minimum net worth of ₹50 crore and may manage every permitted activity, including a main-board equity issue. Category 2 needs ₹10 crore and may do the other functions, not the management of a main-board equity issue. Firms must keep 25 per cent of net worth in liquid assets, meet a three-year cumulative revenue test, and employ a compliance officer who holds a professional legal or company-secretary qualification.

That last line is a hiring rule, not a slogan. A main-board IPO cannot be "managed" by a firm that only clears the ₹10 crore bar. A compliance desk on either category needs a qualified company secretary or a lawyer in the compliance seat. SEBI's annual report also has a chapter on the National Institute of Securities Markets, the body that sits under SEBI for market education. This note does not invent enrolment or placement figures from that chapter. The qualification the merchant banker amendment names is the concrete skill requirement.

On 31 March 2026, Table 3.17 counts 240 merchant bankers, against 230 a year earlier: 17 registrations granted and 7 surrendered or cancelled. During the year, 29 merchant banker applications were received.

## The rest of the primary chain is smaller and specific

The same table counts the other primary-market permissions.

- Registrars to an issue and share transfer agents: 80, after 7 grants and 4 cancellations. Fifteen applications came in during the year.
- Bankers to an issue: 60, after one grant and one cancellation.
- Debenture trustees: 25, unchanged.

Debenture trustees are the debt-side profession the chapter strengthens. They may inspect issuer records and demand information, must segregate non-SEBI work behind a Chinese wall, and may draw the recovery-expense fund for defined legal costs without waiting for a fresh investor consent. A corporate-bond issue, which in 2025-26 was overwhelmingly a private placement, still needs this role when the instrument is listed and secured. Public debt issues were 43 and raised ₹11,343 crore. The trustee book is not sized by the equity IPO count.

Registrars show up again in Chapter 4's process reforms. Under the revised LODR treatment of regulation 39, a registrar or listed company credits securities straight into the demat account instead of issuing a letter of confirmation. SEBI describes the demat-credit timeline compressing from about 150 days to about 30 days. A special window runs from 5 February 2026 to 4 February 2027 for investors who hold original certificates and a transfer deed for a purchase made before 1 April 2019. That is registrar work, and it is dated.

## Depositories, exchanges, and the compliance work after listing

NSDL and CDSL are the two depositories. Chapter 4 says they hold securities in demat form for beneficial owners, through depository participants. Demat accounts across both were 22.5 crore at year-end, and unique accounts were 12.2 crore. The primary-market consequence is a precondition, not a headcount: promoter group, selling shareholders, key managers, senior management, qualified institutional buyers, and directors dematerialise before the draft prospectus is filed. Someone at the company and at the depository participant has to make that true before the file moves.

After listing, the compliance profession is LODR. Chapter 3 records the exchange standard operating procedure for non-compliance. The exchange-wise table lists promoter demat freezes for 486 companies at BSE, 99 at NSE, and 41 at MSEI, and marks 2025-26 fine data as provisional (about ₹32.1 crore, ₹29.5 crore, and ₹2.3 crore on those three venues). Suspensions under that procedure were 22, 8, and 1. The skill on the company side is the ability to file results, shareholding patterns, and governance reports on time. The skill on the exchange side is the procedure that escalates when they do not.

Processing times hint at where specialised staff sit inside SEBI itself. For public-issue offer documents, the median time excluding days when a query sat with the intermediary or another regulator was 25 working days in 2025-26. Including that wait, the median was 84 working days. The bottleneck the table measures is often the issuer's answer, which is a company-secretary and banker task, not a queue at the regulator alone.

None of these figures is an employment forecast. They are the seats the regulations create: a category-matched merchant banker, a qualified compliance officer, a registrar who can credit demat directly, a depository participant who can mark a pledge non-transferable, a debenture trustee on a listed bond, and a listed-company compliance desk that can survive the LODR calendar.

# India's rails set against the G20's own definition

A country ranking of digital public infrastructure would need a shared scorecard, identical definitions, and a publisher willing to stand behind the scores. The G20 did not produce that scorecard. It produced a definition and a voluntary framework. That is the only benchmark this brief will use. Comparisons that declare India first, or that assign points against Brazil, Estonia, or Singapore, are not in the documents below.

## The benchmark text

The G20 New Delhi Leaders' Declaration, adopted on 9 September 2023, describes digital public infrastructure as an evolving concept and as a set of shared digital systems. Those systems are built and used by both the public and private sectors. They rest on secure and resilient infrastructure. They can be built on open standards and specifications, and on open-source software. They can enable delivery of services at societal scale.

The leaders welcomed the G20 Framework for Systems of Digital Public Infrastructure as a voluntary and suggested framework for development, deployment, and governance. They also noted India's plan to build a global repository of digital public infrastructure, voluntarily shared by G20 members and others. A repository of examples is not a ranking.

The Digital Economy Ministers' annex of 19 August 2023 adds the operating detail. It says members' approaches differ, that the concept may not be limited to one pattern, and that it can be tailored and referred to with different names. The word "public" can mean public benefit and access, subject to governance and oversight by public authorities. It then lists three functions that shared systems can fulfil:

- Identification, including complementary trust services such as electronic signatures and verifiable credentials.
- Payments: easy and instant transfer of money among people, businesses, and governments.
- Data sharing with consent where applicable, across public and private sectors, with the safeguards of the applicable data-governance framework.

A fair benchmark asks whether a country has shared, governed systems for those three functions, not whether its monthly payment volume exceeds another country's.

## Identification

India's identity system is Aadhaar, run by the Unique Identification Authority of India. The Press Information Bureau's 29 July 2026 account calls it a biometric and demographic unique digital identity and says more than 144 crore Aadhaar IDs have been generated. The Lok Sabha annexure to the same day's MeitY reply says enrolments rose from 0.42 crore in 2010-11 to over 144 crore by June 2026.

UIDAI's own saturation report as on 30 June 2026 is the more careful document. It estimates 1,34,76,52,046 live Aadhaar assignments against a projected 2026 population of 1,42,46,96,577, which it states as 94.59 percent live penetration. The report adjusts for estimated deaths using death rates from the Registrar General, and it counts holders by the address on the Aadhaar, who may not be domiciled in that state. Live penetration above 100 percent in some states is a result of that method, not a claim that every resident and then some holds a number.

Against the G20 function, India has a shared identification system at population scale, with an official residual: several states are far from the national percentage. Nagaland's live penetration in that report is 62.41 percent. Arunachal Pradesh is 77.77 percent. A benchmark that stops at "India has Aadhaar" passes the existence test and fails the inclusion test the same publisher documents.

Electronic signatures sit beside identity in the G20 text. The 29 July PIB account says eSign providers had issued 155.56 crore e-Signs in total, of which 44.23 crore were issued by CDAC, and that about 12.46 crore were issued in the previous year. That is a trust service in the sense the ministers' annex describes. It is not a second identity system.

## Payments

UPI is India's shared payment system. NPCI's product statistics for August 2026 show 752 banks live, 24,508.96 million transactions, and value of ₹29,82,355.95 crore. The series is monthly, public, and defined. From August 2018 it excludes transactions that debit and credit the same account.

The government has also made an international comparison, and it should be labelled as such. The PIB account of 29 July 2026 says UPI powers 85 percent of India's digital payments and nearly 50 percent of global real-time digital payments, and that it is the world's largest digital payment system. Those sentences are the government's. The release points readers to NPCI for state and Union Territory statistics. It does not reproduce the international worksheet behind the "nearly 50 percent" clause. A benchmark note can report the sentence. It cannot audit it from the material on that page.

What can be audited at home is institutional breadth and recent trajectory. Banks live on UPI rose from 713 in April 2026 to 752 in August 2026 on the product-statistics page. Volume in August was higher than in April, May, June, or July. Value in August was not the high of that window. Scale and composition are both visible. A single "largest" label hides the second fact.

The G20 payments function is "easy and instant transfer" among people, businesses, and governments. UPI's published table shows transfers happening in very large numbers. It does not show the price a small merchant pays, or the share of government disbursements that move on UPI. Direct Benefit Transfer is a related public pipe. The Lok Sabha annexure of 29 July 2026 says over ₹52 lakh crore has been transferred through DBT nationwide. DBT is not the same series as UPI.

## Data sharing with consent

This is the function where a lazy comparison collapses India into one product. The documents describe at least three different mechanisms.

DigiLocker is a wallet for authentic documents issued by original issuers. As on 31 July 2026, the Rajya Sabha was told it had more than 72.43 crore registered users and 5,437 document types or services. API Setu, in the 29 July PIB account, had 8,904 government APIs published so that departments can share through a common catalogue. The Account Aggregator framework, under the Reserve Bank's directions of 28 November 2025, is a consent artefact for specified financial information. The aggregator must not keep that information, must not execute the customer's transactions, and must act on explicit consent. Sahamati's public dashboard for July 2026 shows 326.30 million cumulative accounts linked and 538.32 million cumulative consents fulfilled, with the consent column defined as requests against which data was delivered at once.

The G20 line is "data sharing with consent wherever applicable," plus safeguards under the applicable framework. India's applicable framework is in transition. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is in force only in the slices the government appointed. A gazette notification of 13 November 2025 brought the institutional provisions and the Data Protection Board into effect, and set later dates for much of the operative law. A country can run consent-based rails before the full statute is switched on. That is a sequencing fact, not a claim that the legal benchmark is already complete.

## What a serious comparison would still need

The ministers asked systems to be safe, secure, trusted, accountable, and inclusive, and to respect human rights, personal data, privacy, and intellectual property. India's publications show pieces of that governance: Account Aggregator prohibitions, health-system security audits before joining the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, and hosting and audit practices MeitY described for e-governance platforms. They do not show a single cross-country audit.

They also show a physical dependency the G20 definition assumes and India measures separately. As of June 2026, the 29 July PIB account says 2.21 lakh gram panchayats had been made service-ready under BharatNet, while BharatNet points of presence were operational at 80,472 gram panchayats during that month. A payments rail can post a national volume record and still be thin where the point of presence is down.

Use the G20 text as a checklist of functions and safeguards. On that checklist, India has production systems for identity, instant payments, and more than one consent or document rail, with official counts attached. On that same checklist, coverage is uneven, one celebrated global share is a government sentence without its table, and the data-protection statute is still arriving in phases. That is a comparison with a definition. It is not a medal table.

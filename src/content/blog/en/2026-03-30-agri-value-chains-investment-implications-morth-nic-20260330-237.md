# What bank credit is doing in the agri chain

The credit fact in this chain, as on 31 July 2026, is a faster book. The Reserve Bank’s sectoral deployment release says credit to agriculture and allied activities grew 17.0 percent year on year, against 7.3 percent in the corresponding fortnight a year earlier. The same release says non-food credit of scheduled commercial banks grew 19.1 percent, against 9.9 percent. The sector rates come from 41 banks that account for about 95 percent of non-food credit. They are not a count of every rural cooperative.

That 17 percent is a growth rate on outstanding credit. It is not the government’s ground-level credit target, and it is not NABARD refinance. Mixing the three produces a number nobody published.

## The target, the priority-sector rule, and the refinance rate

For FY 2025-26 the ground-level credit target for agriculture and allied activities was ₹32.50 lakh crore, with a sub-target of ₹5.0 lakh crore for animal husbandry, dairying, and fisheries. The Ministry’s note on rural credit says disbursement from FY 2014-15 to FY 2023-24 grew at over 13 percent a year, and that the FY 2025-26 target is more than four times the ₹8 lakh crore target of FY 2014-15. A target is an instruction to the banking system. It is not, by itself, the achieved disbursement. This note does not treat the target as the outcome.

Priority sector rules, as that same note restates them, require banks to earmark 18 percent of adjusted net bank credit or the credit equivalent of off-balance-sheet exposures, whichever is higher, for agriculture. Inside that, 14 percent is for non-corporate farmers and 10 percent is for small and marginal farmers. Shortfalls can be parked with NABARD. NABARD’s own description of short-term refinance says concessional money for seasonal agricultural operations reaches state cooperative banks through the Short Term Cooperative Rural Credit Fund and regional rural banks through the Short Term Regional Rural Bank fund, both fed by that priority-sector shortfall. The Long Term Rural Credit Fund does the same job for investment credit.

In 2023-24 NABARD disbursed ₹1,83,152.53 crore of short-term refinance. Of that, ₹65,675.67 crore was concessional refinance under those two short-term funds, all of it for seasonal agricultural operations (₹50,517.76 crore to cooperative banks and ₹15,157.91 crore to regional rural banks). Additional seasonal refinance was ₹79,463.32 crore and other short-term refinance was ₹37,933.75 crore. Those are 2023-24 disbursements. They are the latest purpose split on NABARD’s refinance page, not a July 2026 print.

The price of that crop-loan refinance is still published. With effect from 17 April 2025, NABARD’s interest-rate table shows 4.5 percent for state cooperative banks, regional rural banks, and direct district-central financing of crop loans. The 2024-25 operational circulars had already set 4.5 percent where the cooperative structure or the regional rural bank lent up to ₹3 lakh per farmer at 7 percent or less. The rate table says the refinance price. It does not say how much was drawn in 2025-26.

## What an investor should underwrite

A lender or a funder looking at a mill, an FPO, or a warehouse is looking at three official layers that move on different calendars. Bank outstanding credit to agriculture sped up between July 2025 and July 2026. The policy target for the year that ended in March 2026 was ₹32.50 lakh crore of ground-level disbursement, allied activities included. The refinance that actually reaches cooperative banks and regional rural banks was last split in rupees for 2023-24, at a crop-loan refinance rate that was still 4.5 percent in April 2025.

None of those series is the cash a particular FPO will receive.

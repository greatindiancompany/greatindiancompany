An investment that touches an agri value chain in India is underwriting a handoff. Someone aggregates small lots, someone holds them, someone transforms or moves them, and someone pays. The implication is in that handoff, not in a sector adjective.

## What the chain does to a model

Four features show up in the accounts whether or not the pitch mentions them.

- **Seasonal intake.** Cash leaves at harvest and returns when the buyer pays. A model that assumes even monthly volume will miss the working-capital peak.
- **Quality that is settled early.** Moisture, damage, and residue are hard to repair after the lot is aggregated. The investment either owns that check or pays the buyer who does.
- **Loss as a cost line.** Lots shrink, spoil, or get downgraded. If loss is not in the model, the margin is a remainder, not a measurement.
- **Rules at the point of sale.** State market practice, food requirements, and buyer specifications can stop a lot that the plant has already paid for.

Capacity that sits idle outside the season is still a cost. A plant sized to a national crop number, rather than to contracted catchment, imports that mistake into the asset.

## Where the decision actually sits

Ask which contract sets volume, which contract sets price, and which party carries unsold stock. A business that buys spot and sells spot is a trading book with rent. A business with a named buyer and a named catchment is an operating company. Those are different implications for how long capital is exposed.

Capex follows the same split. A cold room or a processing line earns its keep only on throughput you can point to. ## What this page will not do

There is no valuation, no expected return, and no ranking of sub-sectors. An implication here is a line you should be able to find in a contract or a stock ledger. If you cannot find it, the chain has not been underwritten.

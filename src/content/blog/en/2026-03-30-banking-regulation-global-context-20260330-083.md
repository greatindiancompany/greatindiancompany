# What the Reserve Bank reported from India's 2024 FSAP

The global comparison that matters for Indian banking is the Financial Sector Assessment Program, the joint review by the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. India does not leave that review only on the Fund's website. The Reserve Bank published its own note on 24 March 2025: [India: Financial Sector Assessment Program, 2024](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=60047).

This brief stays inside that note. It does not add findings that appear only in the Fund's longer report.

## What the exercise is

The note describes the program as a mandatory review, since September 2010, for jurisdictions with systemically important financial sectors. It says the review is mandatory for 32 jurisdictions, including India, every five years, and for another 15 jurisdictions every ten years. The previous India assessment was in 2017. The Fund published that Financial System Stability Assessment on 21 December 2017.

The Fund published the latest Financial System Stability Assessment on its website on 28 February 2025, from work done in 2024. On the date of the Reserve Bank's note, the World Bank's Financial Sector Assessment was still due for publication. The note welcomes the joint assessment and says it conforms to the highest international standards.

## What the Reserve Bank chose to highlight

The note's reading of the Fund's assessment has a clear banking core.

The financial system is described as more resilient and more diverse than at the 2017 assessment, with rapid economic growth as the driver. The note says the financial sector recovered from distress episodes of the 2010s and withstood the pandemic. Non-bank intermediaries became more diverse and more interconnected. Banks and non-banking financial companies, in the note's words, have sufficient aggregate capital to support moderate lending even in severe macrofinancial scenarios. The note does not publish a system-wide capital ratio alongside that sentence.

On non-banks, the note says the Fund acknowledged India's scale-based prudential framework and the introduction of a bank-like liquidity coverage ratio for large non-banking financial companies.

On banks, the note says the Fund suggested strengthening credit-risk management through IFSR 9 adoption, and upgrading supervision of individual loans, collateral valuation, connected borrower groups, large-exposure limits, and related-party transactions. The printed text of the Reserve Bank note uses "IFSR 9." Readers of international standards will recognise the expected-credit-loss standard under its usual name, IFRS 9. This brief does not treat the suggestion as a statement that India has already switched its bank loan-loss rules.

The note also records a securities-market section, including the Corporate Debt Market Development Fund, swing pricing and liquidity requirements for bond mutual funds, and a wider perimeter around sustainability and investor protection in equity derivatives. Public digital infrastructure is credited with a large gain in retail inclusion. The Fund's recommendation, as summarised, is that credit access for financially underserved sectors can be improved by stronger legal, tax, and information infrastructure for asset-based and digital lending.

Insurance is described as strong and growing, with a suggestion of further steps on risk-based solvency and supervision, and stronger group supervision. The note says financial stability should be the primary objective of the macroprudential authorities.

## The risks the note flags

Cybersecurity, climate change, and system-wide contagion are the emerging risks named in the note. Climate-related stability risks "appear manageable but warrant careful monitoring," and the assessment asked for more granular data. On cyber, the note says the Fund found advanced oversight, especially for banks, and said crisis simulations and stress tests could be extended to cross-sector and market-wide events.

The closing paragraph is the policy stance. Recommendations are framed as further improvement, many of them already in line with regulators' own plans. India remains committed to internationally accepted standards, adopted in a phased way and attuned to domestic needs and economic conditions.

For a reader comparing India with the Basel committees or with the 2017 assessment, the Reserve Bank's own summary is the document to quote: resilience since 2017, aggregate capital described as sufficient for moderate lending in severe scenarios, and a specific supervisory to-do list on bank credit risk. The numbers behind any single bank remain in that bank's Pillar 3 disclosure and in the capital directions, not in this press note.

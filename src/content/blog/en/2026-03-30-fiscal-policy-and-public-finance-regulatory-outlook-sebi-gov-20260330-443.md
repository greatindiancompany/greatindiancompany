The fiscal plan for 2026-27 is not only a deficit ratio. It assumes a tax administration that can collect the gross tax revenue written into the Budget. The rules that matter for that assumption are in the fiscal statement and in the government’s own description of GST, not in a market regulator’s circular.

## Direct tax: a new Act, with continuity as the stated design

The fiscal statement says the Income-tax Act, 2025, comes into effect on 1 April 2026. Parliament enacted it as a simplified text of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The statement lists three principles that guided the rewrite: simpler structure and wording, no major tax-policy changes, and no modification of tax rates. The point of the principles, as written, is continuity for taxpayers.

Other direct-tax steps are listed as already done, not as fresh 1 April surprises. Finance Act, 2025, is credited with slab and rate relief under the new regime. Holding periods for capital assets are described as simplified to 12 or 24 months. The equalisation levy of 2 percent on e-commerce supplies ended on 1 August 2024, and the 6 percent levy on specified services ended on 1 April 2025. Startup tax-benefit eligibility is described as extended to firms incorporated before 1 April 2030. Prosecution thresholds for certain TDS and TCS defaults have been relaxed. None of these sentences replaces the Finance Act text. They are the Budget division’s summary of the base on which 2026-27 receipts are estimated.

Gross tax revenue is estimated at ₹44.04 lakh crore, 8.0 percent above the 2025-26 revised estimates. Direct taxes are ₹26.97 lakh crore, 61.2 percent of that total. If the new Act’s “no rate change” principle holds, this growth is an assumption about incomes and compliance, not about a higher schedule of rates.

## Indirect tax: two main slabs, and a Centre GST estimate

The same statement says that, on the GST Council’s recommendation, the Union moved a four-rate structure to a two-rate structure: a standard rate of 18 percent, a merit rate of 5 percent, and a special demerit rate of 40 percent for a select set of goods and services. A [Press Information Bureau note of 30 June 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2279318&lang=1&reg=3) dates the rate and exemption changes from the Council’s 56th meeting to 22 September 2025, and describes the 40 percent rate as applying to a list that includes certain tobacco, aerated drinks, high-end cars, online gaming, and similar items. The note’s list is illustrative of the category. The notification is the legal instrument.

CGST receipts are estimated at ₹10.19 lakh crore in 2026-27, 6.3 percent above the revised estimates. That is the Centre’s GST, not gross collections of CGST, SGST, and IGST together. Gross collections are published separately by the GST system and are shared with the states.

Registration procedure is part of the receipt story. The fiscal statement says low-risk applicants, and applicants whose output-tax liability on supplies to registered persons will not exceed ₹2.5 lakh a month, are to be registered automatically within three working days. The Bureau note says the goods-supplier threshold has been ₹40 lakh since April 2019, that some special-category states remain on a lower threshold, and that the composition scheme generally runs to ₹1.5 crore of turnover. Quarterly returns with monthly payment cover taxpayers up to ₹5 crore. These thresholds decide who is in the monthly data.

## What is still a Budget assumption

Customs changes in the 2026-27 speech are a schedule of rate edits, including nil rates on specified inputs for solar glass, lithium-ion cell machinery, and nuclear-power goods, effective 2 February 2026 unless the entry says otherwise. The speech annex is the list. A summary that picks three items and calls them the whole customs policy is not.

The statement also says 31 customs-duty notifications were merged into one. That is a compliance change: one reference instead of thirty-one. It does not, by itself, change the deficit.

Provisional refunds on zero-rated supplies, refunds for low-value exports, and the removal of a ₹1,000 floor on certain export refunds are in the Finance Bill proposals described with the speech. Until the enacted text and the notification dates are checked, treat them as proposals attached to this Budget, not as features already lived with for a full year.

The regulatory outlook that matches the fiscal arithmetic is narrow. Direct-tax rates are presented as unchanged by the new Act. GST rates are presented as already simplified, with CGST estimated to grow 6.3 percent. Registration is presented as faster for a defined low-risk set. Collection risk sits in whether incomes, consumption, and compliance deliver ₹44.04 lakh crore of gross tax, not in a surprise rate card hidden in the deficit paragraph.

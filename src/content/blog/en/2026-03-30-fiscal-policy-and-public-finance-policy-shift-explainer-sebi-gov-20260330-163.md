India still has a fiscal deficit target. It is no longer the only sentence the Budget asks Parliament to remember. In the 2026-27 papers, the anchor the government describes is the debt-to-GDP ratio, and the deficit is the instrument used to pull that ratio down.

## What the Act still says

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, as the Finance Minister’s statement recalls it, requires the Centre to limit the fiscal deficit to 3 percent of GDP by 31 March 2021. It asks the Centre to endeavour to keep general government debt at or below 60 percent of GDP and Central government debt at or below 40 percent by 31 March 2025. The Act also asks that those targets not be exceeded after the dates have passed. The rules have been in force since 5 July 2004.

Those dates have passed. The deficit is not at 3 percent, and Central debt is not at 40 percent. The same statement is required, under section 7(3)(b), to explain the deviation. The February 2026 explanation points to an uneven global expansion, shifting trade policy, and geopolitical risk, and says the government will keep consolidating while trying to protect growth. That is an explanation of a miss. It is not a repeal of the Act.

## What the 2026-27 Budget uses instead as the working rule

The Budget speech for 2026-27 says that Budget 2025-26 had set a Central debt-to-GDP target of 50±1 percent by 2030-31. The speech then gives the near numbers: 55.6 percent of GDP in the 2026-27 Budget estimates, against 56.1 percent in the 2025-26 revised estimates. A lower ratio, the speech says, frees resources over time by slowing the growth of interest payments.

The operating number for the year is the fiscal deficit. The speech records that the commitment, made in the 2021-22 Budget, to bring the deficit below 4.5 percent of GDP by 2025-26 has been met: the revised estimate for 2025-26 is 4.4 percent, the same as that year’s Budget estimate. For 2026-27 the estimate is 4.3 percent.

The fiscal statement puts the longer arc in one line: the deficit has come down from 6.7 percent of GDP in 2021-22 to 4.4 percent in the 2025-26 revised estimates. It also says rolling targets for the next two years are not being given, because of uncertainty in the global macro and geopolitical setting. From 2026-27 through 2030-31, the stated endeavour is to set each year’s deficit so that Central debt stays on a declining path.

## How to read 4.3 percent next to the old 3 percent

Four numbers travel together in the rolling-target table, all as a percent of GDP:

| Indicator | 2025-26 revised | 2026-27 Budget |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Fiscal deficit | 4.4 | 4.3 |
| Revenue deficit | 1.5 | 1.5 |
| Primary deficit | 0.8 | 0.7 |
| Central government debt | 56.1 | 55.6 |

The revenue deficit is not improving in this step. Consolidation in the table is a tenth of a percentage point on the fiscal deficit and half a point on debt, with the primary deficit a tenth lower. Gross tax revenue is estimated at 11.2 percent of GDP, down from 11.4 percent in the revised estimates, and non-tax revenue at 1.7 percent, down from 1.9 percent. The path is not “higher taxes, same spending.” It is a small decline in the deficit while the tax ratio is also a little lower.

Debt in this table follows the FRBM definition. It includes external public debt at current exchange rates, public-account liabilities, and special securities issued to the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) for the states. Those NSSF securities are 0.7 percent of GDP in the revised estimates and 0.5 percent in the Budget estimates. Net of them, the statement puts Central debt at 55.5 percent and 55.1 percent of GDP. Quoting 55.6 without that footnote mixes the Centre’s own debt with a slice that the states repay.

## What did not shift

The Sixteenth Finance Commission’s vertical share stays at 41 percent of the divisible pool, and the government says it has accepted that recommendation. Deficit reduction is not being presented as a cut in the states’ share of taxes.

Guarantees remain capped. Section 4(1)(c) limits incremental guarantees on the security of the Consolidated Fund to 0.5 percent of GDP in a year. In 2024-25 the net addition was ₹43,628.39 crore, or 0.13 percent of GDP. Outstanding Union guarantees were about ₹3.33 lakh crore at the end of that year, 1.0 percent of GDP, against 3.3 percent in 2004-05.

The useful question for the next Budget is not whether the speech repeats “50±1 by 2030-31.” It is whether the debt ratio in the next revised estimates is below 55.6 percent, and whether the revenue deficit is still stuck at 1.5 percent while interest remains the largest rigid claim on receipts.

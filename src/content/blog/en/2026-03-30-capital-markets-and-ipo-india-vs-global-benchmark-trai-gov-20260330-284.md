# Where India's IPO mix differs from other listing markets

Cross-country league tables go stale quickly, and most of them are not Indian primary law. The comparison worth using is the one SEBI printed in its own Annual Report 2025-26, with the source named next to the claim. This note does not add a second dataset.

## The offer-for-sale share is the sharp difference

Chapter 3, Box 3.2, reviews IPOs listed on Indian exchanges from April 2011 to December 2025: 543 main-board issues and 1,391 SME issues. Together they mobilised ₹7.9 lakh crore. Fresh proceeds were ₹2.9 lakh crore, 37 per cent. Money paid to selling shareholders was ₹5 lakh crore, 63 per cent.

On the main board, the offer-for-sale share rose from under half in 2011-2015 to above 60 per cent from 2016, peaked at 87 per cent in 2020, and has eased in recent years as fresh capital took a larger relative share. Issues above ₹1,000 crore stayed offer-for-sale heavy, generally 60 to 90 per cent from 2016 onward. The share of main-board IPOs that were more than 70 per cent offer for sale fell from over half in 2017-2021 to about a quarter in recent years. SME issues stayed fresh-issue markets. Their offer-for-sale share generally sat between 0 and 27 per cent.

SEBI then places India against other listing markets, using Bloomberg as the data source. The report says that in almost every year since 2013, India's offer-for-sale share was higher than the others in that comparison and often above 50 per cent of IPO size. China and Hong Kong showed a negligible offer-for-sale share, in a 0 to 4 per cent range. The United States was moderate and cyclical, 6 to 29 per cent. South Korea was 7 to 49 per cent. Those ranges are SEBI's summary of Bloomberg, not a new calculation on this page. They are enough to correct a common reading. A large Indian IPO year can be a large exit year. A large Chinese or Hong Kong IPO year, on the same comparison, has been a capital-raising year.

The 2025-26 Indian tape is consistent with a partial turn toward fresh capital, not with a disappearance of exits. Mixed IPOs raised ₹1,15,901 crore, of which ₹58,166 crore was fresh and ₹57,735 crore was offer for sale. Pure offer-for-sale IPOs were still ₹50,849 crore across 19 issues.

## The secondary-market comparison went the other way in FY26

Size and return are different benchmarks. Chapter 1 says aggregate market capitalisation was ₹411.6 lakh crore at the end of March 2026, and that India remained the fifth-largest stock market on that measure. Chapter 4 states the same capitalisation as ₹411.6 trillion, which is the same quantity. The year's return did not match that rank. Nifty 50 fell 5.1 per cent in rupees and 13.9 per cent in US dollars. MSCI Emerging Markets rose 24.7 per cent and MSCI World rose 17.2 per cent. Chapter 2 attributes the foreign-equity outflow, USD 19.7 billion, to a year of trade-policy uncertainty and then to the Middle East conflict the chapter describes from late February 2026. Domestic institutions were the offset, with net equity buying of ₹8.5 lakh crore. A market can be a top-five venue by capitalisation and a relative underperformer in a single year. Both statements are in the report.

## Market design is where SEBI says it is converging

Chapter 4's closing-auction box is an explicit alignment with practice at the New York Stock Exchange, the London Stock Exchange, and the Hong Kong exchange. India has used a 30-minute volume-weighted average to set the cash close. The new session concentrates orders into one auction from 3:15 pm to 3:35 pm so a single equilibrium price can be found. The report ties the demand for that price to passive money: about 30 per cent of foreign-portfolio equity assets and 28 per cent of domestic mutual fund equity assets were in passive funds at 31 March 2026, and Indian weights in MSCI and FTSE indices, which the chapter puts between 12 and 30 per cent depending on the index, make the close a rebalancing event. The first phase covers stocks that have derivative contracts. The design choice is global. The coverage is still partial.

Block trades moved the other way from a simple copy of foreign practice. The price band widened from 1 per cent to 3 per cent, and the minimum order rose from ₹10 crore to ₹25 crore, so smaller "block" interest is pushed back into the normal book. That is an Indian liquidity choice, described as such in the chapter.

## How to hold the comparison

Use the offer-for-sale ranges when the question is what an Indian IPO finances. Use the index comparison when the question is how the listed market travelled in 2025-26. Use the closing-auction note when the question is whether India's close is still a local method. Do not turn any of the three into a claim that India has "caught up" or "fallen behind" on a metric the annual report does not define.

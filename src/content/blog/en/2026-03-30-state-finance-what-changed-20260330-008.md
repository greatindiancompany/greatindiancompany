# What the 2025-26 state budget study changed

On 23 January 2026 the Reserve Bank of India released *State Finances: A Study of Budgets of 2025-26* (press release 2025-2026/1982). It is the consolidated public baseline for state budgets this year. The theme chapter is demographic transition. The tables behind it use 2023-24 accounts, 2024-25 revised or provisional figures, and 2025-26 budget estimates. Those three labels are the first thing a reader has to keep apart.

## The deficit step, and what the Bank says it is

States' consolidated gross fiscal deficit increased to 3.3 per cent of GDP in 2024-25, after remaining below 3.0 per cent during the previous three years. The 2025-26 budgets place it at 3.3 per cent of GDP again.

The press release gives the reason for the step through 3 per cent. It mainly reflects 50-year interest-free loans from the Centre under the Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment. Those loans are over and above the normal net borrowing ceiling of the states. A line that says only "states crossed 3 per cent" leaves out the loan the study is describing.

Chart II.1 in the study is the consolidated deficit. Chart II.18 places that deficit next to the 50-year interest-free loans. Table II.1 is the set of major deficit indicators.

## Capital spending is budgeted higher

Capital expenditure stayed at 2.7 per cent of GDP in 2023-24 and again in 2024-25. The 2025-26 budgets put it at 3.2 per cent of GDP. The Bank calls this a sustained capital thrust. The 3.2 figure is a budget estimate. Accounts for 2025-26 will show how much of that plan was spent.

The Department of Expenditure has since set the 2026-27 round of the same loan scheme. Its guidelines of 27 March 2026 (F. No. 44(1)/PF-S/2026-27 (CAPEX)) allocate Rs 2,00,000 crore of 50-year interest-free loans for capital investment, again over and above the normal net borrowing ceiling. That allocation is a Union budget decision for the year after the study's 2025-26 estimates. It is part of what changed around the study.

## Debt is still described as elevated

The consolidated outstanding liabilities of states remained elevated in the post-pandemic period. The budget estimate is 29.2 per cent of GDP at end-March 2026. The study's debt tables (Statements 18 to 20) split that stock by instrument and by state, including a GSDP ratio that is a different denominator from the GDP ratio in the press release.

## The study also changed the question

The theme chapter says Indian states are at different stages of demographic transition, and that this now shapes their finances. Youthful states, in the Bank's wording, have a wider window because the working-age population is expanding and revenue mobilisation is stronger. Ageing states face a shrinking tax base and rising committed expenditure. Intermediate states have to fund growth and prepare for ageing together. The consolidated 3.3 per cent is an average across those positions. Chart II.2 shows state-wise gross fiscal deficit to GSDP in the 2025-26 budget estimates, which is where the average comes apart.

## The Finance Commission award that sits beside the study

The Union government's explanatory memorandum on the Sixteenth Finance Commission, published with the Budget documents, covers 2026-27 to 2030-31. The government accepted the recommendation to keep the states' share at 41 per cent of the net proceeds of Union taxes. It accepted in principle a state fiscal-deficit cap of 3 per cent of GSDP, excluding SASCI loans, enforced under Article 293(3) of the Constitution. Recommendations on off-budget borrowing, on amending state fiscal-responsibility laws, and on the Union's own deficit path were left for separate examination.

Read together, the January study and the memorandum say the ordinary borrowing room and the capital-loan window are different lines. Later accounts can still revise the 2024-25 and 2025-26 ratios.

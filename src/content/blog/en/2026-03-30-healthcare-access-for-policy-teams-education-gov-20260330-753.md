## The policy is not the approval

The National Health Policy, 2017, tells the system what it is trying to become: public spending toward 2.5 percent of GDP by 2025, a major share of resources on primary care, up to two-thirds or more, and most secondary care at the district. Ayushman Bharat is the programme built from that policy. Neither the policy nor the programme spends a rupee by being cited. Money moves when a state proposes and the Centre approves inside a resource envelope, and when a State Health Agency chooses how PM-JAY will pay hospitals.

Policy teams waste cycles when they treat a national launch line as an instruction a district hospital can already follow. The useful unit is the approval, the sharing ratio, and the report the state is asked to file.

## What the Centre can decide, and what it cannot

Public health and hospitals are a state subject. The ministry’s PM-ABHIM note says implementation lies with the state, with technical and financial support from the Centre. The mission uses National Health Mission institutions for its centrally sponsored components. The published outlay is ₹64,180 crore for 2021–22 to 2025–26. Approvals across those years are stated as ₹33,081.82 crore, with physical lines for mandirs, block public health units, district laboratories, and critical care blocks. A policy team tracking access should track sanctions, releases, and commissioned facilities as three columns. The scheme window in that published text ended with FY 2025–26. A team writing a 2026–27 note needs the successor provision, not a photocopy of the old outlay.

PM-JAY’s central contribution follows the ratios on the National Health Authority’s about-page: 60:40 in the general case, 90:10 for North-Eastern states and for Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand as that page names them, and up to 100 percent for union territories without a legislature. In insurance mode the Centre pays against a premium ceiling. In trust mode it pays against claims or the ceiling, whichever is lower. Families and rupees a state adds beyond the national design are a state cost. A note that calls every extra beneficiary “PM-JAY funded” misstates the budget.

## Choices that should be published in the open

The about-page lists state flexibilities that decide access and are often missing from public dashboards: trust, insurance, or mixed mode; the beneficiary database; co-branding with a state scheme; cover above ₹5 lakh; package additions and price revisions; the list of procedures reserved for public hospitals; whether a percentage is deducted from public-hospital claims; and whether the state uses its own IT system. Publishing those seven facts for each state would tell a hospital and a patient more than another national family count. Mode lists from older annual reports should be marked with their year. The [state-pattern note](/blog/healthcare-access-state-wise-patterns-education-gov-20260330-313) explains why.

Primary-care reporting has a similar honesty problem. The ministry reply of 25 March 2025 gave 1,76,573 mandirs operationalised till February 2025 and very large screening totals. A policy team should ask states for filled posts against the mid-level provider and IPHS staffing, for drug stock-outs, and for completed referrals. The standards portal defines compliance as a score of 80 percent or higher and records a commitment to that status in 50 percent of facilities by 2025–26. Ask for the score distribution. A binary “centre established” flag hides the bottleneck.

## Grievance is part of the policy

The Authority runs a central grievance system and publishes 14555 as the public number. A cover that cannot be appealed at the hospital desk is an entitlement on paper. Policy teams should read grievance categories beside claim volumes: denied admissions, out-of-pocket demands at empanelled hospitals, and portability disputes. Those categories are the execution record of the cashless promise.

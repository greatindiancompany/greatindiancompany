# India's Energy Transition for Policy Teams

Policy teams inherit a system in which the Centre sets targets, schemes, and a consumption obligation, and the states host the plants, the distribution companies, and the charges. The work is to know which lever is yours. Copying another state's press release is how Rajasthan's solar park gets turned into a rooftop target for a hydro state, or how a national percentage gets imposed twice.

## What the Centre has already taken

The updated NDC is a national pledge: a 45 percent cut in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 from 2005, and about 50 percent of installed electric capacity from non-fossil sources, the second of those written with a finance and technology condition. The 500 GW non-fossil planning figure is what MNRE and the Central Electricity Authority use operationally. States are not each assigned a slice of 500 GW in the documents cited here. They feel the target through schemes, transmission planning, and the consumption obligation.

The Ministry of Power's notification of 27 September 2025 is the demand rule. MNRE has said it subsumes state renewable-purchase obligations, so a state should not keep a parallel percentage alive beside it. For 2026-27 the total is 35.95 percent. Distribution licensees also have a composition: 1.97 percent wind, 1.34 percent hydro, 2.70 percent distributed renewable energy, and 29.94 percent other. Hilly and North-Eastern states, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand carry half the distributed component. A licensee that serves only an urban area carries 75 percent of it. Wind and hydro count from projects commissioned after 31 March 2024. If a state commission's old regulations still quote a pre-2025 purchase trajectory, the policy task is alignment, not a new ambition.

The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme is also central. Intensity targets for obligated entities are notified by the Environment Ministry. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency runs the books. A state energy department is not the issuer of those certificates. It may still have plants in its territory that are obligated entities, and it should know who they are.

Central schemes with money attached in Budget 2026-27 are concrete. PM Surya Ghar is ₹22,000 crore and runs through March 2027, with a national portal and a utility inspection step that states actually perform. KUSUM is ₹5,000 crore, extended to March 2027. The Green Energy Corridor line is ₹599.99 crore, toward intra-state lines and a cumulative 6,000 circuit kilometres. The hydrogen Mission's annual provision is ₹600 crore net, against a ₹19,744 crore envelope administered by MNRE. Small hydro has a ₹2,584.60 crore scheme through FY 2030-31 aimed at about 1,500 MW, especially in the hills and the North-East. Floating solar with storage has a new ₹5,070 crore scheme. These are invitations with rules. A state policy that duplicates the subsidy without using the portal will confuse vendors and slow the inspection the central assistance depends on.

## What remains a state problem

Resource is local. On 31 July 2026, MNRE's location sheet shows Gujarat at 52,610 MW of renewable capacity, Rajasthan at 50,335 MW, Maharashtra at 33,195 MW, Tamil Nadu at 29,802 MW, and Karnataka at 27,442 MW. Himachal Pradesh's 12,980 MW is mostly large hydro. Kerala's solar is mostly rooftop. A state transmission and land policy has to match that pattern. The inter-state plan for 2030 already names the western and southern renewable zones and offshore wind off Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. A state that is a load centre, not a resource state, still carries the consumption obligation and still hosts the households in PM Surya Ghar. Delhi's solar stock is almost entirely rooftop. That is a successful version of a load-centre policy, at a small absolute scale.

Distribution companies are state instruments even when the obligation is central. They have to procure, or their large consumers will procure around them through open access once the load crosses 100 kW. They have to inspect rooftops. They have to produce data for the distributed component, or live with the guideline's fallback of 4 kWh per kW per day where data are missing. The filing extension to 31 May 2026 for FY 2024-25 compliance is a warning about data, not about intent. State regulators still determine retail tariffs and the open-access charges that decide whether a commercial consumer leaves the distribution tariff. A national waiver of inter-state transmission charges, as amended by CERC on 26 June 2025, does not set a state's additional surcharge.

Land, right of way, and intra-state substations will not be solved in a central notification. The Central Electricity Authority's own reason for publishing a 2030 transmission plan early is that plants are faster than lines. State energy departments that clear generation and leave the bay for later are choosing the bottleneck the national planner already described.

## A short agenda for a state team

Publish the state's four cells from the MNRE location sheet every month: ground-mounted solar, rooftop, wind, large hydro. Publish PM Surya Ghar applications and installations side by side, the way the 16 July 2026 Lok Sabha annexure does, so a high application count cannot be mistaken for commissioned systems. Map the consumption obligation onto each distribution licensee, including the hill or urban adjustment if it applies. Identify intra-state lines that are blocking projects already in the inter-state plan. Leave the 2030 national gigawatt figure in the preamble. Put the licensee, the substation, and the inspection queue in the operative paragraph.
